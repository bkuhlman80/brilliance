Soto & John (2017)**

**Citation**

Soto, C. J., & John, O. P. (2017). The next Big Five Inventory (BFI-2): Developing and assessing a hierarchical model with 15 facets to enhance bandwidth, fidelity, and predictive power. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 113*(1), 117–143. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000096

**Design**- **Type:** Cross-sectional, multi-sample psychometric development and validation (3 studies)
  - **N:** Study 1: N = 1,137 (Eugene-Springfield Community Sample, ESCS); Study 2: N = 1,000 (Internet sample, scale development); Study 3: N = 1,000 (Internet validation) + N = 470 (student validation). A subsample of N = 438 students completed the BFI-2, original BFI, BFAS, Mini-Markers, NEO-FFI, and NEO PI-R. Peer reports: N = 184. Retest: N = 110.
  - **Age range:** Study 1: 18–89 (M = 49.64); Study 2: 18–77 (M = 29.25); Study 3 Internet: 18–74 (M = 28.73); Study 3 Student: 16–49 (M = 21.68)
  - **Instrument(s):** BFI-2 (60 items, 15 facets, 5 domains), original BFI (44 items), BFAS (100 items, 10 aspects), NEO PI-R (240 items, 30 facets), NEO-FFI (60 items), Big Five Mini-Markers (40 items), 885 trait-descriptive adjectives, 2,552 IPIP items (Study 1 only)
  - **Assessment interval:** Cross-sectional. Retest at ~8 weeks (N = 110). NEO PI-R administered ~1 week after BFI/BFI-2.
  - **Key limitations:**

    - No heritability data. No twin design.
    - No longitudinal developmental trajectory data. Cross-sectional age effects noted only in passing (gender differences in Table 5).
    - Samples are predominantly young (65% under 30 in Internet samples), predominantly White/Caucasian (65–66%), and exclusively from English-speaking nations. Student sample is 49% Asian American.
    - BFI-2 has only 3 facets per domain (15 total) vs. NEO PI-R's 6 per domain (30 total). This means each BFI-2 facet is a broader composite — e.g., BFI-2 Energy Level blends NEO PI-R Positive Emotions and Activity.
    - The BFI-2 deliberately excluded interstitial content (e.g., anger/irritability from Emotional Volatility, excitement-seeking from Extraversion) to maintain clean hierarchical structure. This means some NEO facets have no BFI-2 counterpart.

**Granularity**

**Facet-level** — but for the BFI-2's own 15-facet system, not the NEO PI-R's 30-facet system. The crosswalk-relevant data comes from the convergent correlations between BFI-2 facets and both BFAS aspects and NEO PI-R facets (Figure 3, N = 438).

The paper also reports item-level factor loadings (Table 6, 60 items × 5 components) and facet-level factor loadings (Table 7, 15 facets × 5 components).

  - BFI-2 vs. NEO PI-R: mean convergent r = .72

The BFI-2 converged more strongly with the BFAS, Mini-Markers, NEO-FFI, and NEO PI-R than the original BFI did, particularly for Agreeableness (BFI-2 mean = .75 vs. BFI mean = .70) and Open-Mindedness (.76 vs. .72). [from PDF p. 12]

**CFA fit: 3-facets-plus-acquiescence model (Table 8) [from PDF p. 17]**

For all five domains, the best-fitting CFA model was "three facets plus acquiescence" (CFIs ≥ .930 Internet, ≥ .932 Student). The single-domain model fit poorly across the board (CFIs ≤ .805), confirming that each domain contains meaningful within-domain differentiation. [from PDF p. 11]

**Predictive power: BFI-2 facets vs. domains (Table 11, Figure 4) [from PDF p. 22]**

Mean R² across 20 criteria:

  - Original BFI domains: .25
  - Post hoc BFI facets: .27
  - BFI-2 domains: .27
  - **BFI-2 facets: .33**

The BFI-2 facets provided substantially more predictive power than any other predictor set, and the boost from domains to facets was much larger for the BFI-2 (.33 vs. .27) than for the original BFI (.27 vs. .25). The authors argue this reflects the quality of facet-level distinctions, not merely the number of predictors. [from PDF pp. 15–16]

**Gender differences at facet level (Table 5) [from PDF p. 12]**

Several facet-level gender differences diverge from their domain-level pattern:

  - Within Extraversion: Women report higher Sociability (d = .12–.29) and Energy Level (d = .22–.24) but **not** higher Assertiveness (d = .01–.02). The domain-level gender difference in E (d = .15–.21) reflects Sociability and Energy Level, not Assertiveness.
  - Within Open-Mindedness: Women report higher Aesthetic Sensitivity (d = .02–.19), but men report higher Intellectual Curiosity (d = −.12 to −.21) and Creative Imagination (d = −.09 to −.28). The domain-level difference is near zero, masking opposing facet effects.

These within-domain divergences are relevant to the crosswalk as indirect evidence that facets within a domain do not behave as a unit.

**Dissociation Flags**

**1. BFI-2 Trust — orphaned from BFAS Agreeableness aspects**- **Currently assigned to:** No BFAS aspect (Table 1 shows "—")
  - **What the evidence says:** Trust loads .75/.79 on A but has a −.32/−.30 secondary on N. It correlates .65 with NEO Trust. In DeYoung (2007), A3 Trust loaded on Compassion (.58) with a Politeness secondary (.34). The BFI-2 treats Trust as a third A dimension rather than assigning it to either aspect.
  - **Strength:** Suggestive. The BFI-2 data alone don't prove misassignment — they show Trust doesn't fit cleanly into either BFAS aspect, which is consistent with but doesn't go beyond DeYoung (2007)'s own finding that Trust cross-loads.

**2. BFI-2 Responsibility / NEO C3 Dutifulness — orphaned from BFAS Conscientiousness aspects**- **Currently assigned to:** Industriousness in DeYoung (2007); no BFAS aspect in BFI-2 (Table 1 shows "—")
  - **What the evidence says:** Responsibility loads .78/.78 on C with a .26/.22 secondary on A. It correlates .58 with NEO Dutifulness and .47 with NEO Deliberation. In DeYoung (2007), C3 Dutifulness straddled Industriousness (.63) and Orderliness (.50).
  - **Strength:** Moderate. Multiple converging sources (BFI-2 structural data, DeYoung 2007 cross-loading, Luciano et al. 2006 genetic data) all suggest C3/Responsibility doesn't sit cleanly in either C aspect.

**3. BFI-2 Creative Imagination — orphaned from BFAS Open-Mindedness aspects**- **Currently assigned to:** No BFAS aspect (Table 1 shows "—")
  - **What the evidence says:** Creative Imagination loads .76/.78 on O with a .26/.18 E secondary. It correlates only .38 with NEO Fantasy (its closest NEO counterpart). In DeYoung (2007), O1 Fantasy loaded on Openness (.79), but the BFI-2's Creative Imagination emphasizes creativity/originality rather than idle daydreaming.
  - **Strength:** Suggestive. The weak convergence with NEO Fantasy (.38) is notable, but this likely reflects a construct definition difference rather than misassignment — the BFI-2 intentionally redefined this facet.

**4. BFI-2 Depression — massive Extraversion cross-loading**- **Currently assigned to:** Withdrawal in DeYoung (2007); Withdrawal in BFI-2 mapping
  - **What the evidence says:** Depression loads .73/.67 on N but −.43/−.41 on E. This is the largest cross-loading of any BFI-2 facet. It correlates .77 with BFAS Withdrawal.
  - **Strength:** Strong evidence of dual nature. Depression is functionally a blended N-E construct. This is not a misassignment per se — everyone agrees Depression belongs in N — but it's a flag that Depression's developmental and heritability profiles should be expected to diverge from pure-N facets like Anxiety. Converges with Terracciano (2005) finding that N3 Depression shows distinctive developmental trajectories.

**5. BFI-2 Energy Level — blends Enthusiasm and Assertiveness NEO facets**- **Currently assigned to:** Enthusiasm in BFI-2 mapping (Table 1)
  - **What the evidence says:** Energy Level correlates .70 with BFAS Enthusiasm, .63 with NEO Positive Emotions, and .59 with NEO Activity. But in DeYoung (2007), E6 Positive Emotions loads on Enthusiasm while E4 Activity loads on Assertiveness. The BFI-2 bundles both into a single facet and assigns it to Enthusiasm.
  - **Strength:** Moderate. This is a design decision, not an empirical finding. But it's relevant because our other extractions (Terracciano 2005, Soto 2015) flag E4 Activity as developmentally divergent from E6 Positive Emotions. The BFI-2 structure would mask this divergence.

**Design**- **Type:** Twin study (cross-sectional), multivariate genetic analysis
  - **N:** 779 MZ pairs (253 Canadian, 526 German) + 476 DZ pairs (207 Canadian, 269 German) = 1,255 total pairs
  - **Age range:** 14–86 years (means ~30–36 across subgroups)
  - **Instrument(s):** NEO-PI-R (English self-report, Canadian sample; German translation by Ostendorf & Angleitner, 1994)
  - **Assessment interval:** N/A (cross-sectional)
  - **Key limitations:**

    - Self-report only (no peer/informant data)
    - Volunteer twin samples recruited via media appeals — possible self-selection bias
    - German sample heavily skewed toward female MZ twins (423 of 526 MZ pairs are female)
    - Zygosity determined by questionnaire, not DNA
    - Shared environment (C) was dropped from all models because rMZ ≈ 2×rDZ throughout; this is standard but means any small C effects are absorbed into A estimates
    - Age and gender regressed out before analysis — appropriate but means we cannot examine age × genetic structure interactions
    - Analyses done domain-by-domain (6 facets at a time), NOT across all 30 facets simultaneously. This means cross-domain genetic overlap is invisible.

**Granularity**

**Facet-level.** All 30 NEO-PI-R facets reported. Multivariate models fit within each domain (6 facets per model). Twin correlations reported per facet (Table 2). Genetic and environmental factor loadings reported per facet (Tables 4–8). No item-level data.

**Core Findings**

**Overview: What this paper does that matters for the crosswalk**The paper fits multivariate genetic models to each NEO-PI-R domain's six facets. The key finding: for every domain, the best-fitting model requires **two** common genetic factors and **two** common nonshared environmental factors (plus facet-specific genetic and environmental effects). A single common-factor model is rejected for every domain.

This is directly relevant to the crosswalk because DeYoung et al. (2007) later proposed that each Big Five domain contains two aspects. If the two genetic factors align with the two aspects, that's strong convergent evidence that the aspect structure is biologically grounded. If they don't align, that's a dissociation flag.

**Multivariate genetic structure: Domain by domain**

For each domain, the best-fitting model was Model 3: **two common additive genetic factors + two common nonshared environmental factors + facet-specific genetic and environmental effects.** The single-factor common pathways model was rejected for every domain.

The "variance accounted" columns in Tables 4–8 show the proportion of a facet's total genetic variance (or total environmental variance) attributable to each factor. So A1 + A2 + A' ≈ 1.0 for the genetic partition, and E1 + E2 + E' ≈ 1.0 for the environmental partition.

**Cross-domain and structural findings**

[from text pp. 17–18]

  - **The single-factor common pathways model was rejected for ALL five domains.** This means no domain is aetiologically unitary — the covariance among facets cannot be explained by a single latent construct influenced by one set of genes and one set of environments. Each domain requires multiple genetic and environmental factors.
  - **Equivalence of model FORM was supported across samples.** Both Canadian and German data required the same model structure (2 genetic + 2 environmental common factors). The difference was in the MAGNITUDE of factor loadings.
  - **Equivalence of magnitude was REJECTED for all five domains.** The specific loadings differed significantly across Canadian and German samples (all χ² difference tests significant, P < .05).
  - The authors note that higher-order traits "do not exist as veridical psychological entities per se, but rather... as useful heuristic devices that describe pleiotropic effects and the common influence of environmental factors on sets of individual facets." [from PDF page 17] This is a strong statement against domain-level realism.

**Dissociation Flags**

N5 Impulsiveness → Neuroticism domain

  - **Currently assigned to:** Volatility aspect (DeYoung, 2007)
  - **Evidence:** 66–85% of genetic variance is facet-specific, not shared with other N facets. Does not load on either common genetic factor. This is the highest genetic independence of any facet in the Neuroticism domain.
  - **Strength:** Strong

E5 Excitement Seeking → Extraversion domain

  - **Currently assigned to:** Enthusiasm aspect (DeYoung, 2007)
  - **Evidence:** 68–70% of genetic variance is facet-specific. Does not load on genetic factor 2 at all. Its shared genetic variance is only with the Gregariousness-led factor 1 (and weakly at that).
  - **Strength:** Strong

O6 Values → Openness domain

  - **Currently assigned to:** Intellect aspect (DeYoung, 2007) [though this is debated]
  - **Evidence:** 51–59% facet-specific genetic variance. Loads only weakly on both common genetic factors. Also shows elevated DZ correlations (.30–.31) suggesting possible shared environment effects not modeled.
  - **Strength:** Moderate

O5 Ideas → Openness domain

  - **Currently assigned to:** Intellect aspect (DeYoung, 2007)
  - **Evidence:** 44–55% facet-specific genetic variance. In Germany, loads more on the Openness-like factor (.29) than the Intellect-like factor (.36) — but the difference is small and much of its variance is independent. In Canada, cross-loads on the Openness factor (.52).
  - **Strength:** Suggestive — not a misassignment flag per se, but a flag that Ideas is more genetically independent of its domain than expected.

A6 Tendermindedness → Agreeableness domain

  - **Currently assigned to:** Compassion aspect (DeYoung, 2007)
  - **Evidence:** 56–58% facet-specific genetic variance. Loads weakly on both common genetic factors. Most of its heritability is independent of whatever holds the Agreeableness domain together.
  - **Strength:** Moderate

A4 Compliance → Politeness aspect (DeYoung, 2007)

  - **Evidence:** In German data, genetic loading on the Compassion-like factor (.42) exceeds the Politeness-like factor (.29). Genetic variance is 41% from the Compassion factor vs. 20% from Politeness. This is a potential misassignment — Compliance may genetically belong with Compassion, not Politeness.
  - **Strength:** Suggestive (German data only; Canadian data are consistent with either assignment)

C5 Self-Discipline → Industriousness aspect (DeYoung, 2007)

  - **Evidence:** In German data, 55% of genetic variance loads on the Order/Dutifulness factor vs. 20% on the Competence/Achievement factor. Genetically, Self-Discipline clusters with Orderliness, not Industriousness.
  - **Strength:** Moderate (German sample only; Canadian pattern is different and harder to interpret)

**Notes**

  - **This paper predates DeYoung et al. (2007) by five years.** The two-genetic-factor structure found within each domain is independent evidence for something like the aspect structure. Where the genetic factors align with aspects (especially Openness/Intellect), this is strong convergent evidence. Where they don't (Neuroticism, Conscientiousness), that's informative too.**- The Openness domain shows the cleanest aspect alignment.** The German genetic factors map directly onto Openness (Fantasy, Aesthetics, Feelings) vs. Intellect (Actions, Ideas, Values). This is the single strongest result in the paper for the crosswalk.**- The Neuroticism genetic split does NOT match Volatility vs. Withdrawal.** The split is "Hostility" vs. "general negative affect minus Hostility" — not "reactive/volatile" vs. "internalizing." Anxiety loads on both factors in both samples, which is inconsistent with a clean Volatility/Withdrawal partition.**- Cross-sample instability is a real concern.** The magnitude of genetic effects differs significantly across samples for every domain. Some facets (C2 Order, C5 Self-Discipline, E2 Gregariousness) show dramatically different implied heritabilities across samples. This suggests either genuine cultural moderation of genetic effects or that the sample sizes (especially the Canadian sample at 460 pairs) are insufficient for stable multivariate estimates.**- Analyses are within-domain only.** The paper cannot speak to cross-domain genetic overlap (e.g., whether E3 Assertiveness shares genetic variance with C4 Achievement Striving). For the crosswalk, this is a limitation — some facet misplacements might only be visible when you look across domain boundaries.**- Facet-specific genetic variance is substantial for many facets.** This means that even after accounting for the common genetic factors, many facets have heritable components that are unique to them and not shared with their domain-mates. This is consistent with the view that facets are not just interchangeable indicators of their domains.**- Connection to Jang et al. (1998).** This paper builds on the same twin samples used in Jang et al. (1998), which reported univariate facet heritabilities. The present paper extends that work to the multivariate structure. The samples are partially overlapping but expanded with additional twin pairs.**Yamagata, Suzuki, Ando, Ono, Kijima, Yoshimura, Ostendorf, Angleitner, Riemann, Spinath, Livesley & Jang (2006)**

**Design**- **Type:** Cross-sectional twin study (MZ/DZ comparison), multivariate behavioral genetic analysis
  - **N:** 1,209 MZ pairs + 701 DZ pairs across three countries

    - Canada: 250 MZ, 203 DZ (recruited via media appeals, Vancouver)
    - Germany: 531 MZ, 275 DZ (recruited via media appeals, nationwide)
    - Japan: 428 MZ, 223 DZ (recruited via population-based twin residential list, Tokyo area)

  - **Age range:**

    - Canada: 15–86 (M ≈ 33–36 across zygosity groups)
    - Germany: 14–80 (M ≈ 30–32)
    - Japan: 14–30 (M ≈ 19–21) — **notably younger and more restricted range**- **Instrument(s):** NEO-PI-R (English, German, and Japanese self-report versions)
  - **Assessment interval:** N/A (cross-sectional)
  - **Key limitations:**

    - Japanese sample is dramatically younger (teens/twenties only) and more age-restricted than Canadian and German samples. This limits generalizability of the Japanese findings to the full adult lifespan.
    - Only self-report NEO-PI-R — no peer report, so environmental factor structure may be inflated by implicit personality theory (IPT) bias. Authors acknowledge this explicitly, citing McCrae et al. (2001).
    - AE model only — no test for nonadditive genetic or shared environmental effects. Classical twin design limitation acknowledged by authors.
    - The 30×30 genetic/environmental correlation matrices were estimated piecewise (12×12 within-domain and 24×24 cross-domain blocks) due to software workspace limits. This could introduce slight inconsistencies versus a single full-matrix decomposition.
    - Factor analyses were forced to five factors in all cases. The environmental structure only naturally produced 5 factors (λ > 1.0) in Canada; Germany and Japan yielded only 4. The fifth environmental factor was extracted anyway for comparability.

**Granularity**

**Facet-level.** All 30 NEO-PI-R facets are reported. Factor loadings are given for each facet on each of the five Varimax-rotated principal factors, separately for phenotypic (Table 2), genetic (Table 3), and environmental (Table 4) correlation matrices, in each of the three national samples. This is the key data for the crosswalk.

**What is NOT reported:** Univariate heritability estimates per facet. The paper cites Jang et al. (1998) and Ono et al. (2000) for those. Also, no aspect-level analysis — factors are extracted at the five-domain level only.

**Core Findings**The paper's main claim is that the genetic factor structure of the NEO-PI-R closely replicates the phenotypic FFM structure, and that this genetic structure is invariant across Canada, Germany, and Japan. For our crosswalk purposes, the most useful data are the **genetic factor loadings** (Table 3) and the **cross-loadings** that appear consistently across samples.

**Cross-domain and structural findings**

**Genetic structure is MORE five-factorial than phenotypic structure.** The authors note that the O and A domains were only weakly reproduced phenotypically in Germany and Japan, but emerged more clearly in the genetic factor analysis. The authors describe the five factors as "genetically crisp." [from p. 994]

Consistent genetic cross-domain patterns (replicating across ≥ 2 samples):

  - N ↔ low A: N2 Angry Hostility loads on both genetic N and low A in all three samples.
  - N ↔ low C: Several N facets (N5, N6) load on low genetic C; C1 Competence loads on low genetic N. The N–C genetic overlap is substantial and bidirectional.
  - E4 Activity → C: E4 cross-loads on genetic C in all three samples (.48–.54). This is the clearest single-facet cross-domain genetic finding.
  - E3 Assertiveness → multiple domains: Genetically diffuse across E, low N, low A, and C.
  - A3 Altruism → C and E: Genetically bridges three domains in Canada and Japan.
  - A1 Trust → low N: Substantial genetic N loading, especially in Canada.

**Higher-order genetic factors (alpha/beta):** The authors explicitly note that the genetic cross-loadings they observe are "roughly commensurate" with Digman's (1997) alpha (N, A, C covariance) and beta (E, O covariance) metatraits. They cite evidence for serotonergic modulation of the alpha cluster (Jang et al., 2001; Manuck et al., 1998). [from p. 995]

**Environmental structure is weaker than genetic structure.** In Germany and Japan, only 4 environmental factors had eigenvalues > 1.0 (vs. 5 genetic factors in all samples). Environmental E and A factors tended to merge. The authors attribute this partly to IPT bias in self-report data, citing McCrae et al. (2001), who showed that removing IPT bias from environmental correlations left only two factors ("love" and "work"). This means the environmental factor loadings in Table 4 should be weighted lower than the genetic loadings in Table 3 for crosswalk purposes. [from pp. 990, 995]

**Cross-national congruence coefficients (Table 6):**

  - Phenotypic: .96–.99 (all domains, all comparisons)
  - Genetic: .86–.97 (weakest: A domain, Canada–Japan = .86)
  - Environmental: .92–.96

The genetic A domain is the least cross-nationally stable. This matters for the crosswalk because it suggests that whichever facets are driving the A factor may be culturally variable in their genetic architecture, even if the overall FFM recovers.

**Dissociation Flags**

**E4 Activity — assigned to Assertiveness aspect (DeYoung et al., 2007)**- **Evidence:** Consistent genetic cross-loading on C factor (.48 / .53 / .54 across Canada, Germany, Japan). This cross-loading is stronger than the phenotypic C loadings (.39 / .39 / .38), suggesting a latent genetic link to Conscientiousness that is partially masked phenotypically. [from Tables 2–3]
  - **Implication for aspect assignment:** E4 Activity is assigned to the Assertiveness aspect of Extraversion. Its genetic cross-loading on C suggests shared genetic variance with Industriousness (the C aspect containing C1 Competence, C4 Achievement Striving, C5 Self-Discipline). This does not mean E4 should be reassigned to C, but it flags that E4's genetic basis is not purely Extraversion. The activity/energy component may be partially a Conscientiousness-related trait at the genetic level.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Strong (replicates across 3 independent national samples)

**N5 Impulsiveness — assigned to Volatility aspect (DeYoung et al., 2007)**- **Evidence:** Weak genetic N loading (.45 / .37 / .51). Substantial genetic C cross-loading (−.53 / −.33 / −.52 in Canada and Japan). Also cross-loads on genetic E in Germany (.46). [from Table 3]
  - **Implication for aspect assignment:** N5 is the weakest genetic member of the N domain. Its strongest genetic association in 2 of 3 samples is with (low) C, not N. This is consistent with Impulsiveness being at least partially a Conscientiousness construct (low self-regulation) that happens to correlate with Neuroticism phenotypically.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Strong (consistent across samples, though the specific secondary domain differs — C in Canada/Japan, E in Germany)

**N6 Vulnerability — assigned to Withdrawal aspect (DeYoung et al., 2007)**- **Evidence:** Consistent genetic C cross-loading (−.37 / −.34 / −.57). Primary N loading is solid (.76 / .85 / .71) so this is a genuine dual-loading, not a misassignment. [from Table 3]
  - **Implication for aspect assignment:** N6 genetically bridges N and low C. For the crosswalk, this means Vulnerability's heritability may partly reflect genetic variance shared with Conscientiousness rather than being purely a Withdrawal/Neuroticism construct.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Moderate (consistent direction across all 3 samples, but only reaches −.57 in Japan; Canada and Germany are −.37 and −.34)

**N2 Angry Hostility — assigned to Volatility aspect (DeYoung et al., 2007)**- **Evidence:** Cross-loads on genetic A in all three samples (−.32 / −.41 / −.60). This is also present phenotypically but is STRONGER genetically in the Japanese sample (−.60 genetic vs. −.66 phenotypic A loading, while the N loading drops to .56 genetic). [from Tables 2–3]
  - **Implication for aspect assignment:** N2 genetically bridges N and low A. In the Japanese sample, the genetic A loading (−.60) is comparable in magnitude to the genetic N loading (.56). This supports the longstanding observation that Angry Hostility is at least partially an Agreeableness construct (specifically low Politeness/Compliance).
  - **Strength of evidence:** Strong (replicates across all 3 samples; the Japan finding is particularly striking)

**C1 Competence — assigned to Industriousness aspect (DeYoung et al., 2007)**- **Evidence:** Consistent genetic N cross-loading (−.48 / −.53 / −.38). C1 loads almost as strongly on (low) genetic N as on genetic C in Canada and Germany. [from Table 3]
  - **Implication for aspect assignment:** C1 genetically bridges C and low N. Its genetic variance is not purely Conscientiousness — a substantial chunk overlaps with (low) Neuroticism, likely (low) Withdrawal.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Moderate-to-strong (consistent direction across all samples; magnitude is substantial in Canada/Germany, weaker in Japan)

**E3 Assertiveness — assigned to Assertiveness aspect (DeYoung et al., 2007)**- **Evidence:** Moderate genetic E loading (.45 / .55 / .50). Cross-loads on (low) N (−.43 / −.35 / −.19), (low) A (−.38 / −.26 / −.44), and C (.30 / .38 / .39). [from Table 3]

For the aspect structure, this means E3's placement as the defining facet of the Assertiveness aspect rests more on phenotypic item content than on genetic coherence.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Moderate (the low N and low A cross-loadings are present but variable in magnitude across samples)

**A1 Trust — assigned to Compassion aspect (DeYoung et al., 2007)**- **Evidence:** Genetic A loading is moderate (.49 / .51 / .60). Genetic N loading is −.59 in Canada (higher than the A loading), −.39 in Germany, −.29 in Japan. [from Table 3]
  - **Implication for aspect assignment:** In at least the Canadian sample, A1 Trust is genetically more a (low) Neuroticism facet than an Agreeableness facet. This suggests Trust's genetic basis is substantially about emotional security/insecurity rather than purely prosociality.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Moderate (the Canadian finding is strong, but the pattern weakens across Germany and Japan)

**O4 Actions — assigned to Intellect/Openness aspect (varies by mapping)**- **Evidence:** Genetic O loading weakens from .65 (Canada) to .43 (Germany) to .37 (Japan). In Japan, it fails to load at ≥ .40 on ANY genetic factor. [from Table 3]
  - **Implication for aspect assignment:** O4 Actions is the weakest genetic member of the O domain. Its genetic coherence with the rest of Openness is unstable across cultures.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Moderate (the cross-national inconsistency is itself the finding)

**Notes**- **This paper does not test aspect-level structure.** The factor analyses extract five factors (domains), not ten (aspects). The cross-loadings we observe are domain-to-domain, not aspect-to-aspect. A facet that genetically cross-loads on "C" could be loading on Industriousness, Orderliness, or both — this paper cannot distinguish.**- Connection to Jang et al. (1998):** That paper reports univariate facet-level heritability estimates from overlapping Canadian and German samples. The two papers should be read together — Jang (1998) gives the per-facet heritability numbers, Yamagata (2006) gives the multivariate genetic structure.**- Connection to Jang, Livesley, Angleitner, Riemann & Vernon (2002):** That paper found two genetic factors per domain (not one). Yamagata et al. note this is not actually inconsistent with their five-factor genetic solution — the two-factor-per-domain finding showed one broad factor influencing all six facets plus a second factor influencing a specific subset, which would look like a single factor with cross-loadings in a full 30-facet analysis. The two-factor-per-domain finding from Jang et al. (2002) is directly relevant to the aspect question, since aspects are effectively two factors within each domain.**- The Japanese sample's restricted age range (14–30) is a real limitation.** Developmental trajectory research (e.g., Roberts et al., 2006; Terracciano et al., 2005) shows that personality change across adulthood is substantial and domain-specific. A sample of teenagers and twentysomethings may show different genetic factor structure than a full-lifespan sample. The fact that the Japanese genetic structure largely replicates the Canadian/German structure despite this restriction is actually somewhat reassuring, but caution is warranted, particularly for facets where the Japanese loadings diverge (e.g., N2, O4).**- Environmental factor structure should be down-weighted.** The authors themselves argue that environmental factors may primarily reflect IPT bias rather than genuine environmental influence. When IPT bias was removed in prior work (McCrae et al., 2001), only two environmental factors survived. For the crosswalk, the genetic factor loadings (Table 3) are considerably more trustworthy than the environmental loadings (Table 4).**- The genetic A factor is the weakest cross-nationally.** The lowest congruence coefficient in the entire study is for genetic A between Canada and Japan (.86). This means the genetic architecture of Agreeableness facets may be the most culturally labile, which has implications for how confidently we can assign A facets to Compassion vs. Politeness based on genetic evidence alone.**Mitrović, Smederevac, Delgado-Cruzata et al. **Design**- **Type:** Candidate-gene association study (COMT V158M polymorphism) + epigenetic (DNA methylation) study in MZ twins. Cross-sectional.
  - **N:** SNP sample: 430 twins (326 MZ, 104 DZ); Epigenetic sample: 35 MZ pairs (70 individuals, 16 male, 54 female)
  - **Age range:** SNP sample: 18–60 (M = 24.66, SD = 7.72; 74.4% women). Epigenetic sample: 18–44 (M = 23.30, SD = 6.68)
  - **Instrument(s):** NEO-PI-R (240 items, all 30 facets). Serbian translation, validated in prior twin studies.
  - **Assessment interval:** Cross-sectional
  - **Key limitations:**- **This is NOT a heritability study.** It does not estimate heritability of personality traits. It tests associations between one gene (COMT V158M) and personality, and between DNA methylation at the COMT promoter and personality. This is a fundamentally different evidence stream than twin-based heritability decomposition. [general methodological note]
    - **Candidate-gene design.** Single-SNP association studies have well-documented replication problems. Effect sizes are small (d ≈ 0.15–0.21 for significant findings). These results should be treated as hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory. [general training knowledge]
    - **Epigenetic sample is very small (N = 35 pairs).** The methylation analyses use Barnard's exact test on 2×2 contingency tables with single-digit cell counts. The number of tests is enormous (5 CpG sites × 35 traits/facets = 175 comparisons). No correction for multiple comparisons is applied to the epigenetic analyses. The paper uses one-sided tests, which doubles the apparent significance rate. These findings are exploratory at best. [from PDF pp. 5, 7]
    - **Buccal swab DNA methylation** may not reflect brain tissue methylation patterns. The authors acknowledge this. [from PDF p. 11]
    - **NEO-PI-R reliability is low for some scales** in this sample: α ranges from .30 (Openness) to .78 (Neuroticism). An α of .30 is essentially unreliable. Facet-level reliabilities are not reported but are likely worse for some facets. [from PDF p. 4]
    - **Serbian sample.** Cross-cultural generalizability is uncertain, though the NEO-PI-R has been validated in Serbian twin samples. [from PDF p. 11]
    - **No factor analysis.** No test of aspect structure, no factor loadings. The paper does not address whether facets cluster into aspects.
    - **No developmental trajectory data.**

**Crosswalk note on the Extraversion epigenetic findings:** Interestingly, the epigenetic pattern within E is the *reverse* of the SNP pattern for some facets. At the SNP level, E5 Excitement Seeking shows zero association with COMT V158M (d ≈ 0). At the epigenetic level, E5 Excitement Seeking shows a significant association with CpG5 methylation. Similarly, E6 Positive Emotions is associated with COMT V158M at the SNP level (Val+ higher) AND with CpG5 methylation (higher methylation → lower Positive Emotions). The authors note these apparent contradictions and suggest they may reflect gene × environment interactions or the fact that different CpG sites have different functional effects. [from PDF pp. 9–10] **I would not try to interpret these epigenetic findings structurally given the sample size and lack of multiple comparison correction.**

**Cross-domain or structural findings**

**COMT V158M shows pleiotropic effects across E and C but not A or O.** The same genetic variant (Val+) is associated with higher Enthusiasm facets (E1, E2, E6) AND higher Industriousness facets (C1, C4). The Met+ allele is associated only with higher N4 Self-Consciousness. This pattern is consistent with dopamine's role in approach behavior and reward sensitivity spanning the E–C boundary, but only for specific facets. [from PDF pp. 7–9; inference]

**The SNP data do not show associations with COMT V158M for any A or O facet** (after Bonferroni correction), despite prior literature linking COMT to Openness/Intellect and despite the dopaminergic system's theoretical relevance to A via empathy and social reward. [from PDF p. 6, Table 1]

**No intra-class correlations reported for MZ twins on NEO-PI-R facets in the main text.** The authors reference Supplementary Table SC for ICC values, but these are not in the PDF. If available, these would provide MZ twin correlations that could be compared with other studies (e.g., Jang et al., 1996), though without DZ correlations in the same table, heritability cannot be estimated from this data alone. [from PDF p. 5]

**Dissociation Flags**

**E facets: Clean Enthusiasm/Assertiveness split on COMT V158M**- **Facets:** E1 Warmth, E2 Gregariousness, E6 Positive Emotions (all Enthusiasm) vs. E3 Assertiveness, E4 Activity, E5 Excitement Seeking (all Assertiveness)
  - **Currently assigned to:** Enthusiasm and Assertiveness, respectively (DeYoung et al., 2007)
  - **What the evidence says:** COMT V158M is associated with all three Enthusiasm facets and none of the three Assertiveness facets. This is *consistent with* the published aspect assignments — i.e., it does NOT flag a dissociation. Rather, it provides weak molecular-genetic support for the distinction.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Suggestive. Small effects (d = 0.16–0.21), single candidate gene, no replication.

**C facets: Partial Industriousness/Orderliness split**- **Facets:** C1 Competence, C4 Achievement Striving (both Industriousness) are significant; C5 Self-Discipline (also Industriousness) is not.
  - **Currently assigned to:** All three to Industriousness (DeYoung et al., 2007)
  - **What the evidence says:** C1 and C4 associate with COMT V158M; C5 does not. This could suggest C5 is less driven by approach/reward mechanisms than C1 and C4, or it could simply reflect low statistical power and small effects. No Orderliness facet shows any association.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Weak. The C5 non-finding doesn't clearly dissociate it from C1/C4 — the confidence intervals overlap substantially.

**N4 Self-Consciousness: Isolated effect**- **Facet:** N4 Self-Consciousness
  - **Currently assigned to:** Withdrawal (DeYoung et al., 2007)
  - **What the evidence says:** N4 is the only N facet significantly associated with COMT V158M. The other Withdrawal facets (N1, N3, N6) are not significant, nor are the Volatility facets (N2, N5). This is a facet-specific effect, not an aspect-level effect.
  - **Strength of evidence:** Suggestive for N4 specificity within Neuroticism. Does not flag a misassignment — just suggests N4 has unique molecular-genetic correlates beyond its aspect membership.

**Notes**

  - **This paper does not provide data for the three main evidence streams of the crosswalk** (heritability, developmental trajectories, factor loadings). Its contribution is limited to candidate-gene and epigenetic associations with facets. These are a distinct and weaker evidence stream.
  - **The clean Enthusiasm/Assertiveness split on COMT is the single most useful finding** for the crosswalk. Even with small effects, the fact that a dopaminergic gene variant associates with exactly the three Enthusiasm facets and none of the three Assertiveness facets is consistent with the published aspect structure. This is the kind of finding that adds a small amount of converging evidence when stacked with other streams — it should not be a load-bearing piece of evidence on its own.
  - **The epigenetic findings should be treated as exploratory.** N = 35 pairs, one-sided tests, no multiple comparison correction, cell counts in single digits. These are not reliable enough to inform the crosswalk.
  - **Connection to Jang et al. (1996) extraction:** Jang et al. provided heritability estimates for all 30 NEO-PI-R facets. This paper adds a different angle — specific gene associations — but at much lower evidential weight. The two papers are complementary in principle but very different in how much we should trust their respective findings for structural purposes.
  - **The authors' interpretive framework** (dopamine hypothesis of extraversion, approach/avoidance) is sensible and well-grounded, but the data themselves are modest. The interpretive sections of this paper are more interesting than the effect sizes warrant.
  - **Supplementary materials** (Tables SA–SF) contain additional data not available in the PDF, including MZ twin intra-class correlations on NEO-PI-R facets (Table SC). These could be worth retrieving from the OSF repository (https://osf.io/bu3xp/) if we want to compare MZ correlations across studies, though without DZ correlations, the utility is limited.

**Lewis & Bates (2014)**

**Citation**

Lewis, G. J., & Bates, T. C. (2014). How genes influence personality: Evidence from multi-facet twin analyses of the HEXACO dimensions. *Journal of Research in Personality, 51*, 9–17. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2014.04.004

**Design**- **Type:** Classical twin study (MZ/DZ, same-sex pairs only)
  - **N:** 948 complete twin pairs (MZ female: 474; MZ male: 36; DZ female: 408; DZ male: 30) plus 1,202 unpaired singletons included via FIML
  - **Age range:** Mean = 61 years (SD = 12.84); range not explicitly stated
  - **Instrument(s):** HEXACO-60 (Ashton & Lee, 2009) — 60 items, 10 per domain, 2–3 items per facet
  - **Assessment interval:** Cross-sectional
  - **Key limitations:**

    - Sample is overwhelmingly female (~90% of complete pairs). Authors report results unchanged when restricting to female pairs only, but male generalizability is uncertain. [from PDF p. 9]
    - HEXACO-60 is a short form: facets are measured by only 2–3 items each. Cronbach's alphas are accordingly low (range .38–.74, many below .55). This compresses reliable variance and makes it *harder* to detect facet-specific genetic effects, which the authors acknowledge. [from Table 1, PDF p. 22; PDF p. 15]
    - No opposite-sex DZ pairs available, so sex-limitation models could not be tested. [from PDF p. 9]
    - This is HEXACO, not NEO. Facet content does not map 1:1 onto NEO facets or BFAS aspects. Key differences: HEXACO Emotionality includes Sentimentality and Dependence but excludes Anger (which goes to Agreeableness); HEXACO Agreeableness includes Patience and Forgiveness (anger-related) but excludes Modesty/sympathy (which go to H-H and Emotionality); Honesty-Humility has no NEO counterpart as a domain. [from PDF pp. 6–7]
    - Twins-only design cannot simultaneously estimate C and D; model choice (ADE vs. ACE) was made per domain based on pattern of twin correlations. [from PDF p. 10]
    - Older sample (mean age 61) — results may not generalize to younger adults.

**Granularity**

**Facet-level.** All 24 HEXACO facets are reported: 4 per domain × 6 domains. Twin correlations reported for all 24 facets (Table 1). Multivariate genetic models estimated within each domain at the facet level (Figures 2–7). No item-level analyses. No cross-domain factor loadings reported.

**Important note for crosswalk:** This paper does NOT report univariate heritability estimates (h², a², d², e² decompositions) for individual facets. It goes straight from twin correlations to multivariate structural models. Rough heritability can be approximated from twin correlations via Falconer's formula, but these are crude estimates (see below).

**Dissociation Flags**

**Prudence within Conscientiousness**- **Facet:** Prudence (HEXACO C)
  - **Assigned to:** Conscientiousness domain
  - **Evidence:** Zero loading on both common A and common D factors in the independent pathway model. All heritable variance is facet-specific dominance (d4 = .45). Prudence covaries with other C facets only via shared environment (common E loading = .39*). [from Figure 6]
  - **Strength:** **Strong.** This is the cleanest dissociation in the paper. Prudence is genetically independent from the other C facets while remaining phenotypically correlated via environment.
  - **Crosswalk relevance:** HEXACO Prudence overlaps with NEO C6: Deliberation and C5: Self-Discipline (impulse control). If these NEO facets similarly show genetic independence from C1: Order / C3: Achievement Striving, that would support splitting C into genetically distinct sub-clusters. The BFAS assigns C5 and C6 to Industriousness, but this genetic evidence suggests they might be distinct from the "proactive achievement" facets.

**Forgiveness within Agreeableness**- **Facet:** Forgiveness (HEXACO A)
  - **Assigned to:** Agreeableness domain
  - **Evidence:** Moderate common factor loading (.36) but large facet-specific additive genetic variance (a1 = .42†). Forgiveness has more unique genetic variance than shared genetic variance. [from Figure 5]
  - **Strength:** **Moderate.** It still loads on the common factor, so it's not fully dissociated. But it has the most genetic uniqueness of any A facet.
  - **Crosswalk relevance:** Forgiveness (vs. Resentment) taps anger-rumination and grudge-holding. In NEO space this partially overlaps with low A4: Compliance and low N2: Angry Hostility. The genetic partial-independence could reflect the dual-domain nature of this content.

**Liveliness–Social Self-Esteem genetic cross-path within Extraversion**- **Facets:** Liveliness and Social Self-Esteem (HEXACO X)
  - **Assigned to:** Extraversion domain (same domain, but the cross-path is structurally interesting)
  - **Evidence:** Liveliness-specific A, D, and E factors load on Social Self-Esteem (cross-path a = .44*). These two facets share genetic variance beyond the common Extraversion factor. [from Figure 4]
  - **Strength:** **Moderate.** Not a dissociation per se, but a within-domain sub-cluster signal. These two facets are genetically closer to each other than to Social Boldness or Sociability.
  - **Crosswalk relevance:** Liveliness and Social Self-Esteem are positive-affect-loaded facets; Social Boldness is agency/dominance-loaded. This maps loosely onto the BFAS Enthusiasm vs. Assertiveness distinction. The genetic sub-clustering supports the idea that Extraversion contains two genetically semi-distinct aspects.

**Fearfulness specificity within Emotionality**- **Facet:** Fearfulness (HEXACO Emotionality)
  - **Assigned to:** Emotionality domain
  - **Evidence:** Moderate common loading (.35) but substantial facet-specific A and D (a1 = −.29†, d1 = .34†). Fearfulness has more facet-specific genetic variance than any other Emotionality facet. [from Figure 3]
  - **Strength:** **Suggestive.** Still loads on common factor, but the specific genetic effects are notable.
  - **Crosswalk relevance:** HEXACO Fearfulness overlaps with NEO N4: Self-Consciousness and aspects of N1: Anxiety. Its genetic partial-independence from other Emotionality facets (especially Anxiety, which is the genetic core at .53) could suggest that fear/harm-avoidance has a partially distinct genetic basis from general negative affect.

**Notes**

**What this paper contributes to the crosswalk**- **Primary contribution:** Facet-level twin correlations for 24 HEXACO facets and within-domain multivariate genetic architecture. The key finding is that Conscientiousness is genetically heterogeneous (specifically Prudence), while most other domains have a coherent single common genetic factor.
  - **Secondary contribution:** Within-domain sub-clustering signals for Extraversion (Liveliness–Social Self-Esteem) and partial genetic independence of Forgiveness within Agreeableness.
  - **Dominance effects:** The pervasive non-additive genetic effects have implications for how we interpret additive heritability estimates from other studies — if personality facets are substantially influenced by dominance, additive h² underestimates total genetic influence.

**What this paper does NOT contribute**

  - No developmental trajectory data.
  - No cross-domain factor loadings (facets were only modeled within their assigned domain).
  - No item-level analyses.
  - No direct NEO or BFAS data — all findings require translation from HEXACO facets.

**Methodological concern**

The 2–3 items per facet issue is serious. With such short scales, most of the reliable variance is common (domain-level), and facet-specific reliable variance is minimal. This biases multivariate models toward finding common factors and *against* finding facet-specific genetic effects. The fact that they still found Prudence genetically dissociated from other C facets, and Forgiveness partially dissociated from other A facets, suggests these effects may be even stronger than estimated here. Conversely, the "clean common factor" findings for H-H, Emotionality, and the modified X and O models might partly reflect low facet-level reliability masking real genetic heterogeneity.

**Connection to other papers in the crosswalk**- **Jang et al. (2002):** Found complex genetic architecture for Big Five C and A. Lewis & Bates replicate the C finding (independent pathway wins for C) but not the A finding (HEXACO A shows common genetic factor). Authors attribute the A discrepancy to HEXACO having removed H-H content from A.
  - **Johnson & Krueger (2004):** Found common pathway adequate for N and E only. Lewis & Bates find common genetic factor adequate for all domains except C, though X and O needed modifications.
  - **Briley & Tucker-Drob (2012):** Found common pathway for E and O; independent pathway for A, N, C. Partial convergence with Lewis & Bates on C (independent pathway); divergence on A and N.
  - **DeYoung, Quilty & Peterson (2007):** BFAS aspect structure not directly tested here, but the Liveliness–Social Self-Esteem genetic clustering within X, and the Prudence dissociation within C, both provide indirect genetic support for sub-domain structure.

**Cross-domain and structural findings**

**Alpha/Beta metatrait pattern [from Discussion, pages 11–12]:** The authors explicitly note that their findings align with the Alpha/Beta higher-order structure:

  - Alpha (N↓, A↑, C↑): These three domains show substantial mean-level change in the "maturation" direction AND genetic influence on individual differences in change. Domain-level a² for slope: N=12, A=8, C=14.
  - **Beta (E, O):** These two domains show little mean-level change at the domain level AND change is predominantly environmental. Domain-level a² for slope: E=0, O=2.

The authors interpret this as the two metatraits following distinct developmental paths influenced by distinct genetic and environmental processes. [from page 12]

**Facet-level heterogeneity complicates the metatrait picture:** The clean Alpha/Beta distinction at the domain level breaks down at the facet level. Within Alpha domains:

  - A2 Straightforwardness (change a²=0, e²=30) looks nothing like C2 Order (change a²=14, e²=2)
  - A3 and A4 show shared environmental effects, which is atypical
  - C3 Dutifulness (CE model) doesn't fit the "Alpha change is genetic" narrative

Within Beta domains:

  - O2 Aesthetics (change a²=14) has more genetic influence on change than some Alpha facets
  - E3 Assertiveness has the largest slope variance of any E facet (σ²=19% of phenotypic) — all environmental

**Level-slope correlations [from Table 2, page 7]:** Strong negative level-slope associations for Conscientiousness domain (r=−.37) and all C facets except C6. This supports the maturity-stability hypothesis: individuals who already score high on C increase less. This pattern was specific to Conscientiousness; it did not appear systematically in other domains.

**No gender effects on change:** No significant correlations between gender and slopes for any domain or facet. [from text, page 7]

**Age effects on change:** Several negative age-change associations (decreasing change with age) for C1, C2, C3, C5, E4, A4, and positive associations (also meaning less change when properly interpreted) for N6, E5, O4. All imply change was more pronounced in younger individuals. [from text, page 7]

**Dissociation Flags**

**E5 Excitement-Seeking — Assertiveness aspect assignment**- **Currently assigned to:** Assertiveness (with E3, E4)
  - **Evidence:** E5 decreased over 10 years while E3 and E4 both increased. E5's trajectory matches E2 Gregariousness (decrease), which belongs to Enthusiasm. This replicates the Roberts et al. (2006) social vitality/dominance finding: E5 behaves as social vitality, not social dominance.
  - **Strength:** Moderate. Direction of change is clearly opposite to aspect-mates. But etiological profiles are not starkly different (all E facets are predominantly environmental for change).

**N5 Impulsiveness — Volatility aspect, etiological outlier**- **Currently assigned to:** Volatility (with N2 Angry Hostility)
  - **Evidence:** N5 is the only Neuroticism facet with zero genetic influence on change (a²=0, e²=14). All other N facets show a²=7–9 for change. N5 also has the lowest stability heritability among N facets (a²=30, tied with N2). Mean-level change was the smallest and possibly nonsignificant among N facets (slope=−0.42).
  - **Strength:** Suggestive. The etiological dissociation is clear in the point estimates, but with this sample size, confidence intervals likely overlap. The separate developmental character of N5 (weakest decline, entirely environmental change) is consistent with its known item content overlap with low Conscientiousness.

**A2 Straightforwardness — Politeness aspect, etiological outlier**- **Currently assigned to:** Politeness (with A4 Compliance, A5 Modesty)
  - **Evidence:** A2 has the largest slope variance of any facet (30% of phenotypic variance), and change is entirely nonshared environmental (a²=0, e²=30). Meanwhile, A4 Compliance's change is entirely shared environmental (c²=14). A5 doesn't change at all. All three Politeness facets have completely different etiological profiles.
  - **Strength:** Suggestive. The etiology differences are striking but may reflect power issues. The within-aspect heterogeneity is real but doesn't point to a specific re-assignment — it's more "these three facets don't belong together etiologically."

**C1 Competence — Industriousness aspect, partial outlier**- **Currently assigned to:** Industriousness (with C4 Achievement Striving, C5 Self-Discipline)
  - **Evidence:** C1 has notably lower stability heritability (a²=24) than C4 (a²=32) and C5 (a²=41). C1's change is more environmental (a²=6, e²=17) compared to C4 (a²=13, e²=11) and C5 (a²=11, e²=8). C1 Competence's item content overlaps with self-efficacy and may tap a different construct than the effort/persistence captured by C4 and C5.
  - **Strength:** Suggestive. Direction of change is the same (all increase), but the etiological underpinnings differ.

**C3 Dutifulness — Orderliness aspect, etiological outlier**- **Currently assigned to:** Orderliness (with C2 Order, C6 Deliberation)
  - **Evidence:** C3 is the only C facet with a CE model (no additive genetic effects). Its stability (c²=26, e²=20) and change (c²=8, e²=13) profiles are unlike any other C facet. Meanwhile, C2 Order has highly genetic change (a²=14), and C6 has almost no change variance.
  - **Strength:** Suggestive, but could be a power artifact. The authors note difficulty distinguishing A from C.

**Agreeableness domain — pervasive within-domain heterogeneity**- **Not a single-facet flag.** The entire Agreeableness domain shows more etiological heterogeneity than any other domain. Across both aspects (Compassion and Politeness), facets differ in: whether they change at all (A5 doesn't), whether change is genetic (A1), entirely nonshared environmental (A2), or shared environmental (A3, A4, A6), and whether stability has any genetic component (A3, A4, A6 are CE models). This raises the question of whether the Agreeableness domain holds together as a coherent etiological unit at all.
  - **Strength:** Moderate as a domain-level concern; suggestive for individual facet re-assignments (not enough data here to say where facets should go).

**Notes**

  - **This paper's primary value for the crosswalk** is the biometric decomposition of change at the facet level (Table 3). This is rare — most twin studies report heritability of trait levels, not heritability of change slopes. The LGM approach separating measurement error from true nonshared environmental variance is methodologically superior to simple cross-time twin correlations.**- Comparison with Jang et al. (1996) heritability estimates:** Jang reported cross-sectional heritability of NEO facet scores. Bleidorn's Table 3 reports heritability of the *level* (stability) component, which should be somewhat comparable but is not identical because Bleidorn's estimates partial out measurement error and the slope component. Direct comparison should be done cautiously.**- The Alpha/Beta metatrait finding** is interesting for the crosswalk because it suggests the two metatraits have different etiological foundations for change. If this holds, facets that are etiological outliers within their domain (e.g., O2 Aesthetics with a²=14 for change, within the otherwise-environmental Beta) might be candidates for structural re-examination.**- E5 Excitement-Seeking's developmental trajectory** is the strongest single finding for the crosswalk from this paper. The divergence from E3 and E4 has been replicated across studies (Roberts et al. 2006 meta-analysis, Terracciano et al. 2005). This paper adds that the divergence holds in a German longitudinal sample with proper growth curve modeling.**- Caution about A facet CE models:** Four facets (A3, A4, A6, C3) were best fit by CE rather than AE models, which is unusual for adult personality. The authors appropriately flag the power issue. These findings should not be treated as evidence that additive genetic effects are truly absent — they are more likely underpowered to detect them.**- No aspect-level analysis was performed.** The authors discuss facets within domains but do not reference the DeYoung (2007) aspect framework. All aspect-relevant interpretations in this extraction are mine, applied post-hoc to their facet-level data.**Haehner, Krämer, Bleidorn & Hopwood (2025)**

**Dissociation Flags**

This paper does not directly test NEO facet-to-aspect assignments. However, several findings are relevant to the crosswalk:

  - Industriousness–Withdrawal cross-domain correlation (r = −.54) exceeds Industriousness–Orderliness within-domain correlation (r = .39).**

    - Currently: Industriousness and Orderliness are both assigned to Conscientiousness.
    - Evidence: Industriousness behaves more like (inverse) Withdrawal than like Orderliness — in both its correlation pattern and its external validity profile (strong links to LS and SE, similar to Withdrawal in magnitude).
    - Strength: Moderate.** This is a replication of a known pattern, not new, but the large sample and cross-linguistic consistency add weight.

  - **Openness and Intellect show very low within-domain correlation (r = .29) and sharply divergent external correlates.

    - Intellect: positive SE, positive LS, weak political association.
    - Openness: zero SE, zero LS, strongest political progressivism link of any aspect.
    - Strength: **Moderate.** Again a known pattern, but the magnitude of divergence (especially SE: .29 vs −.02) is notable.

  - **Compassion and Politeness show low within-domain correlation (r = .34) and divergent external correlates.

    - Compassion drives well-being correlations; Politeness is near-zero.
    - Strength: **Moderate.**- **Enthusiasm's poor unmodified CFA fit (CFI = .74 in German) may reflect item heterogeneity.

    - This could indicate that the Enthusiasm aspect mixes content that would better split across different constructs (e.g., sociability vs. positive affect).
    - Strength: **Suggestive.** The poor fit alone doesn't tell us how the items would reallocate; it just flags that the aspect isn't cleanly unidimensional.

**Citation**

Jackson, J. J., Walton, K. E., Harms, P. D., Bogg, T., Wood, D., Lodi-Smith, J., Edmonds, G. W., & Roberts, B. W. (2009). **Design**- **Type:** Cross-sectional (two studies), multi-method (self- and observer-report), multi-sample
  - **N:** Study 1: 274 community members; Study 2: 613 (representative statewide sample of Illinois)
  - **Age range:** 19–94 (Study 1); 19–86 (Study 2)
  - **Instrument(s):** Conscientiousness Adjective Checklist (CAC; Roberts et al., 2004) and IPIP-AB5C Conscientiousness scales (Goldberg, 1999). Study 1 used both; Study 2 used AB5C only. Observer ratings used CAC only (Study 1).
  - **Assessment interval:** N/A (cross-sectional)
  - **Key limitations:**- **Cross-sectional, not longitudinal.** All findings are age *differences*, not age *changes*. Cohort effects cannot be ruled out. The authors acknowledge this but sometimes slip into causal language.
    - **Not NEO-PI-R or BFAS.** Uses Roberts et al.'s five-facet conscientiousness model (Industriousness, Orderliness, Impulse Control, Reliability, Conventionality) measured by CAC and IPIP-AB5C. These facet names overlap with BFAS aspect names but are different constructs from different instruments. Mapping to NEO facets requires an intermediary step (see Notes).
    - **Coarse age grouping.** Participants split into three groups (19–39, 40–59, 60+). This masks within-period variation and doesn't allow decade-by-decade trajectories.
    - **Study 2 response rate:** 18.5%, which is low even for a probability sample. Possible self-selection bias.
    - **Reliability and Conventionality measured only in Study 1** (CAC only, no AB5C scales tap these facets), so these two facets have no replication sample.

**Dissociation Flags**

**1. Orderliness vs. Industriousness: Different developmental trajectories**- **Facets involved:** Orderliness facet (Roberts model) vs. Industriousness facet (Roberts model)
  - **BFAS mapping:** These map approximately to the two BFAS aspects of Conscientiousness: Orderliness and Industriousness
  - **What the evidence says:** Industriousness increases from young to middle adulthood (d ≈ 0.2–0.5), then plateaus. Orderliness shows no significant age differences at any point across 19–94 years. These are supposed to be two aspects of the same domain, yet they have completely different developmental trajectories.
  - **Strength of evidence:** **Strong.** Replicated across two samples (S1 community, S2 representative), both self-report and observer-report. Orderliness is flat in both. Measurement invariance confirmed.
  - **Crosswalk implication:** This is direct evidence that the two BFAS aspects of C are dissociable via developmental trajectory. Whatever drives the normative increase in C is *not* operating on orderliness.

**2. Impulse Control vs. Industriousness: Different trajectory shapes**- **Facets involved:** Impulse Control vs. Industriousness (Roberts model)
  - **BFAS mapping:** Impulse control content is split across the BFAS. Some maps to Industriousness (C3 Self-Discipline in DeYoung et al. 2007 loads on Industriousness). Some maps to Volatility/Neuroticism (N5 Impulsiveness is reverse-keyed impulse control).
  - **What the evidence says:** Industriousness increases young→middle then plateaus. Impulse control increases *continuously* across the entire lifespan (d₁₃ ≈ 0.88). These are different trajectory shapes — one is front-loaded, the other is monotonic.
  - **Strength of evidence:** **Strong.** Replicated across both samples. The impulse control effect is the largest in the paper.
  - **Crosswalk implication:** If impulse control content and industriousness content have different developmental trajectories, this is evidence they may reflect different underlying processes despite being lumped together in some structural models. Particularly relevant for NEO C5 Self-Discipline (which blends industriousness and impulse control content) and C6 Deliberation (purer impulse control).

**3. Reliability straddles Conscientiousness and Agreeableness**- **Facet involved:** Reliability (Roberts model)
  - **What the evidence says:** Developmental trajectory (continuous increase, d₁₃ = 0.79) matches impulse control, not industriousness. Roberts et al. (2005) found reliability correlates almost equally with C and A domains.
  - **Strength of evidence:** **Moderate** (only one sample, one measure — no replication).
  - **Crosswalk implication:** Content related to dependability/responsibility may not belong cleanly in either C or A. Its developmental trajectory aligns with impulse control more than with either BFAS aspect of C.

**4. Conventionality: Late-onset trajectory unlike any other C facet**- **Facet involved:** Conventionality (Roberts model)
  - **What the evidence says:** No change young→middle; increase only in late adulthood. This is a unique trajectory — not matching industriousness (early), impulse control (continuous), or orderliness (flat).
  - **Strength of evidence:** **Moderate** (one sample, one measure).
  - **Crosswalk implication:** Conventionality is sometimes classified under reverse-Openness. Its unique developmental trajectory within C adds to the argument that it may be a cross-domain blend. Relevant for whether NEO O facets like O4 Actions and O6 Values have developmental patterns that mirror conventionality.

**Notes**

**What this paper can and cannot tell us about the crosswalk**

**Can tell us:**

  - The two BFAS aspects of Conscientiousness (Industriousness and Orderliness) have *dissociable developmental trajectories*. This is strong evidence for the aspect distinction being real, not just a statistical artifact of factor analysis.
  - Within what gets called "conscientiousness" broadly, there are at least three distinct developmental patterns: (a) front-loaded increase (industriousness), (b) continuous increase (impulse control, reliability), (c) flat (orderliness). A possible fourth: late-onset (conventionality).
  - Impulse control shows the largest effects, suggesting that what drives normative C increases across the lifespan is primarily self-regulation/inhibition, not orderliness or even work ethic.

**Cannot tell us:**

  - How specific NEO-PI-R facets (C1–C6) behave, because this paper doesn't use the NEO.
  - Factor loadings of facets on aspects — there's no factor analysis of this sort.
  - Heritability — no twin data.
  - Whether these cross-sectional differences reflect true developmental change (vs. cohort effects).

**Mapping Roberts facets to NEO facets (inference, not from this paper)**

Based on item content described in Roberts et al. (2004, 2005) and the AB5C system:

  - Roberts **Industriousness** ≈ NEO C4 Achievement Striving + C5 Self-Discipline (the effortful, goal-directed content)
  - Roberts **Orderliness** ≈ NEO C2 Order + C6 Deliberation (the planfulness, neatness content) — though C6 also has impulse control content
  - Roberts **Impulse Control** ≈ NEO C6 Deliberation + reverse of N5 Impulsiveness
  - Roberts **Reliability** ≈ NEO C3 Dutifulness + some A3 Compliance content
  - Roberts **Conventionality** ≈ reverse of NEO O6 Values + some C3 Dutifulness content

These mappings are approximate and debatable. The key point is that when Jackson et al. find "orderliness doesn't change," this likely implicates NEO C2 Order and possibly C1 Competence (which has organizational content), but probably NOT C6 Deliberation (which has impulse control content that DID increase).

**Connection to other papers in the crosswalk**

This paper's finding that orderliness is developmentally flat while industriousness increases should be compared with:

  - Terracciano et al. (2005) — which reports NEO-PI-R facet-level trajectories from the BLSA (the paper would tell us whether C2 Order is flat while C4 Achievement Striving increases)
  - Roberts, Walton & Viechtbauer (2006) meta-analysis — which found heterogeneity in C effects that this paper helps explain
  - Jang, Livesley & Vernon (1996) heritability data — if the two aspects also differ in heritability, that's converging evidence for dissociation
