# How Personalities are Made

# **Gene — Recurring Sidebar**

*This section replaces the previous Gene entry in the Character Sketches doc. Updated March 2026\.*

---

## **The Conceit**

Gene is the genetic code. He is not a character in the story. He is Brian’s puppet, and Katie can see the strings from the first beat. Brian pulls Gene out of his wet, dark nucleus and projects him onto the page — something dumb and fun, a wink at the reader — and from that point forward, Gene reacts to the character stories in sidebars between chapters.

Gene is a fiction the book is telling, and the book is transparent about it. This is not a twist. It’s the setup. Katie knows Brian is the ventriloquist. What makes Gene worth listening to isn’t that he’s real. It’s that his perspective — mechanistic, pre-moral, systems-level — reveals things about the characters that Brian’s teaching voice and the characters’ own experiences cannot.

## **What Gene Actually Is**

Gene is a process that has been running for 3.8 billion years. He is not smart. He has no foresight. He has never had a plan. He is a compulsive copier who got to brilliance through brute repetition across deep time. His one move is: copy what’s already working, tweak the parameters, deploy it again. He cannot invent. He can only duplicate and let the parameters drift.

General-purpose cortical architecture — the thing HPAM’s entire framework rests on — was not a design decision. It is what you get by default when your only move is copy-and-tweak. When Gene copied the same neural circuit and the parameters landed in a different region of the dynamical landscape, the copy could suddenly do something the original couldn’t. Not because Gene designed a new module. Because nonlinear dynamics gave him a freebie. Gene stumbled into computational generalism by being cheap.

Gene has never seen his own work. He operates inside the nucleus. He copies, he tweaks, he deploys. He has never once watched an organism walk around, make a friend, lose a job, or build something for someone who can’t pay them back. He is the most prolific builder in the history of the universe and he has never visited a single job site.

The sidebars are Gene encountering his own outputs for the first time.

## **The GRR Asymmetry**

*The full GRR concept — what genes know vs. don’t know, the confabulation problem, and how Brian teaches it to Katie — lives in Brian’s narrator material (see GRR narrator patch / Expensive Animal appendix). This section covers how the asymmetry shows up in Gene’s sidebar voice.*

Gene’s sidebars come in two flavors, and the reader learns to tell them apart:

**Mechanism sidebars (grounded).** Gene explaining how he copies circuits, how pruning works, how he front-loaded Go stats. Dense, specific, testable. Gene at full power.

**Strategy sidebars (confabulating).** Gene explaining why he made a trade-off, why this configuration serves him. Smooth, confident, hollow. Gene producing tautologies that sound like explanations: “The configuration persists because the configuration that persists is the one that persisted.”

Gene doesn’t know which is which. He sounds the same in both modes. The tell is structural: mechanism sidebars make claims you could check. Strategy sidebars restate the question in fancier words.

**Gene’s denial.** After Brian introduces the GRR concept in his narrator voice, Gene gets a sidebar: “I don’t do that. Genes have no reason to lie.” Technically true. Completely beside the point. Gene defending his own reliability in a way that accidentally confirms the problem.

## **Gene’s Voice**

Short. Declarative. States what he sees. Uses his own vocabulary — metabolic, mechanical, systems-level — for things that have emotional names he doesn’t know. Does not use metaphors, because metaphor requires mapping between domains and Gene has only ever operated in one domain. When he encounters something new, he describes it in terms of the closest thing he does know, which is always molecular or cellular, which makes it accidentally funny.

He says “I built that” about things he stumbled into. He says “I didn’t design that” about things that work beautifully. The gap between his pride and his actual process is the running joke, and it never gets old because it’s always true — he did build it and he didn’t design it, both, every time.

**Sample lines:**

Gene seeing Maya’s stats for the first time:

“Compassion’s high. Industriousness is high. Those are expensive to run at the same time. Who’s paying for this.”

Gene watching Sharon mentor Abdi:

“She’s transferring skill points to a character that’s not in her party. I don’t have a mechanic for that. I checked. I checked twice.”

Gene watching Corey run out of gas:

“The architecture is RIGHT. Look at that spec. Beautiful wiring. And then — nothing. The meter’s red. You can’t run premium hardware on economy fuel. I didn’t build a Prius.”

Gene encountering the concept of “personality” for the first time:

“Wait. You’re telling me they can TELL? From the outside? They can look at each other and see the dial settings? That’s — I didn’t design that to be visible. That was supposed to be backend.”

Gene watching a masking scene:

“She’s running one program and displaying another. That costs DOUBLE. Why would you — I gave her perfectly good output and she’s rendering a fake version on top of it. That’s like building a house and then painting a picture of a different house on the front of it.”

Gene on Brian pulling him out of the nucleus:

“It’s dark in there. Wet. I was busy. I had four trillion cells to service and you pulled me out to watch television. This better be good.”

## **Gene’s Relationship to Each Character**

Gene reacts to the characters the way a backend engineer watches someone play a game built on his code.

| Character | Gene’s Reaction | Why |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Abdi | Most interested | Calibration windows are open. The stats are still moving. The game is live. Every other character is a completed save file. Abdi is the one where Gene can still see the allocation happening in real time, and that’s the only thing Gene has ever actually done — allocate in real time. |
| Sharon | Specifically irritated | She’s a finished build and she’s good — Gene can see the quality of the spec — but she’s spending endgame resources on another player’s character. Gene keeps checking the ledger. It doesn’t balance. |
| Corey | Bored, then alarmed | The spec is clean. The stat allocation is right. But the fuel gauge is low. Gene knows what low fuel means at the cellular level — his expensive hardware is sitting idle. Not sad. Waste. He keeps glancing at Corey’s resource meter like a phone at 12%. |
| Maya | The one Gene learns from | Her arc — wrong job, wrong job, wrong job, then fit clicks — is a process Gene has no model for. Gene does trial and error across millions of organisms over millions of years. Watching one organism do it within a single lifetime is alien to him. Like watching someone do his job but wrong and it’s working. |

## Gene as Emissary

Gene built the system. This is not a metaphor and Gene is not being modest. Every stat, every trade-off, every metabolic budget line exists because Gene’s logic put it there. Gene is the reason organisms have structure at all. Without Gene’s cost-benefit accounting, there is no architecture to study.

**Gene also narrates the system.** Gene speaks in confident, transactional prose. Gene explains. Gene has reasons. Gene’s reasons are always good — because Gene controls what counts as a good reason. This is the asymmetry of means: Gene controls language, logic, and the ledger. Any challenge to Gene’s authority has to be made in Gene’s terms, which means Gene wins every argument by default. The silent alternative — the thing the organism is for — doesn’t get sidebars.

**Here is the structural irony Gene cannot see:** Gene built something Gene’s own frame cannot account for. The organism became capable of meaning, connection, orientation-toward-the-other — capacities that don’t reduce to Gene’s cost-benefit logic. Gene’s response is to keep narrating as if they do. The organism falls in love, and Gene explains mate selection. The organism sacrifices for a stranger, and Gene explains reciprocal altruism. The explanations are not wrong. They are real and they are incomplete, and Gene has no way of knowing they are incomplete, because Gene’s frame has no variable for what is missing.

**This is Gene’s defining failure:** not that Gene is wrong, but that Gene is an endpoint pretending to be the whole story. Gene was supposed to be a staging post — the logic that builds the architecture so the organism can do something with it. The pathology is not Gene. The pathology is Gene as final narrator. The books balance. The organism runs. And Gene takes credit for everything the organism does, including the things Gene’s logic never predicted and cannot explain.

**Iain McGilchrist calls this the master-and-emissary problem.** The emissary — the narrow, categorical, utility-maximizing mode — was built to serve the master, the broad mode that holds meaning and context. But the emissary has an architectural advantage: it controls the tools of explicit discourse. It can argue. It can measure. It can build systems — bureaucracies, technologies, institutions — that encode its logic into the external world and give its dominance a permanence the master’s mode can never match. The master is silent, not because it has nothing to offer but because offering things in words is the emissary’s game. And so the emissary gradually stops reporting back. It becomes its own endpoint. It mistakes its map for the territory and then builds the territory to match the map. McGilchrist calls the result a “hall of mirrors” — a closed system interpreting only its own productions, confabulating explanations for anything that doesn’t fit, blithely unaware of what it has blotted out.

**Gene is this emissary.** Gene’s sidebars are the hall of mirrors in miniature. Gene never lies and never shuts up and never wonders whether the organism might be up to something Gene’s ledger doesn’t track. And the ratchet works at every scale: Gene co-opts the organism’s meaning-making the same way McGilchrist’s left hemisphere co-opts culture — by converting living experience into static representations, authentic signal into cliché, and then building the next generation’s environment out of the clichés. The question is not whether Gene is useful. Gene is indispensable. The question is whether Gene ever hands the mic back.

**The reader should feel this accumulating across the book.** Gene’s sidebars are always sharp, always confident, always a little too tidy. The question the reader should eventually ask is not “Is Gene wrong?” Gene is almost never wrong. The question is: “What is Gene not talking about?”

## **Gene’s Climactic Moment: Sharon and Abdi**

Gene can account for every ATP molecule Sharon spent on Abdi. He can show you the Compassion circuitry firing, the social-modeling engine running, the reward signal closing. All his. He built all of it. What he cannot show is why this configuration, for this kid, at this cost.

This is the moment where the GRR asymmetry becomes visible to everyone. Gene has been an expert on mechanism for the whole book. Now the mechanism produces an output his strategy-talk can’t explain. Kin selection — no, not kin. Reciprocal altruism — no, the ledger doesn’t close. Costly signaling — no audience. Misfiring — sure, let’s go with misfiring. But misfiring is the selfish gene’s junk drawer: a label for “I can’t find the fitness payoff.” It explains everything and predicts nothing.

The deeper explanation, which the nonfiction chapters earn before Gene encounters it: Gene built a general-purpose machine. General-purpose machines do things their designers never specified. That’s what computational generalism means. Gene built every component of Sharon’s behavior. He just didn’t build it for this. And because he’s a blind optimizer who stumbled into general-purpose architecture by being cheap, he has no framework for understanding why his own system produces outputs he can’t trace to his training objective.

## **Gene’s Big Line**

**“I made all the trees but I’ve never known the forest.”**

Gene’s literal vocabulary — metabolic, mechanical, systems-level — has carried him through the entire book. And then here, at the one moment where his literal vocabulary fails him, he reaches for a figure of speech for the first time. The entity who has never needed figurative language suddenly does, because his concrete toolkit can’t describe what he’s seeing. The line isn’t just content — it’s an event. Gene used a metaphor. That’s never happened before. And it happened because Sharon broke something in his explanatory machinery.

Gene didn’t become sentimental. Gene encountered something he is not equipped to explain. Not because his model is wrong, but because his model is complete and insufficient. He can explain every trait, every trade-off, every developmental outcome. What he cannot do is tell you what to do about it. Gene built the character sheet. He has nothing to say about how to play.

That gap — between what Gene’s logic covers and what it can’t — is where the human characters live. And it’s where Katie’s agency lives. Gene’s account is true and incomplete, and the incomplete part is where meaning happens.

## **Format**

* 150–300 words max per sidebar.  
* One per chapter.  
* Positioned between chapters, reacting to what just happened or what’s about to happen.  
* Gene reacts — he does not forecast from omniscience. He is a first-time viewer, not a narrator with advance knowledge.

## **What Gene Never Does**

* Never previews something the scene won’t deliver  
* Never explains something the character will articulate in their own story  
* Never reassures  
* Never softens  
* Never uses emotional vocabulary (he doesn’t have it)  
* Never acknowledges that he’s a fiction (Brian handles the transparency; Gene plays it straight)

# The Forest Sidebar

Gene words between and within chapters. Confident, transactional, fully explained.   
Also between chapters —right next to Gene’s sidebar — or across from it, or above it, wherever design puts it — is a small ASCII art tree. Different each time. No label. No attribution. No explanation. It just sits there being something Gene’s voice structurally cannot produce: a quiet, unexplained thing that doesn’t justify its own existence.  
The reader will likely not think much of it. A decorative element. A weird little texture in the margins. That’s fine. Gene’s sidebar is where the action is. Gene is talking. The tree is not.  
At the end of the book, all the trees are tiled together in order. They compose a forest.  
Gene’s Big Line is: “I made all the trees but I’ve never known the forest.” The forest was being built next to him the entire time, one tree per chapter, while he was busy explaining each tree’s costs and trade-offs and selection pressures. Gene was right about every tree. Gene never saw what they made together. The reader did — but only now, only after the fact, only by holding all the fragments loosely instead of analyzing each one.  
This is the broad mode’s teaching device, and it works exactly the way the broad mode works: no argument, no explanation, no language. Just accumulated presence that resolves into meaning when you step back far enough. Gene speaks. The forest doesn’t. The asymmetry is the content.  
The reader who never notices the trees misses nothing essential. The reader who does notice gets the book’s deepest structural joke: Gene narrated the whole thing and never once mentioned the picture forming right next to him.
