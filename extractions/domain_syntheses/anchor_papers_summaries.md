# Structured Summaries — Four Anchor Papers

Per-paper extractions, ordered as listed in the brief. No cross-paper synthesis; each summary reports only what the paper itself claims, rests on, and proposes.

## Eronen & Bringmann (2021) — Theory crisis in psychology

**Citation:** Eronen, M. I., & Bringmann, L. F. (2021). The theory crisis in psychology: How to move forward. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 16*(4), 779–788. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691620970586

**Core claim:** Psychology is in a "theory crisis" that is more fundamental than the replication crisis: theories come and go without cumulative progress (extending Meehl 1978). The root cause is not psychologists' lack of effort but the difficulty of the subject matter. Three structural obstacles — too few robust phenomena to constrain theories, weak construct validity and absent epistemic iteration, and the fat-handedness of psychological interventions — make good theory development extremely hard. The implied intervention is conceptual and methodological rather than statistical: more phenomenon-detection, serious construct-validation programs, and treating causal inference about psychological variables as an unsolved problem rather than a mechanical step after experimentation.

**Evidence or apparatus:** A philosophy-of-science argument illustrated by worked examples rather than a meta-analysis. Examples include: ego-depletion (Hagger et al. 2016 multilab failure; Wimmer et al. 2019 letter-cancellation null) as a case where a "well-supported" phenomenon dissolved on rigorous testing; Major Depressive Disorder as a construct whose validity has not essentially advanced since the 1970s; Skinner's (1996) finding of 30+ overlapping perceived-control constructs; Flake et al. (2017) showing most JPSP articles report no construct validity evidence. Contrasts these with natural-science exemplars (Darwin's gathered phenomena before the *Origin*; Ptolemy-to-Copernicus as constraint by phenomena; the concept "electron" refined by epistemic iteration) and with Wimsatt's "generative entrenchment" as a reason psychological constructs persist despite known problems.

**Key constructs introduced or refined:** *Theory crisis* (the distinct, deeper crisis behind the replication crisis); *robust phenomena* as constraints on theories (data vs. phenomena distinction borrowed from Bogen & Woodward 1988); *epistemic iteration* (Chang 2004) imported into psychology as the missing process for construct refinement; *fat-handed interventions* (Baumgartner & Gebharter 2016; Romero 2015) as the term for psychological manipulations that change many variables at once and so license no clean causal inference; the bidirectional theory-phenomena relation (phenomena constrain theories, not just the reverse).

**Scope and conditions:** The argument applies to "soft" areas of psychology (clinical, social, personality), not to fields with established quantitative laws. The authors are explicit that they are not advocating a return to behaviorism, that they do not claim formalization or mathematization is the answer, and that noncausal/functional theories may be appropriate where causal mechanisms cannot be isolated. They acknowledge that some commonly cited robust phenomena (mere exposure, confirmation bias) are real and useful even without an accepted mechanistic theory.

**Direct quotes (≤3, ≤40 words each):**
- "We are facing a 'theory crisis' that is more fundamental than the replication crisis."
- "In many areas of psychology, there is no broad range of robust phenomena that would impose strong constraints on theories. This means that the possible theories are *underdetermined* by evidence."
- "Interventions on psychological variables are likely to be fat-handed, and such interventions do not provide a reliable basis for causal inference."

**Page references:** Theory-crisis framing pp. 779–780; phenomena-as-constraints pp. 780–781; construct validity and epistemic iteration pp. 781–783; fat-handed interventions pp. 783–784; recommendations pp. 784–785.

## Bleidorn (2024) — Toward a theory of lifespan personality trait development

**Citation:** Bleidorn, W. (2024). Toward a theory of lifespan personality trait development. *Annual Review of Developmental Psychology, 6*, 455–478. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-devpsych-010923-101709

**Disambiguation note:** The repo also contains Bleidorn (2009), Bleidorn (2015), and Bleidorn et al. (2020), all of which are earlier and not 2024. The single 2024 paper is the one selected here. It is filed as `bleidorn_2024_lifespan.pdf` and explicitly functions as a field-level diagnosis of lifespan personality development against the Eronen & Bringmann (2021) criteria. No competing Bleidorn 2024 paper was found.

**Core claim:** After 20 years of work, lifespan personality development has accumulated rich descriptive findings (mean-level maturation, increasing rank-order stability, individual-level variation in trajectories) but lacks a strong theory in the sense Eronen & Bringmann (2021) lay out. The field has good measures (Big Five hierarchical scales) and some robust phenomena, but causal explanation lags far behind description. The implied intervention is to (a) audit construct validity of Big Five measures *for developmental* use, (b) tighten and quantify the developmental phenomena themselves, and (c) shift from main-effect environmental and genetic predictors to formal causal-change models, computational specifications, and quasi-experimental/intervention designs.

**Evidence or apparatus:** Narrative review structured around Eronen & Bringmann's three criteria (construct validity, robust phenomena, causal explanation) and summarized in a Table 1 audit of "state of evidence / obstacles / future directions" for each criterion. Phenomena reviewed: rank-order stability rises from childhood through young adulthood and plateaus around r = .20 over 50+ years; mean levels of emotional stability, agreeableness, and conscientiousness rise (the maturity principle), with the strongest gains in young adulthood; individuals differ in trajectories. Causal evidence reviewed: heritability estimates and gene-environment interplay; life-event studies; absence of well-powered GWAS specifically for personality change; predominance of main-effects, observational designs.

**Key constructs introduced or refined:** No new theoretical primitives; the paper *imports* Eronen & Bringmann's three-criteria framework into developmental personality science and uses it as an audit instrument. Refinements/sharpenings include the distinction between Big Five measures' general construct validity and their *developmental* construct validity (e.g., measurement invariance across age, cohort, and culture as a separate problem); the *maturity principle* and *cumulative-continuity principle* as the two best-established phenomena; a call for *causal change models* that specify when, why, and how trait change unfolds rather than estimating average effects.

**Scope and conditions:** Explicitly restricted to changes from adolescence to old age, broad personality trait dimensions (Big Five), and adult-applicable measures — child-development and non-trait variables (motives, narrative identity) are deferred to other reviews. The author flags WEIRD-sample dominance, predominantly self-report assessment, and unclear measurement invariance as boundary conditions on every claim about "lifespan" generality.

**Direct quotes (≤3, ≤40 words each):**
- "There are currently few theories of lifespan personality development that would meet the very high standards of a strong theory."
- "In contrast to broad evidence for the structural and predictive validity of Big Five domain traits, there is less clarity about the lower-order structure of personality traits."
- "Predominant focus on the main effects of individual environmental features does not capture the dynamic and interactive causal change processes."

**Page references:** Three-criteria framework p. 457; Table 1 audit pp. 458–459; phenomena pp. 462–467; causal-relationships gap pp. 468–471; theory-status conclusion p. 472.

## Bareinboim, Correa, Ibeling, & Icard (2020/2022) — On Pearl's Hierarchy and the Foundations of Causal Inference

**Citation:** Bareinboim, E., Correa, J. D., Ibeling, D., & Icard, T. (2022). On Pearl's hierarchy and the foundations of causal inference. In H. Geffner, R. Dechter, & J. Y. Halpern (Eds.), *Probabilistic and causal inference: The works of Judea Pearl* (pp. 507–556). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3501714.3501743 (Technical Report R-60, July 2020)

**Core claim:** Pearl's "ladder of causation" can be given a fully formal treatment as the *Pearl Causal Hierarchy* (PCH), with three layers — associational (L₁: *seeing*), interventional (L₂: *doing*), counterfactual (L₃: *imagining*) — that emerge naturally from any structural causal model (SCM). The chapter's central new result, the *Causal Hierarchy Theorem* (CHT), proves that the layers are almost-always strictly distinct in a measure-theoretic sense: lower-layer data virtually always underdetermines higher-layer queries. Causal inference is then the discipline of climbing the hierarchy from observational data using *partial* SCM specifications encoded as graphical models, with do-calculus as the inferential tool that is sufficient (and in many cases necessary) for cross-layer identification.

**Evidence or apparatus:** Formal definitions, theorems, and worked examples rather than empirical data. Apparatus: (a) SCM defined as M = ⟨U, V, F, P(U)⟩ — a tuple of exogenous variables, endogenous variables, structural functions, and a distribution over exogenous variables — that *induces* probability distributions at each layer (Defs. 2, 5, 7). (b) The L₂ valuation via the *do-operator*: replacing the natural mechanism for X with a constant x produces submodel M_x and the interventional distribution P(Y | do(x)). (c) The L₃ counterfactual valuation by composing multiple submodels under a shared P(U) (multiple "modified worlds"). (d) The *Causal Hierarchy Theorem* (Theorem 1): "with respect to the Lebesgue measure over (a suitable encoding of L₃-equivalence classes of) SCMs, the subset in which any PCH collapse occurs is measure zero." Examples 7, 8, 9 construct pairs of SCMs that agree on lower layers but diverge on higher ones, demonstrating the theorem's substantive content. (e) The graphical perspective: Bayesian Networks (L₁), Causal Bayesian Networks (L₂), and Functional Networks (L₃) as the encoding levels for *structural constraints* that allow climbing the hierarchy under partial SCM knowledge. **Note: this section runs slightly long because the formal apparatus requires it.**

**Key constructs introduced or refined:** *Pearl Causal Hierarchy (PCH)* as the formal name for the three-layer structure (the chapter coins this term in honor of Pearl). The three layers: L₁ *associational* (P(Y | X)), L₂ *interventional* (P(Y | do(x))), L₃ *counterfactual* (P(Y_x | x', y')). *Structural Causal Model (SCM)* as the unobserved data-generating object that induces all three. *Causal Hierarchy Theorem (CHT)* — the formal statement that PCH-collapse is measure zero; this is the chapter's headline new result relative to Pearl's prior informal "ladder" (Pearl & Mackenzie 2018). *Submodel / interventional SCM* M_x and the *do-operator* (rendered formally with effectiveness, Def. 6). *Potential response* Y_x(u) (Def. 4) connecting SCMs to the Neyman-Rubin potential-outcomes notation. *Structural constraints* as the marks an SCM imprints on its induced distributions, encoded in graphical models. The distinction between full SCM specification (where all PCH layers are computable) and the realistic case of partial specification, which motivates *causal inference* as a discipline.

**What is new relative to Pearl's prior work:** The authors flag this explicitly. Pearl's earlier writings (1995, 2000, 2009; Pearl & Mackenzie 2018) introduced the SCM and discussed the three-layer ladder informally. Their new contribution in this chapter is (a) the unified logical-probabilistic *and* graphical treatment of the PCH within a single formal apparatus; (b) the CHT, proving that layer separation is generic rather than a feature of cherry-picked examples; (c) a systematic account of how do-calculus and graphical SCMs let one perform cross-layer inference under partial knowledge, rather than presupposing full SCM specification.

**Scope and conditions:** The framework presupposes that a domain of interest *can* be represented by an SCM, even if the SCM itself is unknown. The CHT presupposes a temporal ordering of structural mechanisms (needed for unique valuations via Defs. 2, 5, 7) and is otherwise theory-neutral. The authors discuss skepticism about the meaningfulness of L₂ (Maudlin 2019) or L₃ (Dawid 2000) but do not adopt those positions. The do-operator is presented for atomic interventions; conditional, soft, and stochastic interventions exist within the SCM framework but are not the focus of this chapter.

**Direct quotes (≤3, ≤40 words each):**
- "Roughly speaking, the CHT says that data at one layer virtually always underdetermines information at higher layers."
- "Knowledge at each layer allows reasoning about different classes of *causal concepts*, or 'queries.'"
- "Since in most practical settings the scientist does not have access to the precise form of the underlying causal mechanisms... this motivates us to study inferences within the PCH through the graphical lens."

**Page references:** PCH and three-layer overview pp. 1–5; SCM definition and L₁/L₂/L₃ valuations pp. 7–15; CHT statement and proof sketch p. 23, with collapse-failing examples pp. 24–26; graphical perspective and structural constraints pp. 27–28.

## Ringwald (2025) — A call to action for explaining rather than merely predicting psychopathology

**Citation:** Ringwald, W. R. (2025). A call to action for explaining rather than merely predicting psychopathology. Unpublished manuscript, Department of Psychology, University of Minnesota.

**Disambiguation note:** The repo contains three Ringwald 2025 papers: (a) *A call to action for explaining rather than merely predicting psychopathology* (sole-author, unpublished manuscript) — the mechanistic-traits paper selected here; (b) Ringwald, Abramovitch, Agelink van Rentergem, & Kotov (2025), "Do cognitive functions belong in the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) model? A meta-analysis," *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 20*(6), 1049–1064; and (c) Ringwald, Napolitano, Sewell, Soto, Yoon, & Wright (2025), "More skill than trait, or more trait than skill?" *European Journal of Personality.* The other two are not the mechanistic-traits paper: (b) is a meta-analysis on cognitive functions in HiTOP, and (c) is an empirical study on the trait–skill distinction. Paper (a) is the one developing the mechanistic-traits framework and arguing for intensive longitudinal designs over actuarial risk prediction.

**Core claim:** Prevailing dimensional models of psychopathology (HiTOP-style five-domain structure: internalizing, detachment, psychoticism, antagonism, disinhibition) describe *risk* for dysfunction without explaining what makes behavior dysfunctional in the first place. They are actuarial, not mechanistic. Ringwald proposes a shift to *mechanistic traits* — stable, recurring person-environment dynamics underlying people's ability to satisfy needs and make progress on goals. Assessing mechanistic traits requires intensive longitudinal designs (experience sampling, ambulatory assessment, dyadic methods, smartphone sensors) that establish the *temporal sequencing* of behaviors and contexts in real-life settings, not retrospective cross-sectional self-report. The implied intervention is methodological (replace cross-sectional batteries with intensive longitudinal protocols), conceptual (treat dysfunction as a property of person-environment dynamics, not the person alone), and applied (mechanism-matched personalized interventions, more context-sensitive macroscale policy).

**Evidence or apparatus:** A programmatic argument rather than a primary empirical study. Built from: (1) Methodological precedent — Ringwald, Feltman, Cloutson et al. (2025) and other intensive longitudinal work disentangling stress reactivity from stress evocation; Andresen et al. (2024) on the inability of cross-sectional measures to capture dynamics; (2) Theoretical commitment — DeYoung & Krueger (2018) and Hopwood et al. (2022) on psychopathology as failed person-environment transactions; Endler & Magnusson (1976) and Fraley & Roberts (2005) on four kinds of person-environment dynamics; cybernetic and characteristic-adaptations frameworks; (3) Empirical motivation — examples where typical "high-risk" trait levels do not produce dysfunction (e.g., antagonism in political activists, alcohol use in LGBTQ "safe space" venues per Rodriguez-Seijas et al. 2024); examples of dysregulation cycles (stress evocation × stress reactivity producing vicious cycles); biological-level signal (cortisol activity tied to antagonistic stress responses specifically rather than depression generally).

**Key constructs introduced or refined:** *Mechanistic traits* — "traits describing person-environment dynamics that explain psychological (dys)function," distinguished from cross-sectional trait scores by their being relatively stable *sequencings* of variables over time. The four kinds of person-environment dynamics that mechanistic traits index: *Selection* (seeking certain situations), *Reaction* (responding to situations in characteristic ways), *Perception* (reading situations in characteristic ways), and *Evocation* (eliciting characteristic reactions from others). *Mechanism covariance* — covariation across people in the operation of mechanisms (e.g., stress evocation × stress reactivity), used to articulate dysregulation in a psychological system. *Higher-order traits derived from covarying dynamic mechanisms* as a candidate empirical taxonomy alternative to factor-analytic taxonomies of cross-sectional scores. *Actuarial* vs. *mechanistic* models as the contrast term for what current dimensional psychopathology models do vs. what mechanistic traits aim at.

**Scope and conditions:** Applies primarily to dimensional psychopathology and clinical assessment, with extension to broader human-research domains (the "individual differences such as race and sex" example) flagged as the same inferential problem. Limits acknowledged by the author: most extant mechanistic-trait work is self-report and so confounded with perception; the field has been limited to simple bivariate, linear dynamics and lacks empirical work on multivariate/nonlinear dynamic patterns; passive sensing and dyadic ambulatory designs are aspirational rather than established. The argument does not claim to replace cross-sectional measurement entirely but to add the dynamic layer needed for explanation.

**Direct quotes (≤3, ≤40 words each):**
- "Prevailing empirical models of psychopathology describe risk for dysfunction without explaining what makes behavior dysfunctional. This actuarial approach has significantly constrained our ability to understand psychopathology and reduce human suffering."
- "The persistent failure to satisfy needs or achieve goals definitive of psychopathology is explained by a relatively stable sequencing of variables over time–what I'll call mechanistic traits."
- "Cross-sectional methods simply cannot measure mechanisms because they don't capture information on dynamics."

**Page references:** Abstract p. 2; actuarial-vs-mechanistic framing pp. 3–4; four person-environment dynamics p. 5; intensive longitudinal requirement p. 5–6; mechanistic-traits definition p. 6; mechanism covariance and higher-order taxonomy pp. 6–7; scientific/ethical/practical advantages pp. 7–9.
