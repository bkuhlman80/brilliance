# **Behavioral ecology field reconnaissance: 2019–2026**

Behavioral ecology's empirical foundations are shifting faster than its textbooks. **Three foundational frameworks face acute empirical challenge** — Bateman's principles, the good genes hypothesis, and life history trade-off theory — while new capacity in genomics, computer vision, and microbiome research is opening research programs that were impossible a decade ago. The field's most productive frontiers sit at disciplinary boundaries: social transmission of gut microbes as a new benefit of sociality, social network restructuring as adaptive disease defense, and cultural evolution documented outside humans. The most important honest finding is a conspicuous empty cell: nobody has mapped the genomic architecture of behavioral syndromes in a wild population, despite two decades of calls to do so.

---

## **1\. Frameworks under pressure**

The strongest empirical challenges cluster around sexual selection and life history theory — the frameworks most dependent on fitness measurement in wild populations. Optimal foraging theory and kin selection face revision, not rejection. Classical ESS models are being supplemented rather than replaced.

### **Sexual selection: Bateman's principles dismantled, then partially rebuilt**

Bateman's (1948) experiment, the empirical cornerstone of sexual selection theory, has been forensically dismantled. Hoquet, Gowaty, and Anderson reanalyzed Bateman's original handwritten notebooks and found his data were inconsistent with his stated principles. The experimental design suffered from \~25% offspring mortality due to double-mutant lethality, biasing all parentage assignments. **Bateman's principles are now treated as hypotheses requiring system-specific testing, not universal laws.**

* **Hoquet T, Gowaty PA, Anderson WW (2020).** Bateman's data: inconsistent with "Bateman's principles." *Ecology and Evolution* 10(20): 10928–10945. DOI: 10.1002/ece3.6420

  * Design: Reanalysis of archival laboratory notebook data  
  * Taxa: *Drosophila melanogaster*; Bateman's original 64 experimental populations  
  * Establishes that the most-cited dataset in sexual selection does not support the conclusions drawn from it.  
  * Matters because the paradigmatic foundation of sex-difference theory rested on uncritical acceptance of flawed data for 60+ years.  
* **Davies N, Janicke T, Morrow EH (2023).** Evidence for stronger sexual selection in males than in females using an adapted method of Bateman's classic study. *Evolution* 77(11): 2420–2430. DOI: 10.1093/evolut/qpad151

  * Design: Lab experiment (corrected Bateman design with molecular parentage markers)  
  * Taxa: *Drosophila melanogaster*, multiple replicate populations  
  * Shows that when Bateman's experiment is conducted with corrected methods, the principles hold in this species — Bateman was right about the outcome but wrong about the evidence.  
  * Matters because future sexual selection work must use molecular parentage, making the original phenotypic-marker approach obsolete.

### **Good genes hypothesis: conceptual and empirical trouble**

The good genes model remains the default explanation for elaborate ornaments despite mounting evidence that the most attractive mates seldom produce the most viable offspring. Flintham, Savolainen, and Mullon demonstrated mathematically that when male sexual traits harm females (a common scenario), good genes selection paradoxically intensifies harm and can drive population crashes — reversing the predicted demographic benefits.

* **Achorn AM, Rosenthal GG (2020).** It's not about him: mismeasuring "good genes" in sexual selection. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 35(3): 206–219. DOI: 10.1016/j.tree.2019.11.007

  * Design: Systematic literature evaluation  
  * Establishes that the vast majority of studies claiming good genes effects fail to test the model's key predictions.  
  * Matters because decades of "good genes" research may rest on circular reasoning rather than evidence.  
* **Flintham EO, Savolainen V, Mullon C (2023).** Male harm offsets the demographic benefits of good genes. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 120(10): e2211668120. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2211668120

  * Design: Computational modeling (demographically explicit evolutionary models)  
  * Theoretically demolishes the assumption that good genes always benefit populations.  
  * Matters because it explains why sexually selected species show elevated extinction rates.  
* **Martinossi-Allibert I, Thilliez E, Arnqvist G, Berger D (2019).** Sexual selection, environmental robustness, and evolutionary demography of maladapted populations. *Evolutionary Applications* 12(7): 1371–1384. DOI: 10.1111/eva.12758

  * Design: Lab experiment (experimental evolution across 3 mating regimes)  
  * Taxa: Seed beetle (*Callosobruchus maculatus*), N\~150 effective population per regime  
  * Finds no support for the prediction that sexual selection accelerates adaptation under stress.  
  * Matters as one of the most rigorous experimental tests of whether sexual selection aids adaptation — and it fails.

### **Life history trade-offs: genetic correlations run the wrong direction**

This may be the most consequential empirical challenge. **A meta-analysis of genetic correlations between survival and reproduction found them to be positive, not negative** — directly contradicting life history theory's central assumption.

* **Chang C-C, Moiron M, Sánchez-Tójar A, Niemelä PT, Laskowski KL (2024).** What is the meta-analytic evidence for life-history trade-offs at the genetic level? *Ecology Letters* 27: e14354. DOI: 10.1111/ele.14354

  * Design: Meta-analysis (61 studies, multiple animal taxa)  
  * Taxa: Vertebrates and invertebrates, lab and field populations  
  * Shows genetic correlations between survival and reproduction are positive, challenging the mechanistic foundation of life history optimization.  
  * Matters because if genetic trade-offs are undetectable at scale, the field needs fundamentally different models.  
* **Bliard L, Santos-Mayoral D, Bouwhuis S, et al. (2025).** No evidence for a trade-off between reproduction and survival in a meta-analysis across birds. *eLife* 13: e87018. DOI: 10.7554/eLife.87018

  * Design: Meta-analysis of brood-size manipulation experiments across 30+ bird species  
  * Taxa: Multiple passerines and seabirds, hundreds of manipulated broods  
  * Finds the fitness landscape is essentially flat across natural clutch size variation.  
  * Matters because it overturns the textbook answer for why birds don't simply lay more eggs.

### **ESS models and kin selection: revision, not rejection**

Classical ESS models are being expanded rather than abandoned. Király et al. (2024) showed that when fighting ability co-evolves with behavioral strategy, classical mixed ESS predictions break down. Sih, Trimmer, and Ehlman (2019) demonstrated that three mechanistically identical pre-HIREC ESS implementations produce radically different outcomes under environmental change, revealing that classical models' deliberate omission of mechanistic detail is a fatal flaw for prediction.

* **Király B, Varga T, Szabó G, Garay J (2024).** Evolutionarily stable payoff matrix in hawk–dove games. *BMC Ecology and Evolution* 24: 65\. DOI: 10.1186/s12862-024-02257-8

  * Design: Computational modeling  
  * Shows that classical ESS analyses with fixed payoffs systematically mispredict when traits and contests co-evolve.  
* **Sih A, Trimmer PC, Ehlman SM (2019).** Rapid environmental change in games. *Scientific Reports* 9: 43770\. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-019-43770-x

  * Design: Computational modeling  
  * Reveals that mechanistic details, deliberately omitted by classical ESS models, become critical under environmental change.

Kin selection has stabilized after the Nowak-Tarnita-Wilson controversy. The 137-signatory rebuttal (Abbot et al. 2011\) reflected broad consensus. The debate now concerns scope — how much does relatedness explain? — rather than validity. Multi-level selection persists as a complementary accounting method, not a replacement framework.

| Framework | Challenge type | Severity | Replacement? |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Optimal foraging | Conceptual revision | Low | Being enriched, not replaced |
| Kin selection | Philosophical (resolved) | Low–moderate | Consensus restored |
| Classical ESS | Methodological revision | Moderate | Network/dynamic models supplement |
| Life history trade-offs | **Empirical failure** | **High** | Acquisition models emerging |
| Bateman's principles | **Methodological critique** | **High** | Reframed as testable hypotheses |
| Good genes | **Empirical \+ conceptual** | **High** | Sensory bias / Lande-Kirkpatrick null |

## **2\. Rising frameworks and phenomena**

### **Cultural evolution in non-human animals: the strongest new empirical frontier**

This area has moved decisively from "do animals have culture?" to "how does culture evolve?" The landmark finding: cumulative cultural evolution — previously considered uniquely human — has been documented in wild birds.

* **Williams H, Scharf A, Ryber AE, Norris DR, Mennill DJ, Newman AEM, Doucet SM (2022).** Cumulative cultural evolution and mechanisms for cultural selection in wild bird songs. *Nature Communications* 13: 4001\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-022-31621-9

  * Design: Field observation with mathematical modeling (30+ year dataset)  
  * Taxa: Savannah sparrows (*Passerculus sandwichensis*), multi-generational population at Kent Island, New Brunswick  
  * Provides first evidence of cumulative cultural evolution in a wild animal — click trains replaced high note clusters over three decades, with sexual selection driving directional cultural change.  
  * Challenges the long-held assumption that cumulative cultural evolution is uniquely human.  
* **Chimento M, Alarcón-Nieto G, Aplin LM (2024).** Immigrant birds learn from socially observed differences in payoffs when their environment changes. *PLOS Biology* 22(8): e3002699. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pbio.3002699

  * Design: Field experiment (outdoor aviaries with wild-caught birds)  
  * Taxa: Great tits (*Parus major*), 144 birds across 18 micro-populations  
  * Shows immigrant birds use observed payoff differences to adopt locally adaptive behaviors.  
  * First experimental evidence that social learning strategies respond to ecological context in wild birds.

### **Cognitive ecology: producing genuinely distinct predictions**

Bergman and Beehner's "Information Ecology" framework (2023) represents the strongest new organizing concept — tracking information flow from environment through organism to decision, integrating behavioral ecology's adaptive focus with neuroscience's mechanistic focus.

* **Bergman TJ, Beehner JC (2023).** Information ecology: an integrative framework for studying animal behavior. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 38(11): 1041–1050. DOI: 10.1016/j.tree.2023.05.017  
  * Design: Conceptual framework  
  * Taxa: Illustrated with primates, birds, and others  
  * Proposes a named subfield that generates testable predictions about when animals should and should not use available information.  
  * Resolves the traditional lab-vs-field divide.

### **Social network analysis: now producing genuine biological insights**

The field has moved beyond descriptive network metrics to causal integration. The most striking finding links social network position to brain structure in free-ranging primates.

* **Testard C, Brent LJN, Andersson J, et al. (2022).** Social connections predict brain structure in a multidimensional free-ranging primate society. *Science Advances* 8(15): eabl5794. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.abl5794

  * Design: Field observation with neuroimaging  
  * Taxa: Rhesus macaques (*Macaca mulatta*), 68 adults \+ 35 juveniles on Cayo Santiago  
  * Demonstrates that grooming network degree predicts mid-STS and ventral-dysgranular insula volume in free-ranging primates.  
  * Bridges social network analysis with neuroscience, showing that network metrics capture biologically real variation.  
* **Cantor M, Chimento M, Smeele SQ, et al. (2021).** Social network architecture and the tempo of cumulative cultural evolution. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 288: 20203107\. DOI: 10.1098/rspb.2020.3107

  * Design: Computational modeling on empirical network structures  
  * Shows network structure determines the rate and potential for cumulative cultural evolution, with modular networks supporting higher complexity.  
  * Provides the missing theoretical link between social structure and cultural evolution.

### **Collective behavior: new mechanistic insights via AI and robotics**

Collective behavior research has advanced through AI-driven extraction of interaction rules from movement data and multi-robot experimental systems.

### **Niche construction: still theory-heavy, empirically sparse**

Niche construction theory continues to generate conceptual debate but **genuinely empirical behavioral ecology studies remain rare**. Kaiser and Trappes (2024, *BioScience*) distinguish social niche construction, choice, and conformance — but experimental manipulations testing NCT-specific predictions that standard behavioral ecology couldn't already generate remain a major gap.

| Rising area | New predictions? | New empirical results? | Trajectory |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Cultural evolution | Yes, strongly | Landmark | Rising fast |
| Cognitive ecology | Yes | Yes | Rising fast |
| Social network analysis | Yes | Yes, substantially | Rising |
| Collective behavior | Yes, mechanistic | Yes | Rising |
| Movement ecology | Yes, own framework | Massively | Established |
| Animal personality | Mixed, refining | Incremental | Plateauing |
| Niche construction | Limited beyond theory | Sparse | Stalled empirically |

## **3\. The genomics revolution in behavioral ecology**

Genomics has delivered surprises — particularly the discovery that major behavioral differences can be governed by chromosomal inversions acting as "supergenes" — but the dominant picture for most behavioral traits is massive polygenicity with small effect sizes. The field's greatest disappointment is the failure to find replicable "genes for" behavioral types in wild populations.

### **Supergenes for migration: the strongest genomic finding**

* **Sokolovskis K, et al. (2023).** Migration direction in a songbird explained by two loci. *Nature Communications* 14: 165\.

  * Design: Field observation (GPS tracking \+ genotyping at a migratory divide)  
  * Taxa: Willow warblers (*Phylloscopus trochilus*), Sweden  
  * Two loci (chromosomal inversions) explain **74%** of variation in autumn migration direction, with epistatic interaction via a transposable element.  
  * Shows that complex behaviors can sometimes have strikingly simple genetic architecture when chromosomal inversions lock co-adapted alleles together.  
* **Lundberg M, et al. (2023).** Inversions maintain differences between migratory phenotypes of a songbird. *Nature Communications* 14: 452\.

  * Design: Genomic analysis  
  * Taxa: Willow warblers  
  * Large chromosomal inversions maintain migratory phenotype divergence despite gene flow.  
  * Establishes inversions as "supergenes" for migration — a new genetic mechanism for behavioral ecology.

### **GWAS in wild populations: underpowered but beginning to deliver**

* **Saatoglu D, et al. (2024).** The genetic basis of dispersal in a vertebrate metapopulation. *Molecular Ecology* 33(6): e17295.  
  * Design: Animal model \+ GWAS  
  * Taxa: House sparrows (*Passer domesticus*), 8 islands in northern Norway, \>2,500 individuals with 200K SNPs  
  * Finds low but significant heritability of dispersal; GWAS identifies SNPs near circadian rhythm and exercise performance genes.  
  * Demonstrates GWAS feasibility in a wild vertebrate metapopulation.

### **Transcriptomics: behavioral states have molecular signatures**

* **Armenta TC, et al. (2019).** Gene expression shifts in yellow-bellied marmots prior to natal dispersal. *Behavioral Ecology* 30(2): 267–277.

  * Design: Field observation with RNA-seq  
  * Taxa: Yellow-bellied marmots (*Marmota flaviventer*), Colorado, wild population  
  * 150 differentially expressed genes in dispersers vs. residents, enriched for lipid metabolism and immune defense; 22 genes shift expression as a function of days until dispersal.  
  * Demonstrates staggered transcriptional preparation for a major life-history transition in wild mammals.  
* **San-Jose LM, et al. (2023).** Investigating the genetic basis of vertebrate dispersal combining RNA-seq, RAD-seq and quantitative genetics. *Molecular Ecology* 32: e16916.

  * Design: Integrated quantitative genetics, RAD-seq, and RNA-seq  
  * Taxa: Common lizard (*Zootoca vivipara*), semi-natural populations  
  * Links dispersal to serotonin transporter (SLC6A4), nitric oxide synthase (NOS1), and circadian clock genes.  
  * The most multi-omic study of a behavioral trait in a non-model vertebrate.

### **Epigenetics: correlative but accumulating**

* **Viitaniemi HM, et al. (2019).** Seasonal variation in genome-wide DNA methylation patterns and the onset of seasonal timing of reproduction in great tits. *Genome Biology and Evolution* 11: 970–983.  
  * Design: Field observation with RRBS methylation profiling  
  * Taxa: Wild great tits (*Parus major*)  
  * Seasonal methylation changes associated with onset of reproductive timing.  
  * Suggests epigenetic mechanisms contribute to seasonal behavioral transitions.

### **Heritability estimates: GRMs replacing pedigrees**

* **Godoy RI, et al. (2022).** Genetic, maternal, and environmental influences on sociality in a pedigreed primate population. *Heredity* 129: 203–214.  
  * Design: Animal model with multigenerational pedigree  
  * Taxa: White-faced capuchins (*Cebus capucinus imitator*), 376 individuals over 20 years  
  * Low to moderate heritability of social behavior; environmental factors play major roles.  
  * Confirms that behavioral traits in long-lived primates have a genetic component but are substantially environmentally influenced.

### **Classic findings sharpened by genetic data**

Paternity analysis has substantially revised our understanding of mating systems. SNP-based parentage assignment (replacing microsatellites since \~2019) has revealed previously undetected extra-pair paternity in species assumed genetically monogamous.

* **Søraker JS, et al. (2023).** The evolution of extra-pair paternity and paternal care in birds. *Behavioral Ecology* 34(5): 780–789.  
  * Design: Phylogenetic comparative analysis, 271 bird species with molecular paternity data  
  * Finds no significant relationship between EPP rates and male parental care after controlling for phylogeny.  
  * Challenges the classic prediction that EPP should reduce paternal investment.

### **Key methodological trends and an honest gap**

Genomic relatedness matrices from SNP data are increasingly replacing pedigrees for wild heritability estimation. Chromosomal inversions have emerged as major mechanisms for complex behavioral traits. But **sample size limitations remain severe**: wild GWAS typically includes hundreds to low thousands of individuals versus millions in human studies. Most behavioral GWAS in wild populations find few genome-wide significant loci.

**Critical gap**: QTL mapping of behavioral traits in truly wild (not lab-reared F2 cross) populations remains essentially absent. The field has quantitative genetic parameter estimates from a few systems and genomic tools from lab/domesticated systems, but almost nobody has combined them in a single wild system with sufficient power.

## **4\. Methods and instruments — current workhorses**

### **GPS-accelerometer biologging: fully standard**

On-board processing of raw accelerometer data into behavioral categories is now routine for medium-to-large vertebrates. Machine learning classifiers (Random Forest, XGBoost) achieve **\>85–95% accuracy** for 3–5 behavioral states. The main remaining challenges are model transferability across populations and standardization.

### **Camera traps with AI: widespread and maturing**

**MegaDetector** (Microsoft, YOLOv5-based) achieves \~95% accuracy for animal/human/vehicle/empty classification and is the most widely deployed tool. Regional models (DeepFaune for 28 European species; TRAPPER-AI for 18 European mammals) handle species-level identification. Behavioral classification from camera traps — beyond simple detection — is the emerging frontier.

* **Vélez J, Castiblanco-Camacho PJ, Tabak MA, et al. (2023).** An evaluation of platforms for processing camera-trap data using artificial intelligence. *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 14(2): 459–477. DOI: 10.1111/2041-210X.14044  
  * Design: Comparative  
  * First systematic comparison of all major AI platforms for camera-trap processing; documents that AI can process 1 million images in 24 hours.

### **Bioacoustics: BirdNET as transformative infrastructure**

* **Kahl S, Wood CM, Eibl M, Klinck H (2021).** BirdNET: a deep learning solution for avian diversity monitoring. *Ecological Informatics* 61: 101236\. DOI: 10.1016/j.ecoinf.2021.101236

  * Design: Computational  
  * Taxa: 984 bird species; 3 independent test datasets  
  * Identifies hundreds of species by sound with mean average precision of 0.791.  
  * Now deployed by **2+ million citizen science users** with 40+ million observations, transforming avian ecology from expert-dependent to scalable.  
* **Kershenbaum A, Stowell D, et al. (2025).** Automatic detection for bioacoustic research: a practical guide. *Biological Reviews* 100(2): 500–530. DOI: 10.1111/brv.13155

  * First interdisciplinary practical guide bridging biologists and computer scientists for automated acoustic detection.

### **Statistical methods**

**Bayesian hierarchical models** are now mainstream via Stan and brms. **GLMMs** are the default analytical framework. **Social network analysis** is mature and widely applied. **Agent-based modeling** grows but remains underused relative to its potential, with validation against empirical data still a challenge. **Phylogenetic comparative methods** are stable with incremental Bayesian improvements.

### **Methods falling out of favor**

Manual VHF radio-telemetry has been largely replaced by GPS/accelerometer biologging. Purely observer-based behavioral ethograms are being supplemented by computer vision. Traditional manual acoustic analysis is giving way to automated detection. Frequentist NHST without effect sizes continues to decline.

## **5\. Methods and instruments — emerging**

### **Computer vision for behavior: the genuine paradigm shift**

The **DeepLabCut → SLEAP → Keypoint-MoSeq** pipeline represents the most transformative methodological advance since GPS tracking. These tools are open-source, species-agnostic, and increasingly field-applicable.

* **Lauer J, Zhou M, Ye S, et al. (2022).** Multi-animal pose estimation, identification and tracking with DeepLabCut. *Nature Methods* 19(4): 496–504. DOI: 10.1038/s41592-022-01443-0

  * Design: Computational / lab experiment  
  * Taxa: Mice, fish, primates, marmosets; 4 benchmark datasets  
  * Extends DeepLabCut to multi-animal pose estimation with identity tracking.  
  * With **3,000+ citations**, DLC is the most widely adopted animal pose estimation tool; this paper makes social behavior quantification accessible to any lab with a camera.  
* **Pereira TD, Tabris N, Matsliah A, et al. (2022).** SLEAP: a deep learning system for multi-animal pose tracking. *Nature Methods* 19(4): 486–495. DOI: 10.1038/s41592-022-01426-1

  * Design: Computational / lab experiment  
  * Taxa: Flies, mice, bees  
  * Achieves comparable accuracy to DLC at **2–11× faster inference**.  
  * Its lightweight models suit labs with limited GPU resources.  
* **Weinreb C, Pearl J, Lin S, et al. (2024).** Keypoint-MoSeq: parsing behavior by linking point tracking to pose dynamics. *Nature Methods* 21(7): 1329–1339. DOI: 10.1038/s41592-024-02318-2

  * Design: Computational / lab experiment  
  * Taxa: C57/BL6 mice, rats, fruit flies  
  * Introduces unsupervised behavioral "syllable" discovery from continuous keypoint data.  
  * Completes the pipeline: fully automated ethogram construction from standard video without human annotation.  
* **Wiltshire C, Firth E, Sheridan K, et al. (2023).** DeepWild: application of DeepLabCut for behaviour tracking in wild chimpanzees and bonobos. *Journal of Animal Ecology* 92(7): 1468–1480. DOI: 10.1111/1365-2656.13932

  * Design: Computational / methodological  
  * Taxa: Wild chimpanzees and bonobos, handheld field footage  
  * First successful multi-animal pose estimation in wild great apes from handheld footage.  
  * Opens systematic behavioral ecology of apes at previously impossible scale.

### **Miniaturized biologgers: closing the size gap**

* **Ripperger SP, Carter GG, Page RA, et al. (2020).** Thinking small: next-generation sensor networks close the size gap in vertebrate biologging. *PLoS Biology* 18(4): e3000655. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pbio.3000655  
  * Design: Technological development / field observation  
  * Taxa: Vampire bats (*Desmodus rotundus*), N=50; tags 1–2 g  
  * A two-week deployment produced **\~400,000 social interaction records** with 5 m accuracy in forest.  
  * Opens biologging to the vast majority of vertebrate species previously too small for GPS tracking.

The **Motus Wildlife Tracking System** has scaled to nearly 50,000 radio tags deployed on 400+ species across 1,200+ receiver stations in 31 countries, with tags as light as **0.15 g** — enabling tracking of insects, small songbirds, and bats.

### **CRISPR for behavioral ecology: not yet applied**

CRISPR in wildlife contexts concentrates on gene drives for disease vector suppression and conservation genetics. **No published studies use CRISPR to probe behavioral ecology questions in wild populations.** Application is likely 5–10+ years away. This is a genuinely empty frontier.

### **Foundation models for bioacoustics**

BirdNET embeddings are being repurposed for within-species call classification, detection of novel sounds, and cross-taxa applications. A bird-trained model performing competently on frog and gecko calls represents the "transfer learning" paradigm shift arriving in ecology.

## **6\. Animal personality and individual differences**

### **Theoretical status: three frameworks, none generalizing**

The literature has produced rich documentation across \>100 species but no consensus on **why** individuals consistently differ. The three leading explanations — pace-of-life syndrome (POLS), state-dependent feedback, and fluctuating selection — each have mixed empirical support. Laskowski, Moiron, and Niemelä (2022) explicitly concluded that "the major frameworks have mixed support." Dochtermann (2023) called the failure of POLS to generalize "a major failure in our understanding of behavioral evolution."

* **Laskowski KL, Moiron M, Niemelä PT (2022).** Consistent individual behavioral variation: what do we know and where are we going? *Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics* 53: 161–186. DOI: 10.1146/annurev-ecolsys-102220-011451

  * Design: Quantitative synthesis  
  * Provides the most comprehensive assessment that major theoretical frameworks have mixed support.  
  * Essential entry point — identifies exactly where evidence is strong vs. weak.  
* **Prabh N, Linnenbrink M, Jovicic M, Guenther A (2023).** Fast adjustment of pace-of-life and risk-taking to changes in food quality by altered gene expression. *Ecology Letters* 26(1): 99–110. DOI: 10.1111/ele.14137

  * Design: Semi-natural experiment with gene expression analysis  
  * Taxa: House mice (*Mus musculus domesticus*), N=1,442 across 3 generations  
  * Experimentally demonstrates that food quality shifts cause rapid, heritable changes in both life history and risk-taking with concurrent gene expression changes.  
  * One of the few studies providing causal, multi-generational evidence that ecology actively shapes the POLS.

### **Fitness consequences: real but context-dependent**

* **Moiron M, Laskowski KL, Niemelä PT (2020).** Individual differences in behaviour explain variation in survival: a meta-analysis. *Ecology Letters* 23(2): 399–408. DOI: 10.1111/ele.13438

  * Design: Quantitative meta-analysis (104 studies)  
  * Bolder, more aggressive, more active individuals generally have lower survival, but effect sizes are small and highly heterogeneous.  
  * The definitive quantitative assessment: personality–survival links exist but context-dependence is the norm.  
* **Mortelliti A, et al. (2023).** Environmental heterogeneity modifies the link between personality and survival. *Journal of Animal Ecology* 93(2): 233–245. DOI: 10.1111/1365-2656.14042

  * Design: Longitudinal field observation (multi-year mark-recapture)  
  * Taxa: Three small mammal species, multiple populations  
  * The personality–survival relationship reverses direction depending on environmental conditions.  
  * Explains the long-standing mismatch between theoretical predictions and empirical findings.  
* **Dingemanse NJ, Araya-Ajoy YG, Westneat DF (2021).** Most published selection gradients are not suitable for inference about selection. *Evolution Letters* 5(1): 12–23. DOI: 10.1002/evl3.212

  * Design: Methodological/statistical  
  * Demonstrates that most published selection gradients on personality are confounded.  
  * A wake-up call: many claimed fitness consequences may be statistical artifacts.

### **Behavioral syndromes: refined, not abandoned**

* **Dhellemmes F, Finger J-S, Laskowski KL, Guttridge TL, Krause J (2020).** Comparing behavioural syndromes across time and ecological conditions in a free-ranging predator. *Animal Behaviour* 162: 23–33. DOI: 10.1016/j.anbehav.2020.01.009

  * Design: Longitudinal field observation  
  * Taxa: Lemon sharks (*Negaprion brevirostris*), N≈35 juveniles tracked over years  
  * Syndrome structure changes across years and ecological contexts.  
  * One of very few studies tracking behavioral syndromes longitudinally in large free-ranging vertebrates.  
* **Hertel AG, Royauté R, Zedrosser A, Mueller T (2021).** Biologging reveals individual variation in behavioural predictability in the wild. *Journal of Animal Ecology* 90(3): 723–737. DOI: 10.1111/1365-2656.13406

  * Design: Field observation with GPS biologging  
  * Taxa: Brown bears (*Ursus arctos*), N=60+ individuals, multi-year tracking  
  * Introduces "behavioral predictability" — how predictable an individual's behavior is — as a novel personality dimension.  
  * Expands personality beyond mean tendencies to include variance itself.

### **Genetics of personality: massively polygenic, not governed by "personality genes"**

Progress linking personality to specific genetic variants has been slow and disappointing. DRD4 polymorphisms (once the most promising candidate) produce inconsistent results across populations. The architecture mirrors human personality genetics — massively polygenic with no large-effect loci. **This is the field's clearest honest gap** (see Section 13).

## **7\. Cooperation, conflict, and social evolution**

### **Kin selection remains dominant but insufficient alone**

Kin selection continues as the primary organizing framework. But the Kalahari Meerkat Project's long-term data show that helper contributions are not always correlated with kinship, and direct fitness benefits (group augmentation, pay-to-stay) increasingly appear as co-occurring or primary drivers.

* **Groenewoud F, Clutton-Brock TH (2021).** Meerkat helpers buffer the detrimental effects of adverse environmental conditions. *Journal of Animal Ecology* 90(11): 2639–2652. DOI: 10.1111/1365-2656.13396

  * Design: Longitudinal field study (21 years)  
  * Taxa: Meerkats (*Suricata suricatta*), Kalahari  
  * Helpers buffer dominant females against low-rainfall effects on fecundity and pup survival.  
  * Most rigorous long-term evidence that cooperation provides direct fitness benefits via environmental buffering.  
* **Kerr NR, Morris WF, Walters JR (2024).** Inclusive fitness may explain some but not all benefits derived from helping behavior. *The American Naturalist* 203(3): 393–410. DOI: 10.1086/728670

  * Design: Longitudinal field study with fitness decomposition  
  * Taxa: Red-cockaded woodpecker (*Dryobates borealis*)  
  * Inclusive fitness explains only part of the benefits of helping; direct benefits are also substantial.  
  * One of the cleanest demonstrations that kin selection alone is insufficient.

### **Partner choice emerging as the dominant mechanism**

* **Fuentes MA (2025).** Ecology and the evolution of cooperation by partner choice and reciprocity. *Scientific Reports* 15: 4613\. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-025-87984-8

  * Design: Computational simulation  
  * Partner choice evolves under a wider range of conditions than reciprocity and compels near-optimal cooperation.  
  * Elevates partner choice as potentially more important than reciprocity in maintaining cooperation.  
* **McAvoy A, Allen B, Nowak MA (2022).** Evolution of cooperation with contextualized behavior. *Science Advances* 8(6): eabm6066. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.abm6066

  * Design: Computational modeling  
  * Allowing individuals to condition behavior on social context dramatically expands conditions for cooperation.  
  * Resolves a puzzle about why cooperation evolves so readily despite theoretical predictions of defection.

### **Punishment and policing: rarer than assumed**

Riehl and Frederickson (2016, updated 2021\) concluded that both cheating and punishment are "probably rarer than often supposed." Uncooperative individuals typically have lower, not higher, fitness — suggesting cooperation is often self-reinforcing without costly policing.

### **Cleaner fish as the model system for cooperation mechanisms**

* **Truskanov N, Emery Y, Bshary R (2020).** Juvenile cleaner fish can socially learn the consequences of cheating. *Nature Communications* 11: 1159\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-020-14712-3  
  * Design: Lab experiment  
  * Taxa: Cleaner wrasse (*Labroides dimidiatus*), N=20  
  * Juveniles socially learn to cooperate more after observing clients flee from cheating adults.  
  * Challenges the assumption that social learning about cooperation is uniquely human.

### **Dominance hierarchies: fundamentally dynamic**

* **Hobson EA, et al. (2024).** The dynamics of dominance in a "despotic" society. *Animal Behaviour* 218\.  
  * Design: Longitudinal field observation (4 years)  
  * Taxa: Rhesus macaques (*Macaca mulatta*), \~100+ individuals  
  * Even in "despotic" species, hierarchies show significant reshuffling, particularly at middle ranks.  
  * Challenges the long-held view of primate hierarchies as static.

### **Multi-level selection: sidelined, not resolved**

The mathematical equivalence between inclusive fitness and MLS was established decades ago via the Price equation. Most empirical behavioral ecologists use kin selection language because it generates clearer predictions. The debate has been effectively sidelined: researchers focus on testing specific mechanisms rather than debating frameworks.

## **8\. Movement ecology and spatial behavior**

### **Tracking technology is producing genuine insights, not just better natural history**

The critical advance is connecting movement to fitness. Aikens et al. (2020) provided the first quantitative demonstration that an individual movement strategy — "green-wave surfing" — directly predicts body fat and reproductive success. Abrahms et al. (2021) developed the first unified theoretical framework integrating optimal foraging theory with landscape ecology to predict when and where resource tracking should occur.

* **Aikens EO, Kauffman MJ, Merkle JA, et al. (2020).** The greenscape shapes surfing of resource waves in a large migratory herbivore. *Ecology Letters* 23(5): 805–814. DOI: 10.1111/ele.13497

  * Design: Field observation (GPS collaring \+ NDVI remote sensing)  
  * Taxa: Mule deer (*Odocoileus hemionus*), \~95 GPS-collared individuals, Wyoming  
  * Individual deer vary in green-wave surfing ability, and better surfing directly predicts higher body fat and reproductive success.  
  * Landmark paper linking movement strategy to fitness metric.  
* **Abrahms B, Aikens EO, Armstrong JB, et al. (2021).** Emerging perspectives on resource tracking and animal movement ecology. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 36(4): 308–320. DOI: 10.1016/j.tree.2020.10.018

  * Design: Theoretical framework with empirical cases  
  * First unified theory integrating foraging theory with landscape ecology for movement prediction.  
  * The strongest theoretical contribution to movement ecology in this window.

### **Cultural transmission of migration routes**

* **Byholm P, Beal M, Isaksson N, Lötberg U, Åkesson S (2022).** Paternal transmission of migration knowledge in a long-distance bird migrant. *Nature Communications* 13: 1566\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-022-29300-w  
  * Design: Field observation (GPS tracking of family groups)  
  * Taxa: Caspian tern (*Hydroprogne caspia*), \~50 tracked individuals  
  * Juvenile terns learn routes from male parents; four juveniles that lost contact died. Subadults remain faithful to learned routes.  
  * Fundamentally challenges the assumption that migration is primarily genetically programmed.

### **Drones and computer vision enabling field-scale collective behavior study**

* **Koger B, Deshpande A, Kerber T, et al. (2023).** Quantifying the movement, behaviour and environmental context of group-living animals using drones and computer vision. *Journal of Animal Ecology* 92(7): 1357–1371. DOI: 10.1111/1365-2656.13904

  * Design: Methodological / field observation  
  * Taxa: Multiple ungulate species, Kenya  
  * Complete pipeline for drone \+ computer vision quantification of individual movement and group behavior without tagging.  
  * Enables studying emergent group phenomena at ecologically meaningful scales.  
* **Papageorgiou D, Farine DR (2020).** Shared decision-making allows subordinates to lead when dominants monopolize resources. *Science Advances* 6(48): eaba5881. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aba5881

  * Design: Field observation (GPS tracking \+ social network analysis)  
  * Taxa: Vulturine guineafowl (*Acryllium vulturinum*), \~60 GPS-tagged, Kenya  
  * Subordinates can lead collective movements when dominant individuals monopolize food.  
  * One of the first field demonstrations of context-dependent collective leadership.

### **Nathan's paradigm: productive but aspirational on theory**

Nathan et al.'s (2022, *Science*) 14-year update demonstrates the paradigm organized the field and facilitated data sharing (Movebank, ICARUS). But the original aspiration of unified mechanistic movement theory remains beyond reach. **The field is data-rich relative to its theoretical framework** — the gap between big data and big theory is narrowing but not closed.

## **9\. Behavioral responses to rapid environmental change**

### **The COVID-19 anthropause as natural experiment**

The pandemic lockdowns provided unprecedented before/after comparisons for sensory pollution effects.

* **Derryberry EP, Phillips JN, Derryberry GE, Blum MJ, Luther D (2020).** Singing in a silent spring: birds respond to a half-century soundscape reversion during the COVID-19 shutdown. *Science* 370(6516): 575–579. DOI: 10.1126/science.abd5777  
  * Design: Natural experiment (before/after lockdown)  
  * Taxa: White-crowned sparrow (*Zonotrichia leucophrys*), San Francisco  
  * When traffic noise dropped to 1950s levels, sparrows sang **30% more softly**, at lower frequencies, with wider bandwidth. Communication distance roughly doubled. Recovery took weeks, not generations.  
  * The most compelling demonstration that noise pollution effects are immediate and reversible.

### **Continental-scale sensory pollution effects on fitness**

* **Senzaki M, Barber JR, Phillips JN, et al. (2020).** Sensory pollutants alter bird phenology and fitness across a continent. *Nature* 587: 605–609. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-020-2903-7  
  * Design: Continental-scale observational (citizen science \+ noise/light maps)  
  * Taxa: **142 North American bird species**  
  * Light pollution advanced egg-laying by up to 18 days; both pollutants reduced reproductive success in some species.  
  * Scales from "pollution affects behavior" to "pollution affects fitness at macroecological scale."

### **Phenological traps in tropical apex predators**

* **Abrahms B, Rafiq K, Jordan NR, McNutt JW (2022).** Long-term, climate-driven phenological shift in a tropical large carnivore. *PNAS* 119(27): e2121667119. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2121667119  
  * Design: Field observation (30-year longitudinal dataset)  
  * Taxa: African wild dog (*Lycaon pictus*), 60 packs, Botswana  
  * Documents a **7-day-per-decade delay in parturition timing** from warming. Denning periods now experience warmer temperatures, reducing success — creating a "phenological trap."  
  * One of the first demonstrations of climate-driven phenological shifts in a tropical apex predator.

### **Invasion as selective filter on behavior**

* **Michelangeli M, Melki-Wegner B, Laskowski K, Wong BBM, Chapple DG (2022).** Biological invasions as a selective filter driving behavioral divergence. *Nature Communications* 13: 5996\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-022-33755-2  
  * Design: Comparative field study (native vs. 3 independent invasive ranges)  
  * Taxa: Delicate skink (*Lampropholis delicata*), Australia \+ 3 Pacific island populations  
  * Invasion shifted behavioral trait means and reduced among-individual variation across three independent invasions.  
  * Strongest empirical test of whether invasion itself acts as a selective filter on behavior.

### **Light pollution fitness costs at unexpectedly dim levels**

* **Alaasam VJ, et al. (2021).** Effects of dim artificial light at night on locomotor activity, cardiovascular physiology, and circadian clock genes. *Environmental Pollution* 282: 117036\. DOI: 10.1016/j.envpol.2021.117036  
  * Design: Lab experiment (controlled light exposure)  
  * Taxa: Zebra finch (*Taeniopygia guttata*)  
  * Even **1.5 lux** — far below typical urban illumination — disrupted circadian clock gene expression and cardiovascular physiology.  
  * Shifts the threshold for "biologically significant" light pollution dramatically downward.

### **Plasticity vs. evolution: still the central unresolved distinction**

The strongest methodological advance is using **urban heat islands as natural experiments** for climate change (Diamond & Martin 2021, *Journal of Experimental Biology*). Common-garden designs with extended captivity (Mazza & Guenther 2021\) provide the gold standard for behavioral contexts. But conclusively separating plasticity from evolutionary adaptation remains elusive for most systems.

## **10\. Cross-disciplinary bridges**

### **Microbiome × behavior: the most transformative new bridge**

The recognition that gut microbiomes are socially transmitted — and can influence host behavior — creates a feedback loop that fundamentally alters how we think about costs of sociality.

* **Raulo A, Bürkner P-C, Finerty GE, et al. (2024).** Social and environmental transmission spread different sets of gut microbes in wild mice. *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 8(5): 972–985. DOI: 10.1038/s41559-024-02381-0

  * Design: Field observation with RFID tracking \+ repeated microbiome sampling  
  * Taxa: Wood mice (*Apodemus sylvaticus*), Wytham Woods, N=200+  
  * Social networks and shared environments spread **different** microbe sets: anaerobic (potentially mutualistic) bacteria are more socially transmitted.  
  * Social behavior preferentially spreads the gut microbes most likely to be beneficial — a new mechanism for benefits of sociality.  
* **Suzuki TA, et al. (2025).** Selection and transmission of the gut microbiome alone can shift mammalian behavior. *Nature Communications* 16: 9482\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-025-65368-w

  * Design: Lab experiment (serial fecal transplants with artificial selection)  
  * Taxa: Wild-derived house mice, germ-free recipients  
  * Artificial selection on microbiomes alone — no host genomic change — shifts locomotor behavior across generations.  
  * First direct demonstration that microbiome transmission can shape mammalian behavior independent of host genomic evolution.

### **Disease ecology × behavior: adaptive social network restructuring**

* **Stroeymeyt N, Grasse AV, Crespi A, et al. (2018).** Social network plasticity decreases disease transmission in a eusocial insect. *Science* 362(6417): 941–945. DOI: 10.1126/science.aat4793  
  * Design: Lab/field experiment (automated tracking of tagged ants \+ pathogen exposure)  
  * Taxa: Black garden ants (*Lasius niger*), 22 colonies of \~2,000 workers each  
  * Upon pathogen exposure, both infected ants and healthy nestmates reorganize their social networks to isolate the queen and brood.  
  * First experimental demonstration that social animals actively restructure networks in response to pathogens.

### **Eco-immunology: context-dependent sickness behavior**

* **Stockmaier S, Bolnick DI, Page RA, Carter GG (2020).** Sickness effects on social interactions depend on the type of behaviour and relationship. *Journal of Animal Ecology* 89(6): 1387–1394. DOI: 10.1111/1365-2656.13193  
  * Design: Lab experiment  
  * Taxa: Vampire bats (*Desmodus rotundus*), N≈30  
  * LPS-induced sickness reduced food sharing and grooming but not proximity; effects modulated by bond strength.  
  * Sickness behavior is not monolithic — different social behaviors are differentially affected.

### **ML/AI: methodological revolution, not yet theoretical**

ML has dramatically transformed data collection (DeepLabCut, SLEAP, BirdNET) but has **not yet changed what behavioral ecologists think** — only how fast they can collect data. The closest to theoretical impact is unsupervised behavioral decomposition (Keypoint-MoSeq), which can reveal behavioral structure that humans wouldn't hypothesize. Whether ML will generate genuinely new theory remains an open question.

### **Physiology: the glucocorticoid reckoning**

The glucocorticoid-fitness relationship is undergoing important self-correction. Baseline glucocorticoids can be positively or negatively related to fitness depending on context — the "chronic stress is harmful" narrative borrowed from biomedicine does not apply cleanly to wild animals.

* **Vitousek MN, et al. (2018–2020).** Environmental unpredictability shapes glucocorticoid regulation across populations. Multi-population tree swallow comparison.  
  * Populations experiencing greater environmental unpredictability show elevated stress-induced (but not baseline) glucocorticoid responses, consistent with adaptive upregulation.

### **Neuroscience: great frameworks, limited wild integration**

Neuroecology and behavioral ecology are aware of each other but mostly talk past one another. The technical bottleneck — recording neural activity in free-living wild animals — remains severe. Genuine integration is more aspiration than reality, though cichlid fish (Hofmann Lab) and the Testard et al. (2022) macaque social brain study are exceptions.

### **Bridge productivity ranking**

The most genuinely synergistic bridges — producing findings neither discipline alone could achieve — are **microbiome × behavior**, **disease ecology × social networks**, and **eco-immunology × behavioral decisions**. ML/AI and computational social science methods are productive imports but have not yet generated theoretical novelty. Neuroscience integration remains the most important unfulfilled aspiration.

## **11\. Labs and researchers driving the field**

### **Established leaders**

**Iain Couzin** (Max Planck / Konstanz) leads the field's largest concentration of behavioral ecology researchers at the Centre for the Advanced Study of Collective Behaviour. His lab pioneered ML, computer vision, and VR integration into behavioral ecology. Working on a "predictive science of behavior."

**Tim Clutton-Brock** (Cambridge) built multi-decade, individual-based field systems (meerkats, red deer) that remain the gold standard for measuring fitness and selection in wild populations.

**Andrew Sih** (UC Davis) pioneered behavioral syndromes, HIREC responses, and integration of sensory ecology with behavioral prediction.

**Niels Dingemanse** (LMU Munich) brought quantitative genetic methods to personality research and formalized the statistical distinction between among- and within-individual behavioral variation. Cited \>30,000 times.

**Stuart West and Ashleigh Griffin** (Oxford) anchor social evolution theory, extending behavioral ecology principles to microbes and co-authoring the 2025 *Nature Ecology & Evolution* perspective "Behavioural ecology in the twenty-first century."

### **Rising stars reshaping the field**

**Damien Farine** (Zurich / Max Planck) developed statistical methods for wild social network analysis and demonstrated multilevel societies in small-brained birds (vulturine guineafowl), challenging the assumption that complex societies require large brains.

**Kate Laskowski** (UC Davis) authored the definitive synthesis on animal personality's theoretical shortcomings and pioneered automated tracking with reaction norm frameworks.

**Aura Raulo** (Helsinki/Oxford) bridges microbiome science and social networks in wild mammals, producing some of the most transformative cross-disciplinary findings.

**Christina Riehl** (Princeton) runs a long-term field study of greater anis in Panama, uniquely bridging cooperation among non-relatives with kin selection theory. NSF CAREER awardee.

**Zegni Triki** (Bern) integrates neuroanatomy, endocrinology, and ecology to understand cognitive variation in fish. Niko Tinbergen Award 2024\.

**Michael Bertram** (Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences) combines contaminant implants with acoustic telemetry for true field ecotoxicology. Named 2024 Rising Star in Environmental Research.

**Gail Patricelli** (UC Davis) uses robotic models and sensor arrays for multimodal signaling research, pushing technology-enabled behavioral ecology.

**Noa Pinter-Wollman** (UCLA) bridges individual variation and collective outcomes using simulations, field studies, and social network analysis across ants and spiders.

**Ned Dochtermann** (North Dakota State) exposed the failure of POLS to generalize and showed that behavioral syndromes constrain evolutionary trajectories in field crickets via conserved G-matrix structure.

## **12\. What's stuck**

### **Why do individuals consistently differ in behavior?**

Multiple adaptive models exist (state-behavior feedbacks, POLS, social niche specialization), but **none generalizes**. Dochtermann (2023) explicitly called this "a major failure in our understanding of behavioral evolution." The blocking factors are conceptual (competing, non-complementary frameworks), data (genetic correlations underlying syndromes are almost never measured in wild populations), and methodological (most studies still don't implement the repeated-measures designs needed to partition among- vs. within-individual variance).

### **Can we predict behavioral evolution?**

Owens identified this as a key challenge in 2006\. Twenty years later, the situation has barely improved. Multi-generation data with genotype-phenotype links exist for only a handful of systems (great tits, meerkats, red deer, Soay sheep). Behavioral traits have low phylogenetic signal, making comparative approaches weaker than for morphology. Behavioral plasticity confounds genetic mapping.

### **How do behavioral and genetic mechanisms of adaptation interact?**

The "missing heritability" problem for behavioral traits is at least as severe as for human behavioral genetics. GWAS in wild populations requires enormous samples that few field systems can provide. Epistasis, GxE interactions, and pleiotropy make single-gene narratives misleading. Most behavioral ecologists lack genomic expertise; most genomicists lack behavioral field data.

### **Scaling individual behavior to population and community dynamics**

Theoretical models show conceptually how individual behavioral rules generate population patterns, but we rarely have simultaneous individual-level behavioral data and long-term demographic data for the same system. Agent-based models often require more parameters than data can constrain. This scaling problem parallels physics' micro-macro gap.

### **Measuring fitness in the wild**

Schroeder et al. (2022) compared short-term fitness proxies against reproductive value in a wild passerine and found short-term proxies are poor predictors of long-term genetic contribution. Lifetime reproductive success requires tracking individuals from birth to death — possible in only a few long-term systems. Koenig et al. (2023) needed **50 years of acorn woodpecker data** to measure lifetime inclusive fitness. This timescale is impossible for most organisms.

## **13\. Honest gap reporting**

### **Genomic architecture of behavioral syndromes in wild populations: genuinely empty**

This is the most striking gap. Despite extensive calls for integration (van Oers & Mueller 2010; Bengston et al. 2018 in *Nature Ecology & Evolution*), **no study has successfully mapped the full genomic architecture underlying a behavioral syndrome in a wild population.** The existing work consists of candidate gene associations (DRD4, SERT) in great tits with small and inconsistent effects, QTL studies in chickens and lab mice (not wild populations), and Dochtermann's cricket work showing genetically conserved syndrome structure without identifying loci. The closest systems (Wytham Woods great tits, Kalahari meerkat project) are moving toward this but have not published results.

### **Coherent theory of behavioral plasticity: fragmented, not coherent**

There is abundant documentation of behavioral plasticity but **no unified predictive theory** explaining when plasticity should evolve versus fixed types, what determines reaction norm shape and limits, and how plasticity itself evolves. Existing frameworks (reaction norm theory, state-behavior feedbacks, adaptive plasticity models) are fragments without integration. **There is no equivalent of optimal foraging theory or Hamilton's rule for behavioral plasticity.**

### **Behavioral ecology × evo-devo: aspirational only**

Most evo-devo work focuses on morphology, not behavior. The few behavioral examples are almost entirely from social insects (Toth & Robinson on honeybees, Moczek on dung beetles). A 2018 analysis of evo-devo conference content showed the field is "mainly focused on 'Geno'" with essentially no behavioral ecology component. For the vast majority of behavioral ecology systems — birds, mammals, fish personality, cooperative breeding — **there is no meaningful developmental biology integration.** The missing link is that behavioral ecologists rarely study neural development, and evo-devo researchers rarely study behavior in ecological contexts.

### **Geographic and taxonomic biases: documented, unresolved**

Over 75% of behavioral ecology studies focus on birds and mammals. Tropical systems are severely underrepresented. Most canonical theories were developed and tested in temperate birds and mammals. Whether optimal foraging theory, kin selection dynamics, or sexual selection frameworks generalize to tropical ectotherms, marine invertebrates, or arthropod-dominated communities is **essentially untested at the theoretical level.**

### **Individual behavior to ecosystem function: underdeveloped frontier**

There is no standardized framework connecting individual behavioral variation to ecosystem-level outcomes (nutrient cycling, energy flow, community assembly). The "ecology of fear" literature (behavioral cascades from predators to trophic dynamics) is the closest approach but remains controversial beyond mesopredator systems.

### **CRISPR for behavioral ecology: empty**

No published work uses gene editing to address behavioral ecology questions in wild or semi-wild populations. The technology exists but the application has not been attempted for behavioral questions.
