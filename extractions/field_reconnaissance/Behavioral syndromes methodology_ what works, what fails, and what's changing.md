# **Behavioral syndromes methodology: what works, what fails, and what's changing**

**The field's analytical infrastructure has advanced dramatically since 2018, but its empirical base remains weak.** Multivariate mixed-effects models, double-hierarchical GLMMs, and Bayesian estimation frameworks now provide the statistical machinery to correctly partition among-individual from within-individual behavioral (co)variance — the core operation of syndrome research. Yet meta-analytic evidence consistently shows that the average published study measures \~39 individuals with two repeated observations, achieving roughly 20% of the sample size required for precise repeatability estimation. The average phenotypic behavioral correlation reported as a "syndrome" is **r ≈ 0.20** after correcting for publication bias. The gap between what the best analytical methods demand and what typical studies deliver is the field's central methodological problem.

This report covers study design requirements, statistical innovations, persistent failures, unsolved problems, and near-horizon developments, drawing primarily on empirical and methodological papers published 2018–2025. It is written for a reader with expert-level familiarity with mixed models, Bayesian estimation, G-matrices, and reaction norms.

## **The average study is dramatically underpowered**

The empirical foundation for minimum design requirements rests on three sources: Bell, Hankison & Laskowski's (2009, *Animal Behaviour* 77:771–783) meta-analysis of **759 repeatability estimates across 98 species**, finding a weighted mean R \= **0.37**; Wolak, Fairbairn & Paulsen's (2012, *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 3:129–137) simulation-based sample size guidelines; and Biro & Stamps' (2015, *Animal Behaviour* 105:223–230) re-analysis of the Bell et al. dataset against those guidelines.

The numbers are stark. For a trait with true R \= 0.4, achieving a 95% CI width of 0.2 requires approximately **N ≥ 50 individuals × k ≥ 3 repeated measures**, or **N ≥ 100 × k \= 2** (Wolak et al. 2012). Increasing measures per individual beyond k \= 3–4 yields diminishing returns under compound symmetry. Biro & Stamps (2015) showed the average published study (N ≈ 40, k \= 2\) achieves only **\~20% of the sample size needed** for rigorous repeatability estimation. For detecting among-individual variance in reaction norm slopes — a prerequisite for testing whether individuals differ in plasticity — van de Pol (2012, *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 3:268–280) showed total observation counts of \~1,000 (individuals × measures) are needed with observations spread across the environmental gradient. Most field studies fall short by an order of magnitude.

The temporal spacing of measurements introduces further bias. Mitchell, Dujon, Beckmann & Biro (2020, *Behavioral Ecology* 31:222–231) demonstrated substantial temporal autocorrelation (r \> 0.4) in spontaneous activity rates even under controlled laboratory conditions. Ignoring autocorrelation **inflates R upward** and biases plasticity estimates. Over 20% of avian studies reporting repeatability used inter-observation intervals shorter than five days. The recommended remedy is either modeling autocorrelation explicitly (AR1 structures) or adopting the "multiple burst" design proposed by Araya-Ajoy, Mathot & Dingemanse (2015, *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 6:1462–1473): clusters of 3–5 observations within days-long bursts, separated by weeks or months, enabling estimation of short-term versus long-term repeatability.

No formal minimum context number has been established for syndrome detection, but Dingemanse & Wright (2020, *Ethology* 126:865–869) recommend **≥3 traits from distinct functional categories**. Budaev (2010, *Ethology* 116:472–480) cautioned that PCA/factor analysis requires a minimum 5:1 subjects-to-variables ratio — a threshold many studies violate.

## **Six persistent design failures and how they distort the literature**

Recurring methodological critiques cluster around six problems, each with documented evidence of prevalence.

**Single measurements per individual** remain common despite being clearly inadequate. Niemelä & Dingemanse (2018, *Animal Behaviour* 145:99–105) argued forcefully that with average R ≈ 0.4, a single measurement captures **60% within-individual noise**. Studies claiming personality or syndromes from unrepeated measures are making unsupported assumptions about the stability of what they measured.

**Failure to partition variance levels** is the most consequential analytical error. Dochtermann (2023, *Behavioral Ecology* 34:913–918) showed quantitatively that when R ≈ 0.4, within-individual correlations have **1.5× greater influence** on phenotypic correlations than among-individual correlations. Reporting phenotypic correlations as evidence for syndromes therefore captures primarily correlated plasticity or measurement error, not stable individual-level covariance. The analytical solution — multivariate mixed-effects models partitioning r\_ind from r\_e — has been available since Dingemanse & Dochtermann (2013, *Journal of Animal Ecology* 82:39–54), but adoption remains incomplete. Brommer & Class (2017, *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* 71:8) compiled 109 paired estimates from 30 studies and found phenotypic correlations are "reasonable predictors" of among-individual correlations in sign, but the absolute discrepancies were substantial.

**Pseudo-repeatability** was experimentally demonstrated by Niemelä & Dingemanse (2017, *Journal of Animal Ecology* 86:1033–1043) using field crickets. Repeatability for stationary individuals was **31.1%**, but translocation revealed that individual identity explained only **17.8%**, with burrow identity contributing 8.7%. Any study where animals are repeatedly tested in the same microenvironment risks inflating R through environmental confounds.

**Ignoring temporal change** in behavior biases R estimates. Biro & Stamps (2015) demonstrated this using the Bell et al. (2009) corpus, showing that age-related, seasonal, and experience-driven behavioral shifts are routinely absorbed into repeatability calculations without acknowledgment. The temporal autocorrelation problem documented by Mitchell et al. (2020) compounds this: closely spaced observations are non-independent, and failing to model this inflates both R and slope variance estimates.

**Publication bias** is documented. Garamszegi, Markó & Herczeg (2012, *Evolutionary Ecology* 26:1213–1235) meta-analyzed correlated behaviors and found significant publication bias; the uncorrected average behavioral correlation was r \= 0.265, dropping to **r \= 0.198 after bias correction**. Only 40 of 105 estimates were statistically significant. The pace-of-life syndrome literature is especially affected: Royauté, Berdal, Garrison & Dochtermann (2018, *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* 72:64) meta-analyzed predicted behavior–life history and behavior–physiology correlations and found them **largely unsupported** at the individual level.

**PCA misapplication** persists. PCA applied to single-measurement data with small samples and few behavioral variables has no foundation for syndrome inference. Dingemanse & Wright (2020) stated explicitly that PCA "does not allow testing specific biological hypotheses" and that SEM or factor analysis is preferred where sample sizes permit.

## **Multivariate mixed models and DHGLMMs are now the analytical standard**

The analytical state of the art consists of a nested hierarchy of models, each adding complexity and requiring more data. The current best-practice pipeline, synthesized across O'Dea, Noble & Nakagawa (2022, *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 13:278–291), Houslay & Wilson (2017, *Behavioral Ecology* 28:948–952), and Hertel et al. (2020, *Movement Ecology* 8:12), involves four stages.

**Stage 1: Univariate random-intercept models** estimate repeatability per trait. Software: rptR (Stoffel, Nakagawa & Schielzeth 2017\) for frequentist estimation; MCMCglmm or brms for Bayesian estimation with full posteriors.

**Stage 2: Multivariate random-intercept models** estimate among-individual correlations — the behavioral syndrome proper. The critical methodological paper is Houslay & Wilson (2017), which demonstrated that the common "two-step" approach of extracting BLUPs from univariate models and correlating them is **anticonservative** because it fails to propagate uncertainty in random-effect predictions. The correct approach specifies bivariate or multivariate models with unstructured random-effects covariance matrices. In MCMCglmm: `cbind(trait1, trait2) ~ trait-1 + ...` with `random = ~us(trait):animal_ID`. In brms: `bf(trait1 ~ ...) + bf(trait2 ~ ...)` with appropriate group-level effects.

**Stage 3: Random regression models** estimate individual variation in plasticity (behavioral reaction norms). The model adds individual-specific slopes along an environmental gradient: `behavior_ij = (β₀ + u₀ᵢ) + (β₁ + u₁ᵢ)x_ij + e_ij`. The covariance between u₀ and u₁ captures the intercept–slope correlation. Mitchell & Houslay (2021, *Behavioral Ecology* 32:25–29) proved the mathematical equivalence of random regression and character-state approaches in the two-context case, showing that treating behavior in each environment as a separate response variable (character-state) yields identical information to fitting a reaction norm, but with more intuitive parameterization for discrete environments.

**Stage 4: Double-hierarchical GLMMs** model individual variation in predictability (residual intra-individual variance). O'Dea et al. (2022) provided the definitive framework, identifying **10 interpretable types of individual variation** from combined mean and dispersion models: personality syndromes, plasticity syndromes, and predictability syndromes as correlations between different variance components. They recommended minimum \~20 individuals × \~20 observations each for reliable dispersion-level random effects. Hertel et al. (2021, *Journal of Animal Ecology* 90:723–737) applied brms-implemented DHGLMMs to biologging data from brown bears, finding behavioral type and predictability more pronounced within years than across years. Cleasby, Nakagawa & Schielzeth (2015, *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 6:27–37) introduced the CVP (coefficient of variation in predictability) as a standardized effect size for population-level variation in predictability.

Bayesian estimation now dominates. The advantages for syndrome research are specific: variance-covariance matrices estimated via REML can hit boundary constraints (variances at zero, correlations at ±1), while Bayesian methods with appropriate priors avoid this; repeatability is a ratio of variance components whose credible intervals come directly from posterior distributions; and small-sample regularization through priors prevents the overfitting endemic to multivariate models where covariance parameters grow quadratically with traits. Prior specification matters: MCMCglmm uses inverse-Wishart priors that can be unintentionally informative for small k, while brms implements LKJ priors on correlation matrices (recommended: `lkj(2)` for mild regularization) with separate half-Student-t priors on standard deviations, offering more interpretable and flexible control. Model comparison via **WAIC and LOO-CV** (available in brms/Stan) is increasingly preferred over DIC, which behaves poorly for complex hierarchical models.

## **G-matrix estimation remains data-limited for most wild systems**

Estimating genetic variance-covariance matrices for behavioral traits requires the multivariate animal model, partitioning phenotypic variance into additive genetic (V\_A), permanent environment (V\_PE), and residual components. Dochtermann, Schwab & Sih (2019, *Journal of Heredity* 110:403–410) meta-analyzed behavioral heritability and found a mean h² \= **0.235** (phylogenetically controlled), with \~30% of phenotypic variation attributable to additive genetics and no phylogenetic signal in heritability estimates.

The practical barriers are formidable. Pedigree depth in wild populations is rarely sufficient for multivariate G-matrix estimation with behavioral traits; most studies can only estimate among-individual correlations (r\_ind) at the phenotypic level, not the genetic level (r\_A). Genomic relatedness matrices (GRMs) from SNP data can substitute for pedigrees, as demonstrated by Gervais et al. (2019, *Molecular Ecology Resources*) using RAD-seq in wild roe deer. However, Csilléry et al. (2006) showed many wild populations have insufficient variance in pairwise relatedness for marker-based approaches to work. Class & Brommer (2015, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 282:20142777) revealed a further complication: in wild blue tits, a strong genetic correlation underlying a behavioral syndrome **disappeared during development** due to genotype–age interactions, meaning cross-age genetic correlations do not equal within-age correlations — a finding that requires pedigreed populations with measures at multiple ages, an extraordinarily data-intensive design.

Shared environment confounds remain pernicious. Without cross-fostering, V\_A estimates are inflated by shared environmental variance. Ponzi et al. (2018, *PLOS ONE*) provided tutorials for multiple-matrix animal models incorporating social network matrices and natal territory matrices to disentangle genetic from non-genetic effects, but these approaches demand even more data. The positive-definiteness constraint on G-matrices creates additional problems with small samples; Bayesian methods with appropriate priors enforce this constraint naturally.

An emerging frontier is **covariance reaction norm (CRN) models** that allow the G-matrix itself to vary across environmental gradients. Applied to cooperative behaviors in meerkats, these models detected nonlinear effects of group size on genetic (co)variances. This approach is computationally demanding (requiring Stan implementations) but opens the possibility of testing whether genetic syndrome architecture is environment-dependent.

## **Computer vision has not yet been connected to formal syndrome estimation**

The automated behavioral phenotyping toolkit has matured rapidly. DeepLabCut (Mathis et al. 2018, *Nature Neuroscience*) achieves \~4.4 pixel tracking error for mouse pose estimation. SLEAP (Pereira et al. 2022, *Nature Methods*) handles multi-animal tracking with improved usability. Keypoint-MoSeq (Weinreb et al. 2024, *Nature Methods*) segments continuous pose data into discrete behavioral syllables (\~400 ms duration) using autoregressive hidden Markov models, outperforming alternatives (B-SOiD, MotionMapper, VAME) at identifying natural behavioral transitions. These tools are production-ready for mice, rats, flies, zebrafish, livestock, and primates.

The most complete integration of automated tracking with individual difference estimation is **Forkosh et al. (2019, *Nature Neuroscience* 22:2023–2028)**. Using a custom "Social Box" tracking system, they measured 60 distinct behaviors automatically over 4+ days in 168 male mice housed in 42 groups of 4\. Rather than PCA, they applied Linear Discriminant Analysis to derive "Identity Domains" — dimensions maximizing between-individual variability while maximizing within-individual consistency. Four significant identity domains emerged; ID1 predicted social rank (R² \= **0.72**). Traits were stable across regrouping and from juvenile to adult. ID scores correlated with brain gene expression differences. This study demonstrates the throughput possible — but it used LDA rather than the multivariate mixed-effects framework standard in behavioral ecology.

At the other extreme of scale, Buchanan et al. (2022, *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*) quantified individual behavioral biases in **183,000 *Drosophila melanogaster*** across 80 million decisions using automated Y-maze assays. This throughput is orders of magnitude beyond what syndrome studies typically achieve.

**The critical gap**: no published study has yet completed the full pipeline from pose estimation (DLC/SLEAP) → behavioral classification (Keypoint-MoSeq/B-SOiD) → repeated-measures quantification → multivariate mixed-effects models → among-individual correlation matrix. The automated tools generate the data volumes that proper syndrome estimation demands, but the integration has not been formalized. This represents the field's most immediate technical opportunity.

## **Network and latent variable approaches offer structural alternatives to PCA**

Martin et al. (2019, *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 10:245–257) introduced the **EGA+GNM framework**, integrating Exploratory Graph Analysis with Generalized Network Modelling. Applied to behavioral data from common marmosets, they showed that traditional factor analysis captures only latent common causes of trait covariation, while Gaussian Graphical Models capture direct pairwise behavioral interactions. Their GNM models **both simultaneously**, revealing behavioral clusters linked by pairwise interactions (e.g., allogrooming–activity links) invisible to factor analysis alone. The framework uses qgraph, psychonetrics, and EGAnet R packages, with tutorials provided. Goold et al. (2016, *Royal Society Open Science* 3:160009) had earlier applied graphical LASSO networks to domestic dog behavior, demonstrating that partial correlation networks yield different insights than zero-order correlations.

The advantage of network approaches is causal transparency: they represent conditional independence relationships rather than assuming behaviors covary because of a latent common cause. The disadvantage is **sample size sensitivity** — regularized network estimation requires larger N than PCA for stable edges, and the distinction between within-individual and between-individual networks (critical for syndrome research) demands intensive longitudinal data that few animal studies can provide.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA/SEM) offers a complementary structural approach. Martin & Suarez (2017, *American Journal of Primatology* 79:e22670) demonstrated the full information-theoretic model selection paradigm for personality structure in bonobos, comparing seven competing CFA models using AIC. Goold & Newberry (2017, *PLOS ONE* 12:e0183595) applied multi-group CFA to 4,743 shelter dogs and found excellent model fit (CFI \= 0.97, RMSEA \= 0.03), but — critically — detected violations of **measurement invariance** across sex and age groups, meaning that behavioral loadings on latent personality dimensions differed by demographic category. This finding implies that simple factor scores may be misleading when invariance does not hold, a concern the field has largely ignored. CFA's requirement for pre-specified models and N \> 200 limits its applicability in many wild systems.

## **Five unsolved problems and what blocks progress on each**

**Separating personality from plasticity** is the most fundamental unresolved issue. Random regression models treat the intercept as "personality" and the slope as "plasticity," but as Mitchell & Houslay (2021) showed, these are estimated parameters whose biological interpretation shifts with centering of the environmental variable. More fundamentally, there is no empirical method to determine whether an individual's consistent behavioral tendency reflects a stable genetic trait, an environmentally canalized phenotype, or an artifact of consistent environmental exposure — without experimentally manipulating environments while tracking individuals longitudinally. The field currently lacks **consensus on whether "personality" should be defined by the intercept alone or by the full reaction norm shape**.

**Estimating syndrome structure from incomplete wild observation** is constrained by informative missingness. Mixed models handle unbalanced data under the missing-at-random assumption, but capture probability often covaries with behavioral type (bold animals are caught more readily), violating MAR. State-space models are theoretically promising — they model latent behavioral states separately from observation error and handle irregular intervals naturally — but **no off-the-shelf SSM software exists for multivariate behavioral syndrome estimation**, and extending SSMs to the multivariate case remains computationally challenging. Simulation studies evaluating how different missing-data patterns affect covariance parameter recovery are largely absent from the literature.

**Connecting syndromes to genomic architecture** is blocked by a sample-size wall. Human GWAS for personality traits require N \> 100,000; wild population studies rarely exceed N \= 2,000 genotyped individuals. York (2018, *Genetics*) compiled \>1,000 genomic loci associated with behavioral variation across taxa and found courtship and feeding behaviors have **\~3-fold larger genomic effect sizes** than other behavioral categories, suggesting different genetic architectures. But no study has identified specific loci underlying behavioral syndrome structure in wild populations. The candidate-gene era produced unreliable results — the DRD4–exploration association in great tits (Fidler et al. 2007\) failed replication across additional populations (Korsten et al. 2010). Available evidence increasingly favors highly polygenic architectures, making current wild-population sample sizes inadequate by 1–2 orders of magnitude.

**Measuring fitness consequences without confounding** remains unsolved. Observational personality–fitness correlations cannot distinguish direct selection on personality from indirect selection through correlated traits or shared environmental drivers. The Lande–Arnold (1983) selection gradient framework is directly applicable but has been **rarely applied to behavioral syndromes**. Path analysis requires causal structure specification that is usually unknown. Sample sizes in wild populations are typically insufficient for complex SEM. Experimental approaches (phenotypic engineering, reciprocal transplants) are logistically difficult. Frequency-dependent selection on syndromes is theoretically predicted but has **never been convincingly demonstrated empirically**.

**Determining whether syndrome structure is conserved or artifactual** is confounded by the assay battery problem: similar syndrome structures across taxa may reflect similar measurement methods rather than biological homology. White et al. (2020, *Behavioral Ecology* 31:340–356) estimated among-individual covariance matrices for four behaviors in seven freshwater fish species using identical open-field trials and found that the orientation of the first eigenvector matched a shy–bold continuum in only **3 of 7 species**, though higher-dimensional matrix shape showed weak phylogenetic signal. Royauté, Hedrick & Dochtermann (2022, *Journal of Evolutionary Biology* 35:311–323) found phylogenetic shaping of among-individual variances, within-individual variances, and average behaviors in five cricket species, supporting constraints — but cross-species comparisons require identical assays whose ecological validity differs across species with different sensory systems and life histories. Phylogenetic comparative methods for **matrix-valued traits** remain underdeveloped relative to those for scalar traits.

## **Published quality criteria exist but compliance is uneven**

Dingemanse & Wright (2020) is the most explicit published quality standard, written as an editorial for *Ethology*. Their criteria: repeated measures are mandatory; among-individual correlations (not phenotypic correlations) must be estimated using multivariate mixed models; PCA alone is insufficient; and cross-context consistency requires reaction norm approaches. Carter et al. (2013, *Biological Reviews* 88:465–475) identified the **jingle-jangle fallacy** — same behaviors given different labels across studies — and advocated for construct validity (ecological, convergent, and discriminant) borrowed from psychometrics. This paper has over 1,000 citations and is widely considered essential reading.

A critical reader evaluating a new syndrome claim should check, in order: (1) were individuals measured more than once per behavior; (2) were multivariate mixed models used to estimate among-individual correlations, or only phenotypic correlations reported; (3) are repeatabilities reported with confidence intervals for each trait, and are they substantially above zero; (4) were observations spaced sufficiently to avoid temporal autocorrelation; (5) could environmental confounds generate pseudo-repeatability; and (6) are the measured behaviors genuinely distinct traits, or is the "syndrome" a within-context artifact.

The adjusted versus unadjusted repeatability debate remains unresolved. Nakagawa & Schielzeth (2010, *Biological Reviews* 85:935–956) distinguished agreement (unadjusted) from consistency (adjusted) repeatability. Biro & Stamps (2015) argued that excluding systematic variation sources (including time trends) leads to invalid estimates; the Dingemanse camp counters that adjusted R better isolates intrinsic individual differences. The emerging pragmatic resolution is to **report both**, with full model specifications.

## **Three developments will reshape the field within five years**

**Automated phenotyping at syndrome-relevant scale** is the most transformative near-term development. The complete pipeline — pose estimation → behavioral segmentation → repeated-measures quantification → multivariate mixed-effects models — has not been published but all components exist. When someone closes this loop, the field will gain access to datasets orders of magnitude larger than current practice allows, finally meeting the sample-size demands that O'Dea et al. (2022) specify for DHGLM estimation. The Forkosh et al. (2019) identity domains approach provides a conceptual bridge, but formal integration with the behavioral ecology modeling framework is needed.

**Network psychometrics imported from human personality science** reconceptualizes syndromes as emergent systems of mutually reinforcing behavioral components rather than reflections of latent variables. Borsboom et al. (2021, *Nature Reviews Methods Primers*) provides directly transferable methods. The critical analytical challenge is distinguishing within-individual from between-individual network structures — analogous to the within-individual versus among-individual correlation decomposition already standard in mixed-model approaches, but requiring intensive time-series data from individual animals. Bio-logging and accelerometry are making this feasible; a recent PNAS study demonstrated consistent structuring of behavioral sequences across meerkats, coatis, and hyenas using accelerometer-derived data.

**Dynamic systems models** from clinical psychology — time-varying autoregressive models, multilevel vector autoregressive models, idiographic person-specific network models — can distinguish between-individual and within-individual dynamics, directly analogous to personality versus plasticity. These methods require intensive longitudinal data (typically \>50 timepoints per individual), which automated monitoring is beginning to deliver. Their application to animal behavioral syndromes is imminent but has not yet appeared in the behavioral ecology literature.

## **Conclusion**

The methodology of behavioral syndromes research has undergone a quiet revolution since 2018\. The analytical toolkit — multivariate mixed models, DHGLMs, Bayesian estimation, character-state models, network approaches — is now substantially more powerful than the empirical data it is typically applied to. The field's primary constraint is not statistical sophistication but **data volume**: too few individuals, too few repeated measures, too few contexts, measured over too short a time span. Automated behavioral phenotyping is the most likely near-term solution, but no one has yet published the complete pipeline connecting computer vision output to formal syndrome estimation.

Three honest assessments the field should internalize: first, the average reported behavioral syndrome is a weak effect (r ≈ 0.20 bias-corrected) that may be substantially driven by within-individual covariance rather than stable individual differences; second, repeatability estimates in the literature are systematically inflated by pseudo-repeatability, temporal autocorrelation, and ignoring temporal change; and third, connecting syndromes to genomic architecture or fitness consequences remains blocked by fundamental sample-size limitations that no analytical innovation can circumvent — only technological scaling can. The gap between what the field's best methods require and what its typical datasets provide defines the frontier of progress.
