# Calls for Explanatory Theories

# **RUN 1 – Field Reconnaissance: Animal Behavior and Ethology**

## **Scope and Framing**

This pass surveyed methodological self-critique within behavioral ecology, animal personality research, comparative cognition, and the proximate/ultimate explanation literature. The dominant finding is that the conversation is **fragmented across subfields** rather than unified: the strongest material is in (i) meta-analytic audits of personality and pace-of-life-syndrome (POLS) frameworks, (ii) reformulations of the Tinbergen/Mayr explanatory schema, and (iii) replication/methodology reform in comparative cognition. There is no single "Eronen-Borsboom-Bringmann"–style umbrella paper for the field as a whole. The four roles (A–D) defined in the prompt map onto this field unevenly; Role C (formal apparatus) is best represented, Role B (phenomenon audit) has produced the most consequential recent work, Role A is diffuse, and Role D (mechanism vs actuarial prediction) does not have a clean analog.

---

## **1\. Primary Candidates**

### **Candidate 1 — Laskowski, Chang, Sheehy & Aguiñaga (2022)**

**Citation.** Laskowski, K. L., Chang, C.-C., Sheehy, K., & Aguiñaga, J. (2022). Consistent Individual Behavioral Variation: What Do We Know and Where Are We Going? *Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics*, 53, 161–182. DOI: 10.1146/annurev-ecolsys-102220-011451.

**Roles performed: B (primary), with elements of A.**

**Central methodological argument.** This Annual Review is the most explicit recent phenomenon audit of the animal-personality / behavioral-syndromes program. The authors stipulate that the field is now "well established" empirically but ask which of its leading explanatory frameworks have actually accumulated the empirical support their advocates claim. Their answer is sober: the two dominant adaptive frameworks — the pace-of-life syndrome hypothesis and state-dependent feedback (positive feedback / state–behavior loop) models — have at best "mixed support." They argue that consequences of individual variation are well-studied at the individual level but understudied at higher (community, ecosystem) levels, and that the field has accumulated descriptions of consistency without yet constraining the causal architecture that produces it. They call for theory-driven re-evaluation rather than additional descriptive replication, and frame the agenda as "What do we know? What questions remain?"

**Why field-level.** Annual Review article explicitly synthesizing the field's two flagship explanatory frameworks; it is not a narrow technical contribution but a programmatic stocktake from established voices.

---

### **Candidate 2 — Royauté, Berdal, Garrison & Dochtermann (2018)**

**Citation.** Royauté, R., Berdal, M. A., Garrison, C. R., & Dochtermann, N. A. (2018). Paceless life? A meta-analysis of the pace-of-life syndrome hypothesis. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*, 72(3), 64\. DOI: 10.1007/s00265-018-2472-z.

**Roles performed: B (primary).**

**Central methodological argument.** This meta-analysis remains the field's single most cited empirical challenge to a high-profile organizing framework. Aggregating across studies that test the predicted couplings among behavior, life-history, and physiology, Royauté et al. find that the predicted "fast–slow" trait covariation is weak or absent in most contexts, with effect sizes substantially smaller than the framework's advocates assumed. The piece is methodologically substantive: it walks through why measurement at the individual rather than population level is required for a genuine test of POLS, why single-shot phenotypic correlations conflate within- and among-individual variation, and why widely cited supportive studies often used inappropriate designs. The constructive contribution is a set of design and analytical criteria (repeated-measures, multilevel decomposition of variance, attention to acquisition-vs-allocation of resources) under which the framework could in principle be confirmed or rejected. It is the closest analog in animal behavior to the kind of phenomenon-audit work that has reshaped personality psychology.

**Why field-level.** Targets a flagship cross-subfield framework (POLS) explicitly proposed as integrative theory; it is the meta-analytic "bellwether" that subsequent commentators (including Laskowski et al. 2022\) treat as the field's verdict on POLS.

### **Candidate 3 — Laland, Uller, Feldman, Sterelny, Müller, Moczek, Jablonka & Odling-Smee (2015)**

**Citation.** Laland, K. N., Uller, T., Feldman, M. W., Sterelny, K., Müller, G. B., Moczek, A., Jablonka, E., & Odling-Smee, J. (2015). The extended evolutionary synthesis: its structure, assumptions and predictions. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences*, 282(1813), 20151019\. DOI: 10.1098/rspb.2015.1019.

**Roles performed: A and C.** *Caveat: this piece advocates a particular theoretical architecture (the EES); it is not an even-handed audit. It is included here because it is the most explicit recent attempt within evolutionary biology of behavior to argue that the field's existing causal vocabulary — Mayr's proximate/ultimate dichotomy — is impeding explanatory progress, and to propose a shared conceptual reformulation.*

**Central methodological argument.** Laland et al. argue that the Modern Synthesis's proximate/ultimate split — institutionalized after Mayr (1961) and operationalized in behavioral biology through Tinbergen's four questions — has functioned as a methodological filter that ruled out reciprocal-causation explanations (developmental bias, niche construction, inclusive inheritance) on a priori grounds rather than evidential ones. Their constructive proposal is a framework in which causation cycles through biological systems recursively, and in which ontogenetic processes are not bracketed off from evolutionary ones. The methodological payoff they claim is sharper: it reorganizes what kind of evidence counts as bearing on what kind of question. Crucially for the present project, the paper argues at the field level rather than for a particular phenomenon. Its companion paper (Laland et al. 2011, *Science* 334:1512–1516, DOI: 10.1126/science.1210879) is the formal predecessor on Mayr's dichotomy specifically.

**Why field-level.** This is a programmatic Royal Society "Darwin Review" speaking explicitly to and about the conceptual structure of the field as a whole.

---

### **Candidate 4 — Bergman & Beehner (2022)**

**Citation.** Bergman, T. J., & Beehner, J. C. (2022). Leveling with Tinbergen: Four levels simplified to causes and consequences. *Evolutionary Anthropology: Issues, News, and Reviews*, 31(1), 12–19. DOI: 10.1002/evan.21931.

**Roles performed: C (primary).**

**Central methodological argument.** Bergman & Beehner argue that Tinbergen's standard two-by-two (proximate/ultimate × current/historical) has become a source of confusion in animal behavior research because researchers conflate the *temporal* dimension of explanation with the *type* of claim being made. They propose collapsing the four levels onto a single temporal continuum partitioned into "causes" (everything probabilistically antecedent to a behavior — evolutionary history, development, immediate mechanism) and "consequences" (everything downstream — fitness effects, ecological feedback). The aim is to give the field a vocabulary in which it is unambiguous what evidence supports what type of claim, and which kinds of question are being asked at which time scale. This is exactly the role the prompt frames as Role C — formal apparatus for distinguishing levels and types of claim — and Bergman & Beehner are recognized voices within animal behavior (not philosophers writing from the outside).

**Why field-level.** Speaks directly about the explanatory grammar of the discipline; cited within months of publication as the new reference point in Tinbergen-reformulation debates (Leca 2023, Dawkins 2023).

## **2\. Domain Assessment**

**Does animal behavior/ethology have a coherent body of self-critical methodological literature comparable to personality psychology?**

No, not coherently. The methodological self-critique exists but is **fragmented across three sub-conversations that talk past each other**:

1. **Behavioral ecology / animal personality** has the most mature critical machinery, organized around Dingemanse, Dochtermann, Réale, Wright, Royauté, Niemelä, and Westneat. This conversation is largely about variance decomposition (within- vs among-individual, repeatable vs labile), the legitimacy of behavioral-syndrome and POLS frameworks, and the design conditions under which "personality" claims are testable. It contains the field's strongest meta-analytic audits (Royauté et al. 2018; Dochtermann et al. 2019; Niemelä & Dingemanse 2018, *Proc R Soc B* 285:20172823, DOI: 10.1098/rspb.2017.2823) and its most sustained methodological-standards papers (Dingemanse & Wright 2020). It has also produced a polemical sub-debate (Beekman & Jordan 2017 and the Sih/Bell/Briffa/Dingemanse/Pruitt/Jungwirth replies) about whether "animal personality" adds explanatory value above existing behavioral-ecology frameworks at all.

2. **Reciprocal-causation / EES** is dominated by the Laland circle and is more theory-architectural than empirical-audit-driven. It has produced shared vocabulary (Roles A and C) but is also openly advocacy, which is an important caveat: critics within evolutionary biology have argued the EES literature overstates the evidential basis for its proposed reorganization (e.g., Welch, Charlesworth and others; this is not pursued here).

3. **Comparative cognition** has a separate replication-and-reform conversation organized around the ManyX consortia (ManyPrimates, ManyDogs, ManyBirds) and Farrar et al. (2020, *Animal Behavior and Cognition* 7:1–22, DOI: 10.26451/abc.07.01.02.2020). This conversation is mostly about reliability and generalizability rather than the explanation/description distinction per se. There is also a parallel philosophy-of-comparative-cognition literature (Buckner, Andrews, Halina, Dacey) — flagged separately below — which engages most closely with the kind of "what type of claim is being made" analysis that the prompt's Role C calls for, but is largely written *by* philosophers rather than working comparative cognition researchers.

**Role-by-role coverage in this domain:**

* **Role A (theory crisis diagnosis):** Diffuse. There is no single field-level paper diagnosing animal behavior as descriptively rich and explanatorily thin in the way Eronen & Bringmann (2021) does for personality psychology. The closest insider voices are Laland et al. (2015), the Beekman & Jordan (2017) provocation, and (less constructively) more recent natural-history laments such as Guevara-Fiore (2025). Each has limitations: Laland advocates a specific reform; Beekman & Jordan are largely critical without a constructive replacement; Guevara-Fiore is more about academic-incentive structures than explanatory inadequacy.  
* **Role B (phenomenon audit):** Strongest role. Royauté et al. 2018 and Laskowski et al. 2022 are the central pieces; Dochtermann et al. 2019 (heritability meta-analysis) and Niemelä & Dingemanse 2018 (state-personality meta-analysis) supplement.  
* **Role C (formal apparatus):** Well represented. Bateson & Laland 2013, Laland et al. 2011/2015, Bergman & Beehner 2022, and (philosophical adjuncts) Buckner.  
* **Role D (mechanism vs actuarial prediction):** **Effectively absent in the form the prompt describes.** Animal behavior does not frame its problem this way. The closest analogs are calls for repeated-measures and reaction-norm designs over single-occasion phenotypic measurements (Dingemanse & Dochtermann 2013, Dingemanse & Wright 2020, Westneat et al. 2015), but these are about variance decomposition, not about replacing risk-prediction models with process models — because animal behavior does not, on the whole, do clinical-style risk prediction. **This is the cleanest gap.** See gap section below.

## **3\. Secondary Candidates**

### **Bateson & Laland (2013)**

Bateson, P., & Laland, K. N. (2013). Tinbergen's four questions: an appreciation and an update. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution*, 28(12), 712–718. DOI: 10.1016/j.tree.2013.09.013. Role C, with elements of A. The 50th-anniversary reformulation paired with Nesse's response (DOI: 10.1016/j.tree.2013.10.008). Older than the 2018+ window but remains canonical and is actively cited as the reference point. Largely superseded for current debates by Bergman & Beehner (2022) but should be on any reading list.

### **Dingemanse & Wright (2020)**

Dingemanse, N. J., & Wright, J. (2020). Criteria for acceptable studies of animal personality and behavioural syndromes. *Ethology*, 126(9), 865–869. DOI: 10.1111/eth.13082. Role D-adjacent / Role B. A short editorial-style "criteria" paper specifying repeated-measures designs, hierarchical decomposition of variance, and reporting standards. Methodologically substantive but limited in scope (a standards document for one journal); valuable as a normative reference, less so as a field-diagnosis piece. Pairs naturally with Dingemanse & Dochtermann (2013, *Journal of Animal Ecology* 82:39–54, DOI: 10.1111/1365-2656.12013).

### **Carter, Feeney, Marshall, Cowlishaw & Heinsohn (2013)**

Carter, A. J., Feeney, W. E., Marshall, H. H., Cowlishaw, G., & Heinsohn, R. (2013). Animal personality: what are behavioural ecologists measuring? *Biological Reviews*, 88(2), 465–475. DOI: 10.1111/brv.12007. Role B, with construct-validity dimension that Roles A/C touch. Imports psychometric construct-validity logic into behavioral ecology and argues that behavioral-syndrome correlations cannot, by themselves, validate the constructs they invoke. Outside the 5–7 year window but remains a foundational citation; partially superseded but still actively shaping current debate (e.g., the jingle-jangle critique).

### **Farrar, Boeckle & Clayton (2020) / ManyPrimates (2019)**

Farrar, B. G., Boeckle, M., & Clayton, N. S. (2020). Replications in Comparative Cognition: What Should We Expect and How Can We Improve? *Animal Behavior and Cognition*, 7(1), 1–22. DOI: 10.26451/abc.07.01.02.2020. Paired with: ManyPrimates, Altschul, D., Beran, M. J., et al. (2019). Establishing an infrastructure for collaboration in primate cognition research. *PLOS ONE*, 14(10), e0223675. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0223675. Role A-adjacent and methodological reform. Diagnoses comparative cognition's small-sample, narrow-species, single-site infrastructure as a field-level reliability problem and proposes a constructive infrastructure (consortium science). Worth flagging but secondary because the framing is reliability/generalizability rather than the description-vs-explanation axis the prompt centers.

### **West, Dall & Griffin (2025)**

West, S. A., Dall, S. R. X., & Griffin, A. S. (2025). Behavioural ecology in the twenty-first century. *Nature Ecology & Evolution*. DOI: 10.1038/s41559-025-02912-3. Role A-adjacent, but largely a *defense* of behavioral ecology's hypothesis-driven, mathematical-modeling tradition rather than a diagnosis of crisis. Useful for even-handedness — it is the methodological position that would push back against an architectural-explanatory framing, arguing that behavioral ecology already does explanation well via simple models plus empirical test. Worth reading as the counterposition to Role-A diagnoses.

## **4\. Honest Gap Flagging**

### **Role A (theory crisis diagnosis) — partial gap.**

There is no animal-behavior analog to Eronen & Bringmann's "Theory Crisis in Psychology" or Borsboom's network-theory umbrella papers. The closest insider candidates each fall short in a specific way:

* **Laland et al. 2015** is theory-advocating, not crisis-diagnosing of method per se.  
* **Beekman & Jordan 2017** ("Does the field of animal personality provide any new insights for behavioral ecology?", *Behavioral Ecology* 28:617–623, DOI: 10.1093/beheco/arx022) is constructive-adjacent but ultimately polemical: their proposal is essentially that the personality program should fold back into mainstream behavioral ecology, not a positive methodological reform program.  
* **Guevara-Fiore 2025** ("Natural history in practice", *Behavioral Ecology* DOI: 10.1093/beheco/araf125) is a constructive call but addresses incentive structures (publication pressure, natural-history training), not the explanation/description distinction.

The honest reading is that animal behavior does not currently have a flagship Role-A paper. The closest functional substitute is the *aggregation* of Royauté et al. 2018 \+ Laskowski et al. 2022, which together amount to a Role-A diagnosis without explicitly making one.

### **Role D (mechanism over actuarial prediction) — substantial gap; framing does not transfer.**

This framing — predictors-vs-mechanisms, dynamic process models vs cross-sectional trait correlations — does not map cleanly onto animal behavior because the field does not engage in clinical-style risk prediction the way human psychopathology does. The functional analog is the "behavioral reaction norm" program (Dingemanse, Kazem, Réale & Wright 2010, *TREE* 25:81–89, DOI: 10.1016/j.tree.2009.07.013), which advocates dynamic, longitudinal, individual-level decomposition of variance over single-occasion phenotypic correlation. But this is framed as a quantitative-genetic and statistical issue (variance partitioning, plasticity slopes) rather than as a "mechanism vs prediction" methodological choice. The closest piece in spirit is **Dochtermann & Royauté (2019)** — "The mean matters: going beyond repeatability to interpret behavioural variation," *Animal Behaviour* 153:147–150, DOI: 10.1016/j.anbehav.2019.05.012 — which argues that repeatability statistics (the field's standard summary) are indicators of phenomenon presence, not of mechanism, and that the field should report the mean-level information needed to interpret what the variance is *about*. This is the closest near-miss. It does not fully qualify because (i) it is a short methods note, not a field-level statement, and (ii) its framing is descriptive-statistical rather than mechanism-vs-prediction.

**Recommendation: do not force Role D onto this domain.** The architectural-explanatory framing that Role D presupposes (predictive risk models as a dominant mode that needs replacing) is a feature of clinical and human-personality science, not of animal behavior, where the dominant mode being criticized is descriptive-correlational rather than predictive-actuarial. If the four-run program needs a Role-D analog from this field, the honest substitute is the reaction-norm / behavioral-reaction-norm program, which makes a process-vs-correlation argument rather than a mechanism-vs-prediction argument. This should be flagged as a structural disanalogy between fields, not papered over.

### **Philosophers writing from outside the field — flagged separately, not mixed with insider voices.**

Because the prompt asks that philosophers be flagged rather than treated as insider voices, the following are noted as adjacent but not counted as primary domain candidates:

* **Buckner, C.** — extensive work on inference, simplicity, and parsimony in comparative cognition; the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry "Methods in Comparative Cognition" (Halina, M., 2024\) is the synthetic reference. These pieces address Role C (what kind of claim is being made; what counts as a "cognitive" rather than "associative" explanation) but are written from philosophy of science.  
* **Andrews, K.** — on folk-psychological inference in animal cognition; similarly philosophically positioned. These authors should be cited if and when the analytic framework explicitly leans on philosophy-of-science apparatus, but should not be conflated with the Dingemanse/Dochtermann/Laskowski/Bergman insider conversation.

### **Citation integrity caveats.**

* The Royauté et al. 2018 finding is robust and not overstated; the paper itself is appropriately careful about what its meta-analysis can and cannot conclude (it does not say POLS is *false*, only that the predicted couplings are not consistently detected and that design constraints may be partly responsible).  
* The Laskowski et al. 2022 review's claim that POLS and state-dependent feedback models have "mixed support" is a fair gloss of its content; the review is more even-handed than its strongest critics would be.  
* The Laland et al. 2015 EES paper is widely cited as making programmatic claims about the conceptual structure of evolutionary biology; readers should be aware that within evolutionary biology this characterization is contested (Welch 2017; Charlesworth, Barton, Charlesworth 2017, *Proc R Soc B* 284:20170300, DOI: 10.1098/rspb.2017.0300) and the EES literature should not be treated as the neutral consensus position.  
* The Beekman & Jordan 2017 exchange should be cited as a debate, not as a settled diagnosis: their critique drew published replies from at least six personality researchers, and the field's response has been to refine standards (Dingemanse & Wright 2020\) rather than to abandon the program.

## **Big picture**

This is a strong reconnaissance. The honest gap-flagging is doing the most important work — particularly the call to *not* force Role D onto animal behavior. That's exactly the kind of structural disanalogy I'd want to know rather than have papered over.

Three things stand out before getting into specifics.

**The Role D gap is real and tells us something about ABHOC's positioning.** The prompt's four-role template was implicitly built on the structure of human psychology research, which has a clinical wing that produces actuarial prediction models alongside an academic wing that produces theoretical taxonomies. Animal behavior has no clinical wing — there's no "what's the risk that this fish develops behavioral pathology" research program with its own DSM. So Ringwald's complaint doesn't translate. **The Laskowski \+ Royauté pair is doing exactly what we want.** Together they constitute a Role-A diagnosis without explicitly being one — a meta-analysis showing the flagship explanatory framework (POLS) doesn't have its claimed empirical support, plus a review-level synthesis confirming that finding has been absorbed by the field. # **RUN 2 – Sociology and Social Science: Field Reconnaissance on Description, Prediction, and Explanation**

## **Scope note**

This is Run 2 of a 4-run reconnaissance, not a comprehensive bibliography. I focus on field-level, constructive, methodologically substantive pieces (priority 2018–present, with older anchors only when they continue to structure live debate). I have prioritized verifying primary claims of the central pieces against published text (especially the Salganik et al. 2020 result, where the literature's penumbra exceeds the actual finding).

---

## **1\. Primary candidates**

### **Hofman, J. M., Watts, D. J., Athey, S., Garip, F., Griffiths, T. L., Kleinberg, J., Margetts, H., Mullainathan, S., Salganik, M. J., Vazire, S., Vespignani, A., & Yarkoni, T. (2021). Integrating explanation and prediction in computational social science. *Nature*, 595(7866), 181–188. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-021-03659-0**

**Roles: A (theory crisis diagnosis), D (mechanism over actuarial prediction), and partial C (formal apparatus).** This Perspective is the closest thing the computational social science wing has to a programmatic statement on the prediction–explanation question. Its core move is a two-dimensional schema that distinguishes work along two orthogonal axes — extent of explanatory ambition (identifying and estimating causal effects) and extent to which work is held to predictive standards (out-of-sample testing of outcomes) — and then locates pure description, pure prediction, pure explanation, and what the authors call "integrative modelling" within that space. The constructive claim is that the field has been treating prediction and explanation as competitors when they are complements: a good causal account should make non-trivial out-of-sample predictions, and pure-prediction tools (machine-learning pipelines) without explanatory structure routinely fail to generalize. The piece is field-level because its author list is a deliberate cross-section of computational social scientists, sociologists, statisticians, and computer scientists; it is constructive because it offers practical desiderata (transparency about whether a study's claim is explanatory or predictive, holding out test data, pre-registration of explanatory predictions) rather than only critique.

### **Salganik, M. J., Lundberg, I., Kindel, A. T., Ahearn, C. E., Al-Ghoneim, K., Almaatouq, A., et al. (2020). Measuring the predictability of life outcomes with a scientific mass collaboration. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 117(15), 8398–8403. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1915006117**

**Roles: B (phenomenon audit) and D (mechanism over actuarial prediction).** The Fragile Families Challenge is the single most consequential phenomenon audit in recent quantitative sociology. 160 teams, using machine-learning methods on the rich Fragile Families and Child Wellbeing Study (waves 1–5, child age 0–9), competed to predict six outcomes at age 15 (GPA, grit, eviction, material hardship, caregiver layoff, caregiver job training). The headline empirical finding — and this is where care is needed against the literature's broader interpretive penumbra — is narrower than often reported. The paper does **not** show that life outcomes are unpredictable in some absolute sense; it shows that even the best of 160 teams performed only modestly better than a four-variable benchmark OLS model, and that predictive accuracy for continuous outcomes such as GPA was bounded around R² ≈ 0.2 in held-out data, with binary outcomes performing worse. The constructive methodological reading the authors themselves endorse is that this gap between rich data \+ flexible models and modest accuracy implies meaningful unpredictability in life trajectories (an irreducible-error component, not just methodological failure), which has implications both for actuarial uses of such models and for sociological theories that imply outcomes should be more predictable than they are. The piece is field-level because the common-task structure forces every sociological theory of life outcomes to confront the same out-of-sample benchmark, and because the result has been actively absorbed into both analytical sociology and CSS debates. **Verification flag**: many secondary citations claim the paper shows "ML doesn't beat regression" or "life outcomes are unpredictable"; the precise claim is that under this design, with these data and this prediction horizon, gains over a sparse benchmark were small and absolute accuracy was modest.

### **Lundberg, I., Johnson, R., & Stewart, B. M. (2021). What is your estimand? Defining the target quantity connects statistical evidence to theory. *American Sociological Review*, 86(3), 532–565. DOI: 10.1177/00031224211004187**

**Role: C (formal apparatus for distinguishing types of claim), with strong A overtones.** This is the most influential recent piece supplying sociology with a shared vocabulary for what kind of claim a study is making. The argument is that quantitative sociology routinely conflates the *theoretical estimand* (the population-level quantity an investigator actually cares about, defined outside any model — e.g., "the average effect of attending a charter school on the conditional expectation of test scores for students who would have attended a traditional public school"), the *empirical estimand* (the quantity a research design can identify from observable data, given assumptions), and the *estimator* (the procedure used to compute a number from a sample). The constructive contribution is a three-step framework — set theoretical estimand, link to empirical estimand, learn from data — that forces investigators to surface assumptions, makes the gap between description and causal explanation explicit, and recasts methodological disputes as disputes about which estimand is being targeted. The piece is field-level because it speaks to all of quantitative sociology, won the 2022 ASA Methodology Section Outstanding Publication Award, and has rapidly become a teaching and reviewing reference. It is the strongest current entry under Role C, and arguably more influential within sociology than older counterfactual-framework treatments (Morgan & Winship) have been in the past five years.

### **Hedström, P., & Ylikoski, P. (2010). Causal mechanisms in the social sciences. *Annual Review of Sociology*, 36, 49–67. DOI: 10.1146/annurev.soc.012809.102632**

**Role: C (formal apparatus), and A (theory crisis diagnosis) as a foundational anchor.** Included as the canonical reference because the analytical-sociology mechanism program has not produced a successor of equal stature in the last seven years. This Annual Review distinguishes mechanism-based explanation from covering-law and from variable-based explanation, formalizes the Coleman-boat macro→micro→micro→macro structure, articulates Merton's middle-range program in modern terms, and ties mechanism-based explanation to agent-based modelling and to a broader account of causation as a generative process. It remains the standard citation in sociology for "what is a mechanism" and is still actively shaping the analytical-sociology, computational-social-science, and qualitative-mechanism literatures. The piece is field-level because it both diagnoses the field's reliance on covering-law / variable-based explanations as inadequate and offers a constructive program; it remains live because subsequent work in CSS (Hofman et al. 2021\) and in cultural and stratification sociology continues to position itself relative to it.

## **2\. Domain assessment**

**Does sociology have a coherent self-critical methodological literature? Yes — but it is fragmented, not unified.** Sociology is in fact stronger than personality psychology on methodological self-critique going back at least to Boudon, Stinchcombe, Coleman, Merton, and the 1998 Hedström–Swedberg *Social Mechanisms* volume. The analytical-sociology program (Hedström, Ylikoski, Manzo, Demeulenaere, Goldthorpe in his "population science" mode) constitutes a 25+ year coherent research program with explicit mechanism-based-explanation commitments. But the live methodological-self-critique literature is currently fragmented across **at least four schools** that engage with the same phenomena from different vocabularies and only partly talk to each other:

1. **Analytical sociology / mechanism-based explanation** (Hedström, Ylikoski, Manzo, Demeulenaere; institutionally anchored at Linköping's Institute for Analytical Sociology and the European Academy of Sociology). Strong on Role C, weaker on Role B.  
2. **Computational social science** (Watts, Salganik, Lazer, Hofman, Kleinberg, Mullainathan; Princeton, Penn CSSL, Northeastern NULab, MIT). Strong on Roles B and D; converging on Role A.  
3. **Causal-inference sociology** (Lundberg, Johnson, Stewart, Elwert, Winship, Morgan, Gangl). Strong on Role C, methodologically rich but, as the prompt anticipates, sometimes "explanatorily thin" — heavily focused on identification of effects rather than on what would constitute a mechanistic account of why those effects exist.  
4. **Qualitative/cultural and process-oriented sociology** (Small, Calarco, Vaisey, Abbott, Bearman). Strong on Role D in its sequence/process variant and on the qualitative-evidence-for-mechanism question, but mostly absent from the formal apparatus debate.

**Are analytical sociology and computational social science talking to each other?** Increasingly — but not yet fully. The Linköping group (Keuschnigg, Lovsjö & Hedström 2018 *J. Comput. Soc. Sci.*; Jarvis, Keuschnigg & Hedström 2021 in Engel et al. *Handbook of Computational Social Science*) has explicitly argued that computational tools fit the analytical-sociology program (mechanism \+ emergence \+ agent-based modelling). Hedström's "Coda" in Manzo's 2021 *Research Handbook on Analytical Sociology* directly engages CSS prediction–explanation debates. Conversely, the Hofman et al. 2021 *Nature* piece includes Filiz Garip and Salganik (sociologists) as co-authors. But the two literatures still cite different canons; analytical-sociology pieces lean on Coleman, Boudon, and Elster, while CSS pieces lean on Breiman, Donoho, and Shmueli. A definitive synthesis has not yet appeared.

**Has the CSS prediction-vs-explanation debate produced a definitive synthesis?** Not yet. Hofman et al. 2021 is the most ambitious attempt, but it is a position paper rather than a worked-through methodology. The Salganik et al. 2020 phenomenon audit established the empirical anchor; Lundberg et al. 2024 PNAS *origins of unpredictability* paper begins the explanatory follow-up; but the field is still mid-debate on how unpredictability bounds should constrain explanatory theory.

**Is the analytical-sociology literature showing its age?** Partly yes. Hedström & Ylikoski 2010 remains canonical because there has been no Annual Review-level successor consolidating mechanism-based explanation in light of the post-2015 causal inference and ML waves. Manzo's 2021 *Research Handbook on Analytical Sociology* and Gërxhani et al.'s 2022 *Handbook of Sociological Science* are the closest substitutes, but neither has had the disciplinary-shaping impact of the 2010 review.

**Is causal-inference-in-sociology methodologically rich but explanatorily thin?** Largely yes — and Lundberg, Johnson & Stewart 2021 is significant precisely because it tries to bridge that gap by forcing the estimand back into theoretical territory, but it remains a vocabulary, not a substantive theory of mechanisms.

**Is cultural sociology's mechanism debate fragmented?** Yes — Vaisey, Bail, Mohr, DiMaggio, and Gross all push different versions, and there is no current field-level consolidating piece comparable to what Hedström & Ylikoski offered for analytical sociology.

### **Watts, D. J. (2014). Common sense and sociological explanations. *American Journal of Sociology*, 120(2), 313–351. DOI: 10.1086/678271**

Foundational anchor for Role A and Role D. Argues sociologists routinely confuse *understandability* (verstehen-style accounts that "make sense" of action) with *causal validity*, and proposes that sociological explanations should be evaluated against out-of-sample predictive performance. Field-level programmatic book arguing that sociology's proper task is establishing *probabilistic population regularities* as descriptive findings, then explaining them via mechanisms reducible to (boundedly rational) action. Roles A and C. Useful as a counterpoint to mechanism-only and to causal-inference-only positions, though six years out it is showing its age. ## **4\. Honest gap-flagging**

**Gap 1: No contemporary successor to Hedström & Ylikoski 2010 at *Annual Review of Sociology* level.** The mechanism-based-explanation literature has developed substantially since 2010 (incorporating ML, agent-based modelling at scale, the Fragile Families results, causal DAGs, the estimand debate), but no single recent piece has consolidated these advances in an Annual Review-stature article. Closest near-misses: the Hedström "Coda" (2021, Manzo Handbook chapter) and Jarvis, Keuschnigg & Hedström (2021, Handbook of Computational Social Science chapter on "Analytical sociology amidst a computational social science revolution"). Both are handbook chapters, not field-level programmatic articles, and both are partial.

**Gap 2: No definitive synthesis of the prediction-vs-explanation debate.** Hofman et al. 2021 *Nature* is the most ambitious attempt and is an excellent position paper, but it remains schematic rather than a worked-through methodology with case-study demonstrations of integrative modelling actually producing new theoretical insight. The Salganik et al. 2020 → Lundberg et al. 2024 line is the closest empirical demonstration but is one extended case rather than a synthetic program.

**Gap 3: Cultural sociology has no field-level mechanism manifesto comparable to what analytical sociology has produced.** The Vaisey "dual-process" line, Bail's computational cultural sociology, Mohr's text-as-data work, Gross's pragmatist mechanisms — none has produced a field-level consolidating statement that culturally-oriented sociologists across schools cite as the agenda. Closest near-miss: Gross, N. (2009). A pragmatist theory of social mechanisms. *American Sociological Review*, 74(3), 358–379 — but this is now 17 years old and has not been substantially updated.

**Gap 4: Causal-inference sociology is methodologically rich but explanatorily thin.** Lundberg, Johnson & Stewart 2021 is the strongest recent attempt to push the estimand back into theoretical territory. But there is no current piece that combines DAG-based / potential-outcomes formalism with mechanism-based explanation in a way that satisfies both the analytical-sociology and the causal-inference camps. Elwert & Winship 2014 (ARS, on collider bias) and Morgan & Winship 2014 (*Counterfactuals and Causal Inference*, 2nd ed., Cambridge) remain the standard references but are now over a decade old.

**Gap 5: The defenders of prediction-oriented or descriptive sociology are mostly speaking through commentaries and in scattered methodological notes rather than through a field-level constructive piece.** Garip's 2020 PNAS commentary (DOI 10.1073/pnas.2003390117) is the cleanest example of this: it accepts the Salganik et al. 2020 result but argues that prediction failure does not invalidate the standard inferential approach, and that aggregate description and causal estimation should continue alongside prediction. There is no current monograph-length defense of descriptive sociology against mechanism-based imperialism. Andrew Abbott's older work (*Time Matters*, 2001; *Processual Sociology*, 2016\) sits in this defensive position from a process-sociology angle but predates the current debate. **Watts's own evolution** is itself worth noting as a complication: his 2014 AJS piece argued for prediction as the test of explanation, but his 2021 *Nature* co-authored piece argues for *integrating* explanation and prediction — a softening that suggests even the most prominent CSS voices are not committed to a pure-prediction position.

**Gap 6: No mainstream sociology piece has yet absorbed Manski's partial-identification framework into the explanation-vs-description debate.** This is a structural gap; partial identification is a natural fit for sociology's typical observational-data situation, but its uptake has been slower in sociology than in econometrics, and no field-level piece has positioned it within the mechanism-based-explanation conversation.

## **Verification flags**

* **Salganik et al. 2020**: claims about "unpredictability of life outcomes" in the secondary literature often outrun the paper's actual argument. The paper documents that 160 teams, on these specific outcomes with this specific design, performed only modestly better than a four-variable benchmark; absolute predictive accuracy was bounded (e.g., R² around 0.2 for GPA in held-out data). The 2024 Lundberg et al. follow-up offers a more developed account of *why* prediction failed.

# **RUN 3 — Technology Studies (Broadly Construed): Field Reconnaissance on Description-vs-Mechanism Methodological Self-Critique**

## **1\. Primary Candidates**

### **1A. Geels, F. W. (2022). Causality and explanation in socio-technical transitions research: Mobilising epistemological insights from the wider social sciences.**

*Research Policy*, 51(6), 104537\. DOI: 10.1016/j.respol.2022.104537

**Roles performed: A (theory crisis diagnosis), B (phenomenon/framework audit), C (formal apparatus for distinguishing types of claim).**

This is arguably the single most direct field-level methodological essay in technology studies in the past five years on the description-vs-explanation axis, written by the architect of the Multi-Level Perspective himself in response to a wave of critiques (Genus & Coles 2008; Smith, Stirling & Berkhout 2010; Sorrell 2018; Svensson & Nikoleris 2018\) that the MLP has functioned as a heuristic taxonomy rather than a genuinely explanatory theory. Geels concedes that "mainstream explanatory formats" are inadequate for transitions and explicitly works through three philosophy-of-science positions (positivism, pragmatism, critical realism), settling on critical realism as the appropriate epistemological frame. He then unpacks "complex causalities" — conjunctural, configurational, and event-chain causality — and argues that MLP, Technological Innovation Systems (TIS), and Strategic Niche Management (SNM) should be re-grounded in causal-mechanism, process-tracing, and narrative-explanation methods imported from political science and sociology (drawing on Bennett & Checkel, Mahoney, Beach & Pedersen). The piece is field-level because it explicitly addresses the explanatory adequacy of the dominant frameworks of an entire subfield (sociotechnical transitions research), not a single empirical case. It is also notable because it concedes ground to critics: Geels effectively acknowledges that the field has produced typologies and pathway descriptions in lieu of mechanism, and proposes a constructive program for repair.

### **1B. Russell, A. L. & Vinsel, L. (2018). After Innovation, Turn to Maintenance.**

*Technology and Culture*, 59(1), 1–25. DOI: 10.1353/tech.2018.0004

**Roles performed: A (theory crisis diagnosis), B (phenomenon audit — auditing the empirical centrality of "innovation" as the field's master variable).**

A field-level intervention in the history and sociology of technology that diagnoses the field's — and adjacent innovation studies' — fixation on novelty, breakthrough, and discrete inventive events as the engine of technological change. Russell and Vinsel argue that this framing has produced descriptive and celebratory case studies of "innovation moments" while leaving the much larger phenomenon of how technologies actually persist, decay, fail, are repaired, and accumulate infrastructural debt almost entirely untheorized. Their constructive proposal is the "maintenance turn": shifting the unit of analysis from the inventive event to the longitudinal process of upkeep, which they argue forces theorization of mechanisms (deferred maintenance, technical debt, care labor, institutional reproduction) that the innovation paradigm cannot see. This is field-level because it explicitly indicts a research paradigm and proposes an alternative organizing question for SHOT and adjacent communities; it has shaped a substantial subsequent literature (Henke & Sims, Jackson's "broken world thinking," Mattern, Denis & Pontille). Note: it is more diagnostic than mechanistic in the sense Roles C/D demand — it does not propose formal apparatus — but it is constructive in tone and explicitly methodological-meta.

### **1C. Park, M., Leahey, E. & Funk, R. J. (2023). Papers and patents are becoming less disruptive over time.**

*Nature*, 613(7942), 138–144. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-022-05543-x

**Roles performed: B (phenomenon audit at scale), D (call for mechanism over actuarial prediction — implicit).**

Using the Funk & Owen-Smith CD index across 45 million papers and 3.9 million patents over six decades, Park, Leahey & Funk document a near-universal secular decline in the "disruptiveness" of scientific papers and patents and link it tentatively to a narrowing of the knowledge base on which new work draws. Within the present taxonomy this functions as a phenomenon audit: it offers an empirical regularity at a scale that should constrain any theory of technological change, and it implicitly demands that the field move beyond descriptive S-curve and diffusion models to mechanism-level accounts of why combinatorial inventive capacity might be slowing.

**CITATION INTEGRITY FLAG — IMPORTANT.** The headline empirical finding is contested by at least three substantive published critiques, and any use of this paper in a positioning document should explicitly note the controversy:

* Petersen, Arroyave & Pammolli (2024, *Journal of Informetrics*; arXiv 2306.01949 / 2406.15311) argue that the CD index is biased by citation inflation and reference-list length growth, so the temporal decline is largely a measurement artifact.  
* Macher, Rutzer & Weder (2024, *Research Policy* 53(5), 104992; DOI: 10.1016/j.respol.2024.104992) show that approximately 88% of the reported decline in disruptive patents over 1980–2010 is explained by truncation of backward citations before 1976 and exclusion of patent applications, and that correcting for these biases yields no decline (and in some technology classes an increase) in highly disruptive patents.  
* Holst, Algaba, Tori, Wodtke, Gusev (HATWG; arXiv 2402.14583) attribute the decline to zero-backward-citation works.  
* Park, Leahey & Funk have replied (arXiv 2503.00184) defending the result under HATWG's preferred specifications, but the patent-side decline in particular is significantly less robust than the headline claim suggests.

The paper still belongs in this dossier because it has reframed how the science-of-science / technology-studies community discusses progress, but as a *contested* phenomenon claim rather than a settled empirical regularity. Its methodological-meta significance — that we should be auditing aggregate technological progress through citation-network mechanisms rather than through forecasting — survives the empirical dispute.

### **1D. Funk, R. J. & Owen-Smith, J. (2017). A Dynamic Network Measure of Technological Change.**

*Management Science*, 63(3), 791–817. DOI: 10.1287/mnsc.2015.2366

**Roles performed: C (formal apparatus for distinguishing types of claim), D (call for mechanism over actuarial prediction).**

The originating methodological piece behind the CD index. Its argument is explicitly that the patent-and-citation literature has overrelied on forward-citation-count "impact" measures, which conflate magnitude of subsequent attention with the qualitatively different question of whether an invention *consolidates* or *destabilizes* the technological lineage on which it builds. The authors propose a dynamic network framework that distinguishes the *direction* of an invention's effect on its predecessors (does it crowd out or amplify use of prior art?) from its sheer quantity of follow-on use, and validate the measure across U.S. patents, university patenting, and case studies. It is field-level because it is offered as a general methodological apparatus for the patent-mining/science-of-science wing of technology studies, and its central claim is precisely that better measurement constrains better mechanism (the introduction explicitly flags the "limitation" of citation-count-based reasoning for capturing "conceptually important phenomena"). This is the cleanest Role-C/Role-D candidate in the dossier.

## **2\. Domain Assessment**

**Is technology studies a unified methodological-self-critique conversation? No.** The field is genuinely fragmented across at least four distinct conversations that barely cite one another:

1. **STS proper (ANT, SCOT, infrastructure studies)** is methodologically anti-mechanistic in a deep sense. ANT in particular treats "explanation" as the wrong vocabulary: Latour repeatedly insisted that ANT does not "explain actors' behaviour and reasons" but only traces associations, and the principle of generalized symmetry rules out the kind of mechanism-based explanation Roles A and C presuppose. SCOT's "interpretive flexibility / closure / stabilization" framework is officially agnostic about underlying mechanisms (see Bijker & Pinch's own "ontological agnosticism," reaffirmed in the 2012 anniversary edition and in third-decade retrospectives such as Sovacool & Hess on the SCOT methodological conundrum, Social Epistemology 2022). The dominant self-critical conversation inside STS in recent years has been about post-truth, decolonization, and engagement (Sismondo 2017 "Post-truth?" *Social Studies of Science* 47(1), DOI: 10.1177/0306312717692076; Lynch 2017; Collins, Evans & Weinel 2017; the *ESTS* exchange) — *not* about whether STS produces explanatory mechanisms. **There is no constructive synthesis between mainstream STS and mechanism-based explanation; the field is at an impasse on this axis, and the requesting party should expect mainstream STS voices to actively resist the mechanism-based framing.**

2. **Sociotechnical-transitions / innovation studies (the Geels camp and its critics)** is the *one* conversation that genuinely has the explanation-vs-description debate as a central methodological theme. Sorrell (2018, *Research Policy* 47(7), 1267–1282; "Explaining sociotechnical transitions: A critical realist perspective"), Svensson & Nikoleris (2018, *Research Policy* 47(2), 462–473), and Geels (2022) constitute a coherent critical-realist intervention pushing transitions research toward causal mechanism, process tracing, and configurational causality. This sub-literature is the strongest single candidate exemplar of Role A and Role C inside technology studies broadly construed.

3. **Evolutionary economics of technology (Arthur, Funk, Owen-Smith, Park-Leahey-Funk, the Wuyts/Wang/Evans cluster)** has the strongest Role-C and Role-D candidates by a wide margin — formal apparatus for distinguishing types of claim, large-N mechanism extraction from patent/citation networks, and explicit critique of forecasting as actuarial. Brian Arthur's *The Nature of Technology* (2009) remains the canonical anchor for combinatorial-evolution mechanism claims; there has been no comparable book-length successor, though Arthur has continued to publish on the structure of invention.

4. **Maintenance studies (Russell, Vinsel, Jackson, Henke, Sims, Mattern, Denis & Pontille)** is a meta-critique of the innovation paradigm but offers diagnosis more than formal mechanism.

5. **Philosophy of technology / postphenomenology** has produced almost no field-level methodological-self-critique on the description-vs-explanation axis in the last 5–7 years. The closest item is the Aagaard, Friis, Sorenson, Tafdrup & Hasse edited volume *Postphenomenological Methodologies* (Lexington 2018), which is methodological but inward-looking and does not engage explanation/mechanism debates. The Houkes & Vermaas ICE-theory program in philosophy of engineering is constructive but not framed as a reform of how the field theorizes — be honest that this domain does not have a strong recent Role-A piece.

6. **Critical algorithm studies (Seaver 2017 "Algorithms as culture," *Big Data & Society* 4(2), DOI: 10.1177/2053951717738104; Burrell, Christin, Ziewitz)** is, like ANT, methodologically *anti-mechanistic by design* — Seaver's central move is to recast algorithms as enacted multiples and reject mechanism-talk as a category mistake. This is the conversation a mechanism-based reformulation has to argue *against*, not with.

**Role coverage summary:**

* Role A: Best represented in the transitions critique (Sorrell, Svensson-Nikoleris, Geels 2022\) and in maintenance studies (Russell-Vinsel).  
* Role B: Park-Leahey-Funk 2023 is the headline phenomenon audit; Geels 2022 audits MLP frameworks; the Liebowitz-Margolis path-dependence audits remain canonical but pre-2018.  
* Role C: Strongest in evolutionary economics of technology (Arthur 2009; Funk & Owen-Smith 2017; Geels 2022).  
* Role D: Strongest outside STS proper, in the science-of-science / patent-mining wing (Funk & Owen-Smith 2017; Park-Leahey-Funk 2023). STS proper is anti-predictive but also anti-mechanistic, so Role D maps awkwardly.

# **RUN 4 – AI Safety, Alignment, and Mechanistic Interpretability: From Description to Mechanism**

## **Orientation**

This domain is unusual within the four-run program: the move "from description to mechanism" is not a quiet methodological subtext but the field's *explicit* self-marketing. The Anthropic / Distill / Transformer Circuits tradition was founded on the claim that interpretability should reverse-engineer mechanisms rather than describe behavior; the alignment tradition argues for mechanism-level access on the grounds that behavioral training cannot rule out deceptive alignment. Roles A (theory-crisis diagnosis) and D (mechanism-over-actuarial) are therefore heavily populated. Role C (formal apparatus) is also unusually well-developed because of the academic causal-abstraction tradition. Role B (phenomenon audit) is the *least* mature: the SAE-evaluation literature is real but recent and fragmented, and the field's most prominent methodological self-critiques remain at the level of essays and surveys rather than systematic audits.

A second peculiarity: the most influential pieces here are not peer-reviewed conference papers. Distill articles, Transformer Circuits Thread reports, Anthropic technical reports, and Alignment Forum essays by recognized voices (Olah, Nanda, Hubinger, Greenblatt) play a load-bearing role. I have flagged venue type explicitly throughout.

## **1\. Primary Candidates (5)**

### **A. Sharkey, L., Chughtai, B., Batson, J., Lindsey, J., Wu, J., Bushnaq, L., Goldowsky-Dill, N., Heimersheim, S., Ortega, A., Bloom, J., Biderman, S., Garriga-Alonso, A., Conmy, A., Nanda, N., Rumbelow, J., Wattenberg, M., Schoots, N., Miller, J., Saunders, W., Michaud, E. **Roles: A, B, D.** Venue: arXiv preprint → accepted at TMLR (peer-reviewed journal-equivalent).

This is the closest thing the field currently has to a consensus, multi-institution, field-level methodological audit. The 29-author lineup spans Apollo Research, Anthropic, DeepMind, OpenAI, MIT, Northeastern (Bau lab), Stanford (Geiger), Harvard, and the singular learning theory community (Murfet, Hoogland), which is itself notable as an inclusion gesture across the academic / industry divide. The core argument is that mechanistic interpretability, despite producing many demonstrations, has *not* established the conceptual or empirical foundations needed for its scientific or safety promises to be cashed out: methods lack validation, "conflating hypotheses with conclusions has regrettably been commonplace," interpretability illusions recur, different methods yield contradictory interpretations of the same phenomenon, and the field lacks an agreed model organism (a "Drosophila of mechanistic interpretability"). The paper proposes a constructive program: reverse-engineering, model architecture interventions for interpretability-by-design, theoretical foundations, validation standards, and clearer ties to downstream safety/scientific goals. This is the field-level Role-A piece for mechanistic interpretability circa 2025, and because it explicitly catalogs which empirical phenomena are robust (induction heads, linearity in some forms) versus which are not (SAE latents as "true features," circuit faithfulness across methods), it also performs Role B.

The piece is constructive — it provides a taxonomy of access types and concrete proposals for how white-box audits should be structured, including legal and security safeguards. **Role: C.** Venue: peer-reviewed journal (JMLR), originally arXiv 2023\.

I verified the methodological claims directly against the paper's own framing, because public commentary has occasionally overreached. ## **2\. Domain Assessment**

**Is mechanistic interpretability's self-presentation as "the mechanistic alternative to behavioral safety" borne out by the field's internal critical literature?** Partially, but with substantial internal pushback that has matured rapidly in 2024–2025. The Sharkey et al. (2025) "Open Problems" review is the single most important piece of evidence here: an unusually broad coalition explicitly conceding that the field has produced demonstrations rather than validated mechanistic theories, that interpretability illusions recur, and that "conflating hypotheses with conclusions has regrettably been commonplace." Saphra & Wiegreffe's "Mechanistic?" (2024 BlackboxNLP, peer-reviewed at ACL workshop) goes further, arguing that "mechanistic interpretability" is partly a cultural label whose technical content is not reliably distinguished from earlier NLP-interpretability work, and that the cultural movement has tended to underrate prior critical literature. Casper has been the most consistent internal skeptic across multiple venues — his 2023 SaTML survey (Räuker et al.) and the 2024 black-box-audits paper both argue from inside the safety community that current interpretability methods do not yet deliver auditable assurances. So the self-presentation is *contested from within*, and there is now a genuine methodological-self-critique conversation, not merely promotional output. It is not yet a Kuhnian normal-science paradigm but it is no longer purely preparadigmatic — Sharkey et al. functions as something like a field-level disciplinary stocktake, even if its prescriptions remain programmatic.

**Are the academic causal-abstraction line and the empirical Anthropic / DeepMind tradition one conversation or two?** They were running mostly parallel through 2022–2023 and have been actively merging since 2024\. The merger is visible in three places: (1) Geiger et al. Large-N existing data could test this directly via multilevel modeling. **Among the strongest predictions in the set.**

**Hypothesis 6 — Scott's "legibility" describes a real configuration of administrative computing.** The triple Stability \+ Sovereignty \+ Efficiency (absent Reciprocity), born at Industries (Scott's grain-state), persists at Computers. Computational systems organized around this configuration — government data warehouses, civil registries, ID systems, surveillance infrastructure — should show structurally similar attractors to the grain-state despite different substrate. *Falsification:* if administrative computing shows attractors that don't track the grain-state's configuration, cross-regime persistence weakens.

**Hypothesis 7 — Researcher-degrees-of-freedom variation in social science findings is partly caused by level-of-analysis confusion.** A sharper version of the Breznau et al. finding. Studies that explicitly specify unit-level commitments (using Lundberg-Johnson-Stewart estimand vocabulary) should show tighter convergence than studies that don't. Testable in the Breznau dataset and similar multi-team analyses.

### **Technology studies**

**Hypothesis 8 — Technological transitions consolidate within rows and destabilize at row boundaries.** Within-row innovation (better processors, cheaper memory) consolidates the technological lineage. Row-boundary innovation (Computers→Networks: packet switching; Networks→AI/ML: backpropagation at scale; AI/ML→Agents: tool use and action loops) destabilizes the lineage. *Falsification:* if destabilizing inventions are randomly distributed across the technology lifecycle rather than concentrated at row boundaries, row-as-structural-unit fails. **Status:** flagged for circularity check — risk that "destabilizing innovations are at row boundaries because that's how we identified row boundaries."

**Hypothesis 9 — MLP-style sociotechnical-transitions theory fits within-regime transitions cleanly and strains at regime-bridging transitions.** The canonical MLP transitions (horse-to-automobile, sail-to-steam) are within-regime transitions inside Industries. ABHOC predicts these look structurally different from regime-bridging transitions (Industries→Computers; the in-progress AI/ML→Agents question), where the inheritance modality itself changes. *Falsification:* if MLP fits regime-bridging transitions as cleanly as within-regime, the regime-architecture claim weakens.

### **AI safety, alignment, and mechanistic interpretability**

**Hypothesis 10 — Large models at scale show internal representational organization consistent with Comprehension as the Row 3 attractor.** The contested question in mech-interp is whether large models do representation-mediated cognition or sophisticated lookup. ABHOC predicts the former: scaling produces models whose internal organization is genuinely representational, not models that have grown elaborate fast pattern-matching. Recent mech-interp evidence (induction heads, function vectors, in-context learning circuits) is consistent with the representational reading. *Falsification:* if interpretability work converges on accounts where large models are best characterized as fast pattern-matching without genuine internal mediation, the claim that AI/ML is Row 3 (organized interiority) rather than Row 1 or Row 2 fails.

**Hypothesis 11 (most speculative) — Agents show internal organization not reducible to AI/ML's Comprehension.** If Initiative is the Row 4 attractor genuinely coming online, agentic systems should show structural signatures distinguishable from non-agentic AI/ML at the internal-representational level — goal representations, plan structures, self-model components — not just behavioral differences. Mech-interp on agentic systems is nascent; testable over the next 3-5 years. *Falsification:* if agentic systems show internal organization that is just AI/ML plus an action API, the claim that Agents is a genuinely distinct row weakens.

## **4\. Cross-domain relationships, summarized**

ABHOC's relationship to the literature it engages is qualitatively different per domain:

* **Personality psychology \+ psychopathology:** retrodictive. ABHOC explains why the field's existing taxonomies converged on the structures they did.  
* **Animal behavior:** predictive into a gap. ABHOC supplies structural predictions where the flagship integrative framework (POLS) is underdetermined.  
* **Sociology:** agnostic across schools. ABHOC contributes structural commitments that any of the four major schools could find compatible with their apparatus.  
* **Technology studies:** selective with explicit non-engagement of opposing positions. ABHOC's natural intellectual partners are evolutionary economics and transitions critique; mainstream STS is methodologically opposed.  
* **AI safety / mech-interp:** narrow, row-specific only. Citations support the AI/ML row's positioning; do not generalize as ratification of the framework writ large.

These positioning differences are organizing principles for the citation strategy, not material to spell out at length in the paper itself. If the paper is well-written, the technology professor gets what she wants from the technology sections, the sociology professor gets what he wants from the sociology pieces, etc.

## **5\. Methodological discipline carried forward**

From the prior synthesis, items that survive into the academic work:

* **Pearl-hierarchy layer discipline.** Each prediction is explicitly marked as Layer 1 (observational), Layer 2 (interventional), or Layer 3 (counterfactual). Most ABHOC predictions are Layer 1 by design — testable on existing data without intervention. This is what makes Eronen & Bringmann's fat-handedness critique inapplicable to the cross-regime persistence prediction.

* **Estimand vocabulary (Lundberg, Johnson & Stewart 2021).** Each prediction states its theoretical estimand (the population-level quantity ABHOC claims) explicitly, separate from any model or estimator. Forces surface of assumptions; makes the gap between description and causal explanation explicit.

* **Citation hygiene.** Salganik et al. 2020 cited only for what the paper actually shows (the literature's broader interpretive penumbra is misleading). Park, Leahey & Funk 2023 cited only for methodological-meta significance with explicit dispute acknowledgment, or omitted. Hedström & Ylikoski 2010 cited alongside Hedström 2021 Coda to acknowledge the analytical-sociology program's evolution. Industry technical reports (Sleeper Agents, Transformer Circuits Thread) flagged as non-peer-reviewed venue type.

* **Felt-ness is downstream.** Motivations operate as attractors regardless of subjective experience. Dissolves the anthropomorphism objection at the genetic end and the AI-phenomenology objection at the computational end without committing to either.

* **Row 4 is constitutive risk.** Three possible fates (failure, off-thread success, bridge to next regime), distinguishable only retroactively. The framework does not speculate about bridge-eligibility for current Row 4 entities (Agents) and therefore does not speculate about a fifth regime.

## **6\. The digital column question**

**Decision: keep the digital column.** Cutting it would simplify acceptance odds at any single venue but at high cost. The column-emergence prediction loses statistical traction (n=2 transitions instead of n=3+1-in-progress). The AI/ML row's interpretability engagement (Hypotheses 10 and 11\) is the one place ABHOC does forward-looking work testable within five years. The "fifth regime speculation" objection mostly evaporates given the Row 4 constitutive-risk commitment.

**Framing move: the digital column is the framework's first forward-looking application, not its culmination.** The architecture is developed and defended across three completed regimes (genetic, neuronal, symbolic) where data is rich and the bridge pattern is observable. The digital extension is then applied with explicit acknowledgment that bridge-eligibility is methodologically undecidable.

The first sixty percent of any ABHOC scholarly artifact should be unimpeachable on disciplinary terms — dense citations to personality, ethology, and sociology literatures, the architecture defended on retrodictive grounds in domains where it has rich data — so that by the time the reader reaches the digital application, the framework has earned the right to extend itself.

## **7\. Venue strategy: book first**

**ABHOC is structurally a book.** Its closest cousins (Heyes *Cognitive Gadgets* 2018, Arthur *Nature of Technology* 2009, Bennett *Brief History of Intelligence* 2023, Maynard Smith & Szathmáry *Major Transitions* 1995\) all lived in book form because cross-substrate architectural arguments need the room. The publishing guide's current "paper first, book second" sequencing is probably backwards for this kind of work.

**Recommended sequencing:**

1. **Book** as primary scholarly artifact. Trade-academic crossover press (Princeton, Harvard, Chicago). Heyes's path (Harvard hardcover) is the cleanest precedent.

2. **2-3 journal article extractions**, each engaging *one* literature rather than a single mega-article covering all five:

   * **Personality \+ psychopathology** → *Perspectives on Psychological Science* or *Psychological Review*. Lead with Big-Five-as-configurational retrodiction and maturity-principle-as-structural prediction. \~10,000 words.  
   * **Comparative ethology** → *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* or *Behavioral Ecology*. Lead with Hypothesis 3 (syndrome-structure-by-row-slot). \~5,000-8,000 words.  
   * **Cross-regime persistence** → *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. The article that *needs* peer commentary because it asks readers from sociology, anthropology, and STS to engage simultaneously. \~12,000 words. Hypotheses 5, 6, 7\.  
3. **Technology and AI/ML domains do not get standalone articles.** They live in the book and possibly as a Research Policy or TMLR piece if a specific worked-out claim warrants it.

**On BBS specifically.** BBS's strength (peer commentary across disciplines) is also its weakness for ABHOC: the format rewards single-legible-thesis arguments with clear falsification. ABHOC's cross-domain retrodiction is hard to compress without losing what makes it interesting. BBS works well for *one specific extraction* (cross-regime persistence) but not for a comprehensive ABHOC overview.

## **Methodological commitments to articulate**

### **Capabilities creep**

Capabilities don't pop into existence at row-emergence. They grow in earlier substrates as proof-of-concept versions, then become load-bearing — the organizing move of a level — when configurational pressure makes them so. Two sub-patterns: within-column precursors (chemotaxis growing into steering; chiefdoms growing into statecraft) and off-thread precursors (plant guard cells incubating allostasis; eusocial Hymenoptera incubating norms). Symmetric to eclipsing-not-deletion: the capability side of the same observation. Has a named home in the literature — plasticity-led evolution / genetic accommodation — which dramatically lowers the risk of being read as teleological.

Next step: section in the regimes doc methodological commitments list. Cite West-Eberhard 2003, Pfennig empirical work, Uller et al. 2024 retrospective.

Origin: conversation, sparked by the Powell paper.

### **Configurational concurrency and absence**

ABHOC triples are defined by which of four foreground motivations is present and which is absent within a four-element set. They are not defined by epistemic dependence (Peirce's normative science hierarchy), methodological partition (Griffiths' three frameworks for cognition), or domain coverage. This filter has been used implicitly across multiple sessions to decline candidate triadic taxonomies. It belongs in the methodological commitments list as a stated rule.

Next step: one paragraph in the methodological commitments list. The publishing guide is the right home.

Origin: prior project work, reapplied this conversation to Griffiths and Powell.

### **Organism agency, weak form**

The framework treats units as actively configuring behavior around attractors. This commitment lives in the citations ABHOC already uses (Maturana & Varela, Sterling, Friston, Bennett) without being articulated. It should be stated explicitly using the operational sense the field has settled on: niche-construction / constructive-development plus active-inference, with directed adaptive variation explicitly ruled out.

The dividing-line filter from the Lamarck research is the boundary marker. Three filters operate together: (1) mechanism realism — concrete biochemistry passes, generic "use and disuse" doesn't; (2) direction of the arrow — environmentally induced traces pass, *adaptive* directed variation doesn't; (3) population-level versus individual-level — selection-actable variation passes, organism-generates-fit doesn't.

Felt-ness is independent of agency. ABHOC already commits to felt-ness being downstream; agency is a parallel commitment on a separate axis.

Next step: methodological commitment in the regimes doc, alongside felt-ness-downstream and measurement-theoretic-not-architectural.

Origin: Riskin surfaced the question; the Lamarck research clarified the operational version.
