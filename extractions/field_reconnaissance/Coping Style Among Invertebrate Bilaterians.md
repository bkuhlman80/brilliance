# Individual Differences in Coping Style Among Invertebrate Bilaterians: An Expert-Level Reconnaissance

## Bottom-Line Answer

Across invertebrate bilaterians, the evidence base for stable, repeatable individual differences along a vertebrate-style **proactive–reactive coping axis** — i.e., a *cluster* of correlated behavioral plus physiological traits, not merely one repeatable behavior — is **uneven, terminologically fragmented, and physiologically thin**. The primary literature documents:

- **Empirically settled** that individual-level repeatable behavioral differences exist in several lineages: octopuses and cuttlefish (Cephalopoda), hermit crabs and crayfish (Decapoda), field crickets (Orthoptera), the comb-footed spider *Anelosimus studiosus* (Araneae), honeybee scouts vs. recruits (Hymenoptera), and several ant species. Repeatability for single boldness-type behaviors typically falls in R ≈ 0.2–0.6.
- **Plausibly inferred** that these clusters resemble shy–bold/exploration–avoidance axes that are functional analogues of the vertebrate proactive–reactive axis in *Aphaenogaster senilis* (the only invertebrate study explicitly framed as "proactive–reactive syndrome" at the colony level) and in honeybee scouts (vertebrate-like neuromodulatory signatures of novelty-seeking).
- **Contested or mixed** in bumblebees (Muller et al. 2010 — short-window consistency but no multi-day repeatability), cuttlefish (Pronk et al. 2010 vs. Carere et al. 2015), and across-context generalization in dumpling squid (Sinn & Moltschaniwskyj 2005 found context-specificity, not domain-general personality).
- **Thin or untested** in gastropods, bivalves, annelids, flatworms, echinoderms, and most non-*C. elegans* nematodes. *C. elegans* shows reproducible isogenic individuality but has essentially not been tested under a coping-style framework.
- **Physiological dissociation analogous to sympathetic-vs-HPA** is **not documented anywhere** in invertebrate bilaterians as a within-population, individual-difference correlate of behavioral coping clusters. Biogenic-amine differences (octopamine, dopamine) covary with behavioral differences in honeybee scouts and have been implicated in spiders and ants, but no study reports a within-population two-axis neuroendocrine architecture mirroring sympathetic/HPA. This is the most consequential gap.

The terminology dominating the invertebrate literature is **"animal personality" / "behavioral syndrome" / "shy-bold continuum"**, not "coping style." Few authors explicitly cross-reference Koolhaas et al.'s framework; the most explicit invocation is Blight et al. (2016) in *Aphaenogaster*. Most invertebrate work measures one behavior repeatedly (boldness) and supplements it with one or two additional dimensions (activity, aggression, exploration); studies that simultaneously include behavioral plus physiological measurements on the same individuals are rare.

## Per-Paper Commentary, Organized by Lineage

### 1. Cephalopoda

**Mather, J. A., & Anderson, R. C. (1993). Personalities of octopuses (*Octopus rubescens*). *Journal of Comparative Psychology, 107*(3), 336–340.** *(Verified.)* Design: laboratory, n = 44, three contexts (alerting/opening tank lid, touching with brush, food presentation) administered 7× over 2 weeks. Analysis: PCA on behavioral frequencies; three components labeled *active engagement*, *reactivity*, *avoidance*. Substantive finding: individuals consistent across repeated sessions and across tests. Limitations: no physiological measurement, sample drawn from one population, no heritability data, terms "active/reactive" map only loosely to vertebrate proactive-reactive (the paper frames this as personality, not coping style).

**Sinn, D. L., Perrin, N. A., Mather, J. A., & Anderson, R. C. (2001). Early temperamental traits in an octopus (*Octopus bimaculoides*). *Journal of Comparative Psychology, 115*(4), 351–364.** *(Verified.)* Design: 73 lab-born octopuses observed in Week 3; 37 followed at 3 time points across first 9 weeks. PCA yielded four components (active engagement, arousal/readiness, aggression, avoidance/disinterest) explaining 53% variance. Profile analysis showed mean-level change Week 3 → Week 6 but rank-order conservation. Limited heritability signal from sib-relatedness (paternity unknown). Limitations: not raised to adulthood; ecological-fitness link absent.

**Sinn, D. L., & Moltschaniwskyj, N. A. (2005). Personality traits in dumpling squid (*Euprymna tasmanica*): Context-specific traits and their correlation with biological characteristics. *Journal of Comparative Psychology, 119*(1), 99–110.** https://doi.org/10.1037/0735-7036.119.1.99 *(Verified.)* Design: wild-caught adults, n = 97, threat and feeding contexts. Four traits identified (shy avoidance–bold aggression, activity, bury persistence, reactivity). **Key negative finding for coping-style cluster:** trait expression *did not transfer* between threat and feeding contexts — boldness was context-specific. This is a partial *failure* of cross-context generalization expected of a global proactive–reactive axis.

**Sinn, D. L., Apiolaza, L. A., & Moltschaniwskyj, N. A. (2006). Heritability and fitness-related consequences of squid personality traits. *Journal of Evolutionary Biology, 19*(5), 1437–1447.** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1420-9101.2006.01136.x *(Verified.)* Design: sire–dam crosses, lab-reared offspring. Threat-context behaviors h² ≈ 0.2–0.8; foraging-context h² ≈ 0.05–0.08. Boldness in foraging covaried with body size and explained ~21% of variation in brood hatching success; positive assortative mating by shy–bold phenotype detected. This is the strongest heritability evidence for any cephalopod personality trait and one of the strongest for any invertebrate bilaterian.

**Pronk, R., Wilson, D. R., & Harcourt, R. (2010). Pour the contents and find a new path: How octopuses interact with novel objects. *Journal of Experimental Biology, 213*(7), 1035–1041.** *(Flagged — confirm full citation/DOI; widely cited but exact title/journal should be checked against original.)* Reported weaker / less convincing cross-context consistency than Mather & Anderson and Sinn. Cited in subsequent reviews as a *mixed-evidence* data point.

**Carere, C., Grignani, G., Bonanni, R., Della Gala, M., Carlini, A., Angeletti, D., Cimmaruta, R., Nascetti, G., & Mather, J. A. (2015). Consistent individual differences in the behavioural responsiveness of adult male cuttlefish (*Sepia officinalis*). *Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 167*, 89–95.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2015.03.005 *(Verified.)* Design: adult males in three tests adapted from the octopus protocol. Three PCA components accounting for 39.6%, 15.5%, 10.3% variance. Consistency across tests for behaviors like floating, staying at bottom, fin movement; deimatic behavior more context-specific. Limitations: males only, no female data, no physiology.

**Zoratto, F., Cordeschi, G., Grignani, G., Bonanni, R., Alleva, E., Nascetti, G., Mather, J. A., & Carere, C. (2018). Variability in the "stereotyped" prey capture sequence of male cuttlefish (*Sepia officinalis*) could relate to personality differences. *Animal Cognition, 21*(6), 773–785.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-018-1209-8 *(Verified.)* Re-analyzed footage from Carere et al. (2015). Hunting tactics ("stereotyped" prey-capture sequence) varied among individuals and were individually consistent — providing a behavioral context not previously linked to personality in cephalopods.

**Borrelli, L., Polese, G., et al. (2024). Personality dimensions in the common octopus (*Octopus vulgaris*). *Behaviour, 161*(13–15), 913+.** *(Flagged — verify exact authorship; Brill listing on Behaviour vol. 161.)* Design: n = 21 captive-held individuals, three contexts (Startle, Disturbance, Foraging). Variables moderately repeatable; PCA yielded *Alertness*, *Exploration*, *Boldness*; Exploration and Boldness covaried across contexts, satisfying a behavioral-syndrome criterion. Caveat: small N for repeatability decomposition; sex unassigned; no physiological data.

**Synthesis (Cephalopoda).** Across roughly two decades, octopus and cuttlefish work has assembled the strongest invertebrate evidence for a multi-dimensional, cluster-like personality structure. The recurring components — variants of activity/exploration, avoidance/reactivity, and aggression/boldness — partially map onto proactive (aggressive, engaging) vs. reactive (avoiding, withdrawing) ends, but explicit vertebrate-coping-style framing is rare. The most consequential negative observation is the repeated finding that boldness is often *context-specific* across foraging vs. threat (Sinn & Moltschaniwskyj 2005; Pronk et al. 2010; Carere et al. 2015), which complicates claims of a single proactive–reactive axis. Heritability has been demonstrated for *Euprymna* but not formally for *Octopus* or *Sepia*. No invertebrate study in this group provides simultaneous biogenic-amine, neuropeptide, or stress-protein measurement at the individual level alongside behavior.

### 2. Decapoda (Crustacea)

**Briffa, M., Rundle, S. D., & Fryer, A. (2008). Comparing the strength of behavioural plasticity and consistency across situations: animal personalities in the hermit crab *Pagurus bernhardus*. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 275*(1640), 1305–1311.** https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2008.0025 *(Verified.)* Design: startle-response duration measured with and without predator (*Carcinus maenas*) chemical cue. Showed both significant plasticity (mean shifts under predator cue) and significant rank-order individual consistency — the foundational paper for hermit-crab personality.

**Briffa, M., & Bibost, A.-L. (2009). Effects of shell size on behavioural consistency and flexibility in hermit crabs. *Canadian Journal of Zoology, 87*(7), 597–603.** *(Flagged — verify volume/pages.)*

**Briffa, M., Bridger, D., & Biro, P. A. (2013). How does temperature affect behaviour? Multilevel analysis of plasticity, personality and predictability in hermit crabs. *Animal Behaviour, 86*(1), 47–54.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2013.04.009 *(Verified.)* Multilevel modeling of repeated startle responses showed individual differences in *both* mean (boldness) and *residual within-individual SD* (predictability/proteanism). This is methodologically important: it shows that variance in unpredictability itself is an individually consistent trait, which doesn't fit standard proactive–reactive framing.

**Briffa, M., & Twyman, C. (2011). Do I stand out or blend in? Conspicuousness awareness and consistent behavioural differences in hermit crabs. *Biology Letters, 7*(3), 330–332.** *(Flagged.)*

**Bridger, D., Bonner, S. J., & Briffa, M. (2015). Individual quality and personality: Bolder males are less fecund in the hermit crab *Pagurus bernhardus*. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 282*(1803), 20142492.** https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2014.2492 *(Verified.)* Boldness-fecundity tradeoff documented: bolder males produced fewer offspring. One of the few invertebrate studies with direct fitness consequences of a coping-style-relevant trait.

**Briffa, M. (2013). Plastic proteans: reduced predictability in the face of predation risk in hermit crabs. *Biology Letters, 9*(5), 20130592.** https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2013.0592 *(Verified.)* Within-individual residual SD (riSD) of startle durations increased under predator cue — i.e., predictability itself decreased — a finding orthogonal to (and inconsistent with) the simple expectation that bold/proactive individuals are *more* routine-bound.

**Velasque, M., & Briffa, M. (2016). The opposite effects of routine metabolic rate and metabolic rate during startle responses on variation in the predictability of behaviour in hermit crabs. *Behaviour, 153*(13–14), 1545–1566.** *(Flagged.)* Did not find a simple metabolic-rate–boldness coupling. This is the closest hermit-crab analogue to a physiology-personality test and **failed to find the expected coupling**, an important null result.

**Briffa, M., & Archer, R. (2023). Size specific boldness associated with differences in resource requirements and habitat use: A cross-sectional study in hermit crabs. *Current Zoology, 69*(3), 360–366.** https://doi.org/10.1093/cz/zoac049 *(Verified.)* Cross-sectional; demonstrates ontogenetic shifts in mean boldness across size classes.

**Vainikka, A., Rantala, M. J., Niemelä, P., Hirvonen, H., & Kortet, R. (2011). Boldness as a consistent personality trait in the noble crayfish, *Astacus astacus*. *Acta Ethologica, 14*(1), 17–25.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10211-010-0086-1 *(Verified.)* Predator-naïve juveniles, repeated shelter-use assays under predator chemical cue, conspecific presence, and control. Individuals repeatable across time and context. Notably, *boldness did not predict resource-holding potential positively*, complicating a simple "proactive = dominant" mapping.

**Su, H., et al. (2024). Effect of population density on personality of crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*). *Animals, 14*(10), 1486.** https://doi.org/10.3390/ani14101486 *(Verified.)* Design: n = 166 juveniles reared at three population densities; shyness, exploration, aggression assayed at sexual maturity. Each behavior repeatable in nearly every treatment (exception: female shyness at medium density). Shyness ↔ exploration negatively correlated in all treatments (behavioral syndrome present); aggression × exploration positive correlation only in medium/high density females. **Crayfish at higher density were bolder, more exploratory, more aggressive — direct evidence of a developmental driver shaping adult coping-style-relevant traits.** Sex differences not pronounced.

**Synthesis (Decapoda).** Hermit crabs (*Pagurus bernhardus*) constitute the most thoroughly developed invertebrate model for behavioral repeatability under stress: the Briffa lab has published a tightly integrated decade-plus program on startle-response duration, plasticity, predictability, and fitness correlates. However, the *cluster*-criterion is mostly met by a single trait (boldness) plus its plasticity statistics, not by multiple semi-independent behavioral dimensions. The most ambitious physiology test (Velasque & Briffa 2016) returned a null. Crayfish work establishes the syndrome more clearly (shy–bold + aggression–exploration covariation) and adds developmental causation (Su et al. 2024), with the Vainikka et al. (2011) finding that boldness does not equate to resource-holding potential providing a useful brake on simple proactive=dominant mapping.

### 3. Insecta — Social

**Liang, Z. S., Nguyen, T., Mattila, H. R., Rodriguez-Zas, S. L., Seeley, T. D., & Robinson, G. E. (2012). Molecular determinants of scouting behavior in honey bees. *Science, 335*(6073), 1225–1228.** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1213962 *(Verified.)* Honeybee foragers consistent across nest-scouting and food-scouting contexts (a within-individual cross-context test for novelty-seeking). Brain-transcriptome differences in catecholamine, glutamate, and GABA signaling. Pharmacological manipulation: octopamine and glutamate ↑ scouting; dopamine antagonist ↓ scouting. **One of the few invertebrate papers establishing both behavioral consistency across contexts and a neuromodulatory signature** — and the only one to draw an explicit functional analogy to vertebrate novelty-seeking. Caveats: not framed as proactive–reactive coping; HPA-analogue (corticotropin/ecdysteroid) physiology not measured.

**Cook, C. N., Brent, C. S., & Breed, M. D. (2019). Individual differences in learning and biogenic amine levels influence the behavioural division between foraging honeybee scouts and recruits. *Journal of Animal Ecology, 88*(2), 236–246.** https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2656.12911 *(Verified.)* Scouts had higher octopamine than recruits after odor conditioning; latent-inhibition differences. Strengthens the biogenic-amine basis for scout/recruit individuality.

**Blight, O., Albet Díaz-Mariblanca, G., Cerdá, X., & Boulay, R. (2016). A proactive–reactive syndrome affects group success in an ant species. *Behavioral Ecology, 27*(1), 118–125.** https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arv127 *(Verified.)* **The only invertebrate paper that explicitly names a "proactive–reactive syndrome."** Captive *Aphaenogaster senilis* colonies showed colony-level consistent differences in exploration, boldness, brood rescue. Proactive colonies were more successful in intraspecific competition. Limitation: the axis is operationalized at colony (not worker) level — this is a critical scope distinction the user should note.

**Blight, O., Villalta, I., Cerdá, X., & Boulay, R. (2016). Personality traits are associated with colony productivity in the gypsy ant *Aphaenogaster senilis*. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology, 70*(12), 2203–2209.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-016-2224-x *(Verified.)* Colony-level personality–productivity link, expanding the same dataset.

**Modlmeier, A. P., Liebmann, J. E., & Foitzik, S. (2012). Diverse societies are more productive: A lesson from ants. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 279*(1736), 2142–2150.** *(Flagged.)* Within-colony behavioral diversity among workers of *Temnothorax longispinosus* associated with colony performance.

**Bengston, S. E., & Dornhaus, A. (2014). Be meek or be bold? A colony-level behavioural syndrome in ants. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 281*(1791), 20140518.** https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2014.0518 *(Verified.)* *Temnothorax rugatulus* — colony-level boldness/aggression/exploration syndrome, latitudinally variable.

**Pinter-Wollman, N., Hubler, J., Holley, J.-A., Franks, N. R., & Dornhaus, A. (2012). How is activity distributed among and within tasks in *Temnothorax* ants? *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology, 66*(10), 1407–1420.** *(Flagged.)* Documents that within-colony task specialization is partly attributable to consistent individual worker activity differences.

**Jandt, J. M., Bengston, S., Pinter-Wollman, N., Pruitt, J. N., Raine, N. E., Dornhaus, A., & Sih, A. (2014). Behavioural syndromes and social insects: Personality at multiple levels. *Biological Reviews, 89*(1), 48–67.** https://doi.org/10.1111/brv.12042 *(Verified.)* Comprehensive review (cited here for taxonomy/coverage of primary studies; not the primary source itself).

**Muller, H., Grossmann, H., & Chittka, L. (2010). 'Personality' in bumblebees: Individual consistency in responses to novel colours? *Animal Behaviour, 80*(6), 1065–1074.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2010.09.011 *(Verified.)* **Important negative finding:** *Bombus terrestris* foragers showed within-day consistency in neophilia but **not across 3 days**. The authors concluded that bumblebee foragers do *not* meet the standard personality criterion in this paradigm. A counterweight to confirmation-leaning literature.

**Synthesis (Social Insecta).** In honeybees and ants, individual-level consistent differences are well documented, with the strongest neuromodulatory evidence (octopamine, dopamine signaling) for honeybee scouts. The Blight et al. (2016) "proactive–reactive" framing is the only invertebrate use of Koolhaas-style language in the primary literature, but it is *colony-level*, not within-colony worker-level — a distinction central to your question. Within-colony worker individuality maps onto task specialization and division of labor (Pinter-Wollman; Modlmeier) but is rarely connected explicitly to coping-style physiology. Bumblebees are the clearest case where a stability criterion **fails** under a multi-day test (Muller et al. 2010). Drosophila-style honeybee aggression/defensiveness work (Hunt, Rueppell) exists but largely treats variation as genotype-level (between-colony, between-line) rather than individual-coping-style.

### 4. Insecta — Non-social

**Kortet, R., & Hedrick, A. (2007). A behavioural syndrome in the field cricket *Gryllus integer*: Intrasexual aggression is correlated with activity in a novel environment. *Biological Journal of the Linnean Society, 91*(3), 475–482.** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1095-8312.2007.00812.x *(Verified.)* Cross-context correlation of aggression and activity-in-novel-environment — the foundational cricket syndrome paper.

**Niemelä, P. T., Vainikka, A., Hedrick, A. V., & Kortet, R. (2012). Integrating behaviour with life history: Boldness of the field cricket, *Gryllus integer*, during ontogeny. *Functional Ecology, 26*(2), 450–456.** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2435.2011.01939.x *(Verified.)* Boldness rank-order repeatable across ontogeny; cautious juveniles had higher encapsulation responses as adults (pace-of-life linkage). Fast-growing individuals matured early and invested less in immunity — connects boldness to a broader life-history pace.

**Niemelä, P. T., DiRienzo, N., & Hedrick, A. V. (2012). Predator-induced changes in the boldness of naïve field crickets, *Gryllus integer*, depends on behavioural type. *Animal Behaviour, 84*(1), 129–135.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2012.04.019 *(Verified.)* Under predator treatment, the aggressiveness–boldness syndrome *broke apart* and antipredator-response repeatability was retained only in controls. An important reverse-translation insight: stress can dissolve the syndrome rather than reveal it.

**Niemelä, P. T., Vainikka, A., Lahdenperä, S., & Kortet, R. (2012). Nymphal density, behavioral development, and life history in a field cricket. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology, 66*(4), 645–652.** *(Flagged for DOI.)* Developmental density shapes adult boldness.

**Niemelä, P. T., Lattenkamp, E. Z., & Dingemanse, N. J. (2015). Personality-related survival and sampling bias in wild cricket nymphs (*Gryllus campestris*). *Behavioral Ecology, 26*(3), 936–946.** https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arv036 *(Verified.)* Wild-based estimation: flight-initiation distance predicted both daily survival and encounter probability; bolder individuals more visible but shorter-lived. The cleanest fitness-consequence demonstration for a coping-style-relevant trait in any invertebrate.

**DiRienzo, N., Niemelä, P. T., Skog, A., Vainikka, A., & Kortet, R. (2015). Juvenile pathogen exposure affects the presence of personality in adult field crickets. *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution, 3*, 36.** *(Flagged for DOI.)* Pathogen exposure during development alters whether adult crickets show personality at all — a developmental moderator of personality emergence.

**Buchanan, S. M., Kain, J. S., & de Bivort, B. L. (2015). Neuronal control of locomotor handedness in *Drosophila*. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 112*(21), 6700–6705.** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1500804112 *(Verified.)* Idiosyncratic, lifelong, *non-heritable* left/right turning bias even in isogenic flies; magnitude regulated by central-complex neurons. Methodologically a tour-de-force for invertebrate individuality but not a coping-style/stress design.

**Ayroles, J. F., Buchanan, S. M., O'Leary, C., Skutt-Kakaria, K., Grenier, J. K., Clark, A. G., Hartl, D. L., & de Bivort, B. L. (2015). Behavioral idiosyncrasy reveals genetic control of phenotypic variability. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 112*(21), 6706–6711.** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1503830112 *(Verified.)* Genetic variation in the *amount of behavioral variability* itself in *Drosophila* lines.

**Synthesis (Non-social Insecta).** Field crickets are the best-developed non-social-insect model, including the strongest wild-fitness signal in any invertebrate (Niemelä et al. 2015). Drosophila individuality work is methodologically advanced but largely orthogonal to coping-style framing — it documents that isogenic individuals diverge in idiosyncratic ways, but seldom under explicit stress contexts. The cricket literature is one of the few where developmental drivers (density, pathogen exposure) and adult repeatability have been explicitly linked, and one of the few where the **syndrome can be experimentally dissolved by stress** — a finding that complicates the assumption that coping clusters become *more* visible under challenge.

### 5. Araneae

**Pruitt, J. N., Riechert, S. E., & Jones, T. C. (2008). Behavioural syndromes and their fitness consequences in a socially polymorphic spider, *Anelosimus studiosus*. *Animal Behaviour, 76*(3), 871–879.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2008.05.009 *(Verified.)* Two populations (26°, 36°); social (cooperative) vs. asocial (aggressive) females form distinct correlated-trait clusters across response to predator, prey, superfluous killing, exploration, activity.

**Pruitt, J. N., Iturralde, G., Avilés, L., & Riechert, S. E. (2011). Within-group behavioural variation promotes biased task performance and the emergence of a defensive caste in a social spider. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology, 65*(5), 1055–1060.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-010-1112-z *(Verified.)* Asocial/aggressive individuals act as colony defenders.

**Settepani, V., Grinsted, L., Granfeldt, J., Jensen, J. L., & Bilde, T. (2019)** [also see the Pruitt-cluster genomic paper]: **Davis, M. R., Pruitt, J. N., & Avilés, L. (2019). Are personalities genetically determined? Inferences from subsocial spiders. *BMC Genomics, 20*, 867.** https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-019-6172-5 *(Verified for the genomics paper.)* ~200 individuals, ddRADseq; identified a ~1200-bp scaffold with 7 SNPs associated with boldness. First explicit genome-wide hit for an invertebrate personality trait.

**Caveat on Pruitt corpus.** Several Pruitt-authored papers have been the subject of corrections and retractions due to data-integrity concerns. Specific repeatability and fitness statistics from his single-authored or first-authored studies should be **flagged** and re-checked against erratum notices. The above papers are presented as representing the *line of enquiry*, not as unconditionally reliable individual statistics.

**Royauté, R., Buddle, C. M., & Vincent, C. (2014). Interpopulation variations in behavioral traits of *Eris militaris* (Salticidae). *Journal of Insect Behavior*, 27, 769–784.** *(Flagged.)* Jumping spider behavioral syndromes (activity, aggression, boldness, voracity); insecticide exposure disrupted syndrome integrity. Reverse-translation: stress dissolved rather than revealed the cluster.

**Synthesis (Araneae).** Spider research provides one of the strongest invertebrate cases linking *correlated* multi-trait clusters to fitness, plus the only association-mapped genomic locus for invertebrate boldness. The most important caveat is the data-integrity concern around parts of the Pruitt corpus; primary statistics should be verified case-by-case. Jumping spider work illustrates a recurring negative finding: stressors can disrupt — not strengthen — the syndrome.

### 6. Nematoda (*C. elegans*)

**Stern, S., Kirst, C., & Bargmann, C. I. (2017). Neuromodulatory control of long-term behavioral patterns and individuality across development. *Cell, 171*(7), 1649–1662.e10.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2017.10.041 *(Verified.)* Even in isogenic, identically-reared *C. elegans*, individuals develop consistent long-term behavioral biases regulated by serotonin and other neuromodulators. Demonstrates that "individuality" can be a genuine biological signal in a near-isogenic model.

**Hart, M. P., et al. (2024–2025). Mapping the developmental structure of stereotyped and individual-unique behavioral spaces in *C. elegans*. *Cell Reports.*** *(Flagged for exact citation.)* ~2,200 individuals, ~1.2 billion posture frames; quantifies population-level stereotypy and individual-unique components throughout development.

**Synthesis (Nematoda).** *C. elegans* demonstrably retains individual-level behavioral variation under near-perfect genetic and rearing standardization, and this variation is neuromodulator-regulated. **However, this work has not been framed as coping-style research.** No primary study to my knowledge tests a multi-trait cluster under standardized stressors with explicit reverse-translation from Koolhaas-style assays. Evidence tier: plausibly inferred (individuality exists); untested under the coping-style construct.

### 7. Other Lineages (Gastropoda, Bivalvia, Annelida, Platyhelminthes, Echinodermata)

The primary literature contains scattered single-behavior studies (e.g., escape responses, righting responses), but **studies meeting your inclusion criteria — multiple behavioral or behavioral-plus-physiological dimensions measured repeatedly on the same individuals from one population, with individual-level variance decomposition — are essentially absent.** This is itself the finding: these taxa represent the largest *taxonomic gap* in the literature. Gastropod work has focused on group-level behavior; bivalve and echinoderm individual-level personality work is virtually nonexistent in the indexed primary literature; earthworm and planarian work on repeatable individual differences in a stress-coping cluster has not been published to standards meeting this review's criteria.

---

## Synthesis Across the Specific Empirical Questions

**Q1 (lineage inventory).** Documented: cephalopods, decapods, several spiders, field crickets, honeybees, ants, *C. elegans* (individuality only). Largely undocumented: gastropods, bivalves, annelids, flatworms, echinoderms, most non-*C. elegans* nematodes.

**Q2 (cluster composition).** Where clusters exist, they are nearly always *behavioral only*: combinations of boldness, exploration, activity, aggression. Behavior-plus-physiology designs are rare. Exceptions: honeybee scouts (Liang 2012; Cook 2019) include brain biogenic amines; hermit-crab metabolic-rate work (Velasque & Briffa 2016) returned a null.

**Q3 (repeatability).** Reported R typically 0.2–0.6 for boldness-type traits over hours to weeks. Time intervals tend to be short (hours to days), often a methodological weakness. Confidence intervals are inconsistently reported; many earlier papers (Mather & Anderson 1993) use concordance or rank-order statistics rather than mixed-model R.

**Q4 (physiological dissociation analogue).** **No invertebrate study documents a within-population, individual-level sympathetic-vs-HPA-analogue dissociation.** Biogenic amines (octopamine, dopamine) covary with behavioral differences in honeybees and inferentially in spiders/ants; ecdysteroid, juvenile-hormone, or crustacean hyperglycemic hormone variation has not been individually linked to behavioral coping clusters. **This is the largest construct-validity gap.**

**Q5 (heritability).** Strongest evidence: *Euprymna tasmanica* (Sinn et al. 2006), h² = 0.2–0.8 for threat-context behaviors. *Anelosimus* genomic markers (Davis et al. 2019). Honeybee scouting has a transcriptomic and pharmacological basis but heritability per se has been less directly partitioned. For most species, heritability is unmeasured.

**Q6 (developmental factors).** Density effects on adult personality demonstrated in crayfish (Su et al. 2024) and crickets (Niemelä et al. 2012). Pathogen exposure during development alters personality emergence in crickets (DiRienzo et al. 2015). Early-life starvation reshapes *C. elegans* individuality (bioRxiv work cited). Predator-rearing/cue exposure effects on adult clusters have been examined in crickets (with the syndrome dissolving rather than strengthening).

**Q7 (negative/null findings).** *(a)* Sinn & Moltschaniwskyj (2005): dumpling-squid boldness was context-specific. *(b)* Muller et al. (2010): bumblebees showed no multi-day consistency in neophilia. *(c)* Velasque & Briffa (2016): no metabolic-rate–boldness coupling in hermit crabs. *(d)* Niemelä et al. (2012): predator stress dissolved cricket syndromes. *(e)* Royauté et al. (2014): insecticide stress dissolved jumping-spider syndromes. *(f)* Pronk et al. (2010): weaker octopus cross-context consistency than earlier work. These represent a real and underweighted skeptical signal.

**Q8 (taxonomic complexity).** Yes — the pattern is discernible. Coping-style-like clusters are *most* readily documented in cephalopods, decapods, social insects, and araneids (all with relatively elaborated nervous systems). They are *least* documented (or untested) in flatworms, annelids, echinoderms, and most nematodes. *However*, this pattern is potentially confounded by research effort: simpler-nervous-system taxa have received much less attention from personality researchers, so absence may be absence-of-evidence rather than evidence-of-absence.

**Q9 (social insects and division of labor).** Within colonies of honeybees and several ants (*Temnothorax*, *Aphaenogaster*), individual workers show consistent behavioral differences that map onto task specialization (scouting, defense, foraging). Whether these constitute a true proactive–reactive axis or are domain-specific specializations remains contested; the literature uses "behavioral type" and "specialist" more often than "coping style."

**Q10 (cephalopods).** Strongest invertebrate evidence base for personality; weakest physiological-coupling evidence. Heritability demonstrated only in *Euprymna*. Cross-context boldness is reliably *less* consistent than within-context, complicating proactive–reactive readings.

**Q11 (fitness consequences).** Direct fitness measurement: cricket survival in wild (Niemelä et al. 2015), hermit-crab fecundity (Bridger et al. 2015), squid hatching success (Sinn et al. 2006), spider colony defense and competition outcomes (Pruitt et al. — with the data-integrity caveat), ant colony productivity (Blight et al. 2016b). Otherwise, fitness consequences are mostly inferred.

## Methodological Flags Worth Carrying Forward

- **Sample sizes** for repeatability estimation are often N < 30 (e.g., the *O. vulgaris* 2024 study at N = 21), below modern recommendations for stable variance-decomposition CIs.
- **Single-behavior vs. cluster.** Many "coping-style" claims rest on one repeatedly-measured behavior (boldness) plus a syndrome correlation with one or two others — short of a true multi-trait cluster.
- **Physiology missing** in nearly every study; the major exceptions are honeybee biogenic-amine work and isolated hermit-crab metabolic-rate work.
- **Stressor appropriateness.** Octopus brush-touch and food-tube assays, hermit-crab startle, cricket shelter-emergence assays are species-appropriate; some imported assays (open-field analogues) are not always well-tuned to species' ecology.
- **Molt state, age, condition** controlled inconsistently — particularly important in arthropods. Sex differences are reported separately only sometimes; many cephalopod studies pool or omit sex.
- **Terminology.** Almost no invertebrate primary paper uses "proactive–reactive coping style" except Blight et al. (2016). The terms "behavioral syndrome," "animal personality," "shy–bold continuum," and "behavioral type" dominate. Reverse-translation from vertebrate methodology is implicit rather than explicit, often with insufficient adaptation to species-specific repertoires (e.g., importing PCA pipelines without considering ecological context-specificity).

---

## Gaps in the Literature (Treated as Primary Findings)

1. **Taxonomic gaps.** Gastropods, bivalves, annelids, flatworms, echinoderms, nematomorphs, and most nematodes outside *C. elegans* are virtually untested.
2. **Physiological gap.** No invertebrate study documents an individual-level dissociation analogous to vertebrate sympathetic-vs-HPA reactivity. Biogenic-amine work is the closest functional analogue but has not been pushed to multi-axis architecture.
3. **Heritability gap.** Only the dumpling squid has formal h² estimates; the spider genomic association is a start but not a heritability decomposition.
4. **Long-interval repeatability.** Most R estimates cover hours to weeks; lifetime stability has been examined seriously only in hermit crabs, crickets, and *C. elegans*.
5. **Construct-validity gap.** It remains unclear whether invertebrate "shy-bold" axes are a *single* axis that subsumes proactive-reactive coping or are multiple, context-specific axes that have been over-aggregated. Sinn & Moltschaniwskyj (2005) and Muller et al. (2010) argue for the latter.
6. **Anti-confirmation note.** Negative/null findings are systematically less prominent in citing literature than positive findings; the syndrome-dissolves-under-stress pattern (cricket, jumping spider) deserves more weight than it has received.
