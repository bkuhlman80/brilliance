# **Cross-disciplinary bridge-builders in cognition, genetics, and development**

The researchers reshaping the boundaries between cognitive neuroscience, behavioral genetics, and developmental science are disproportionately **early-career scientists trained in one discipline who adopted the computational or methodological toolkit of another** during postdoctoral work. Three convergence zones concentrate the most boundary-crossing empirical work: (1) genetics of specific cognitive facets rather than diagnostic categories, centered at CU Boulder's Institute for Behavioral Genetics and Queen Mary University of London; (2) computational modeling of social and developmental processes, flowing from training hubs at UCL, Oxford, Caltech, and Zurich; and (3) attention–emotion integration in developmental populations, anchored by Penn State and UCLA training lineages. Institutional investment is accelerating — the Wu Tsai Institute at Yale, Harvard's Kempner Institute, and NIMH's IMPACT-MH program all explicitly fund the convergence these researchers represent. The field's most consequential gap remains that **no major cohort simultaneously combines genetics, neuroimaging, ecological momentary assessment, and deep cognitive phenotyping**, leaving the researchers who could integrate these data streams without a dataset adequate to their ambitions.

## **The genetics-cognition frontier moves beyond broad intelligence**

The most significant shift in behavioral genetics since 2019 is the move from broad phenotypes (intelligence, educational attainment) to **genetically distinct cognitive facets**. This movement's empirical center is the CU Boulder Institute for Behavioral Genetics, specifically Naomi Friedman's lab, which has produced a cohort of early-career researchers who treat executive functions as separable genetic constructs rather than proxies for general ability.

**Alexander Hatoum** (Assistant Professor, Washington University in St. Louis) co-led the landmark GWAS of common executive function in the UK Biobank (N=427,037), identifying **129 genome-wide significant variants** and establishing that EF has a genetic architecture separable from IQ, with GABAergic process enrichment and differential genetic correlations with psychiatric disorders (*Biological Psychiatry*, 2023). Hatoum also developed the "addiction genetic factor" using Genomic Structural Equation Modeling, connecting substance use genetics to cognitive architecture. **Daniel Gustavson** (Assistant Research Professor, CU Boulder / Vanderbilt) extends this program by showing that genetic influences on executive function and impulsivity independently predict variance across internalizing, externalizing, thought, and compulsive disorder dimensions (*Clinical Psychological Science*, 2024). He holds a NIDA R01 (2024–2029) for this work.

Across the Atlantic, **Margherita Malanchini** (Reader, Queen Mary University of London) co-led the GWAS-by-subtraction study that identified the genetic architecture of noncognitive skills independent of cognitive ability (N\>1.1 million; *Nature Genetics*, 2021, Demange, Malanchini et al.). Her subsequent work in *Nature Human Behaviour* (2024) demonstrated that genetic associations between noncognitive skills and academic achievement **increase across development** — a finding that only emerges when genetics and developmental science are studied jointly. She is a Jacobs Foundation Research Fellow and directs the Cognition, Development, and Education (CoDE) Lab. Her colleague at Queen Mary, **Giorgia Michelini** (Lecturer), bridges genetics and transdiagnostic neurodevelopmental phenotyping. Michelini proposed integrating a "neurodevelopmental spectrum" into the HiTOP framework (*World Psychiatry*, 2024\) and demonstrated using TEDS twin data (N\>10,000) that this spectrum has heritability of **0.60–0.82** with significant polygenic score associations. She currently serves as president of the International Behavioural and Neural Genetics Society.

**Andrea Allegrini** (Research Fellow, UCL) provides the methodological infrastructure for this intersection, having published the leading guide to computing and implementing polygenic scores in developmental research (*JCPP*, 2022\) and recent work on intergenerational transmission of neuropsychiatric polygenic predisposition (*Nature Communications*, 2025). **Nicole Karcher** (Assistant Professor, Washington University) pushes the ABCD Study's utility for gene-phenotype mapping by conducting phenome-wide association studies linking polygenic scores for psychiatric disorders to \~1,300–1,700 phenotypes in developmental populations (*Nature Mental Health*).

The methodological linchpin enabling much of this work is **Genomic Structural Equation Modeling** (Genomic SEM), developed by Andrew Grotzinger (now at UT Austin) and used extensively by the CU Boulder and Washington University clusters. Genomic SEM allows researchers to model the multivariate genetic architecture of cognitive and psychiatric traits simultaneously — it is the tool that makes cognitive facet genetics computationally tractable at GWAS scale.

## **Computational models create a shared language for social and developmental processes**

Reinforcement learning has become the **lingua franca** connecting social neuroscience, developmental science, and computational psychiatry. The same Rescorla-Wagner/temporal difference learning equations now describe prosocial learning, social threat acquisition, impression formation, adolescent self-esteem dynamics, and social media engagement — providing a common mathematical substrate for previously disconnected research programs.

**Patricia Lockwood** (Professor, University of Birmingham) pioneered the application of RL models to prosocial behavior, demonstrating a dedicated "prosocial prediction error" in subgenual anterior cingulate cortex that drives learning to benefit others (*PNAS*, 2016). Her work established that social reward learning follows computational principles parallel to, but mechanistically distinct from, self-interested reward learning. She co-organizes the Computational Social Cognition Summer School (Birmingham–Leiden), a key training pipeline. **Björn Lindström** (PI, Karolinska Institutet) extends RL models to social threat learning and racial bias, showing that social threat transfers to instrumental decision-making through the same RL update rules (*PNAS*, 2019). His work on reward-learning mechanisms explaining social media engagement (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2021\) demonstrates the framework's reach into digital behavior. **Leor Hackel** (Assistant Professor, USC) reveals how social concepts restructure the RL architecture itself, showing that domain-general model-based and model-free computations interact with social-specific representations during impression formation (*Psychological Science*, 2023).

The developmental application of these frameworks is led by **Tobias Hauser** (Professor, University of Tübingen; ERC Consolidator Grant), who coined "developmental computational psychiatry" and traces how Bayesian information-gathering and exploration-exploitation parameters change normatively during adolescence and derail in OCD and ADHD (*JCPP*, 2019). **Caroline Charpentier** (Assistant Professor, University of Maryland; NIMH K99/R00) applies the same social-learning RL models to autism and anxiety across development, having established her lab in 2023 after training at UCL (Roiser, Sharot) and Caltech (O'Doherty, Adolphs). **Geert-Jan Will** (Utrecht University) formalizes how adolescents dynamically update self-esteem from social feedback using Bayesian updating models, linking computational parameters directly to depression vulnerability — a rare triple intersection of social process, developmental timing, and formal modeling.

Bayesian inverse planning represents a second convergence point. **Julian Jara-Ettinger** (Associate Professor, Yale / Wu Tsai Institute) formalizes theory of mind as inverse reinforcement learning, treating children's mental state inferences as Bayesian inference over a "naive utility calculus" (*Annual Review of Developmental Psychology*, 2025). This framework provides formally testable predictions for developmental data, bridging computational cognitive science and developmental psychology. **Shawn Rhoads** (incoming Assistant Professor, Mount Sinai; NIH Director's Early Independence Award 2024\) combines computational theory-of-mind models with intracranial recordings during two-player social games, studying how strategic belief updating relates to loneliness and social connection. He co-directs the Summer Program in Computational Psychiatry Education (SPICE).

**Lei Zhang** (Associate Professor, University of Birmingham) provides critical methodological scaffolding, publishing best-practice frameworks for applying RL models in social neuroscience (*SCAN*, 2020\) and co-developing the hBayesDM package that lowers computational barriers across labs.

## **Attention, executive function, and social-emotional processing converge through development**

The researchers bridging attention/EF and social-emotional neuroscience almost universally arrive at this intersection **through developmental science** — studying how cognitive control capacities emerge alongside, and shape, social-emotional functioning. Two training lineages dominate.

The **Penn State–Maryland pipeline** (Koraly Pérez-Edgar, Nathan Fox) has produced several bridge-builders. **Santiago Morales** (Assistant Professor, USC) integrates attention bias paradigms with developmental temperament research, showing how executive attention mechanisms mediate the pathway from early temperamental fearfulness to social withdrawal and anxiety (*Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience*, 2016). **George Buzzell** (Assistant Professor, Florida International University) uses EEG-based mediofrontal theta oscillations to dissect how distinct executive function pathways — inhibitory control versus cognitive flexibility — differentially channel temperamental risk into social anxiety outcomes (*Developmental Science*, 2020). **Brendan Ostlund** (K99/R00 awardee, Penn State) studies neural noise (aperiodic EEG activity) in infants, linking neural excitation-inhibition balance to both attention processing efficiency and social-emotional risk trajectories.

The **Columbia–UCLA lineage** (Kevin Ochsner, Jennifer Silvers) focuses on cognitive reappraisal as an EF-dependent strategy operating in social-developmental contexts. **Jennifer Silvers** (Associate Professor, UCLA; Flux Young Investigator Award 2022\) demonstrates how prefrontal-subcortical circuits supporting cognitive control underlie age-related gains in emotion regulation in social contexts, including how caregiver relationships shape regulatory neurobiology. **João Guassi Moreira** (early career, trained in Silvers' lab) integrates MVPA methods from cognitive neuroscience with questions about social decision-making and value-based cognitive control during adolescence.

**Catherine Insel** (Assistant Professor, Northwestern; K99/R00 awardee) bridges these traditions by using computational modeling — specifically reinforcement learning — to study how emotional cues influence cognitive control and value-guided goal pursuit during adolescence. Her work appears in both affective neuroscience venues (*SCAN*) and cognitive development outlets (*Nature Communications*), with new intensive longitudinal work on brain-behavior dynamics. **Monica Rosenberg** (Yale, returning from University of Chicago; CNS 2026 Young Investigator Award; Sloan Fellow) developed connectome-based predictive modeling of sustained attention, which generalizes to predict ADHD symptoms and is being extended to social-cognitive and naturalistic contexts — providing a network-neuroscience bridge between attention research and broader psychological phenotyping.

Outside the US, **Alexandre Heeren** (Research Professor, UCLouvain; STAR Early Career Award 2020\) pioneered computational network analysis connecting attention control deficits to social anxiety, combining Attention Network Test data with intensive longitudinal EMA and neuromodulation (tDCS to dlPFC). **Rachel Romeo** (Assistant Professor, University of Maryland; Flux Young Investigator Award 2024\) studies how early language environments and socioeconomic factors shape both executive function circuitry and social-emotional development, mechanistically linking conversational turn-taking to prefrontal development supporting cognitive control.

## **Cohorts enabling cross-domain work and where the measurement gaps lie**

The **ABCD Study** remains the most consequential platform for cross-domain research, with \~11,875 youth tracked longitudinally, multimodal neuroimaging, genotyping (\~11 million imputed SNPs), and an embedded twin cohort. A 2023 special issue in *Behavior Genetics* provided heritability estimates for over 50,000 brain and behavioral phenotypes. However, ABCD's cognitive battery (NIH Toolbox) **does not decompose executive functions** into shifting, updating, and inhibition — the very facets that genetic research increasingly shows are distinct constructs. Social-emotional phenotyping relies on parent/self-report questionnaires rather than behavioral tasks, and the study includes no ecological momentary assessment or wearable data.

The **UK Biobank** (N\~500,000) has been the workhorse for cognitive GWAS but has minimal social-emotional or affective measures. A major development is the **NIHR BioResource Genes and Cognition cohort** (N=21,051), published in *Nature Medicine* (2024), which provides detailed multi-domain cognitive profiling linked to genotyping. This study found **distinct genetic architectures for different cognitive domains** — one implicating microglia, another glycogen metabolism — and uniquely allows participant recall stratified by genotype × cognitive phenotype for interventional studies.

Several lesser-known cohorts are enabling genuinely novel cross-domain work:

* **ORIGINs Consortium** (16 sites, 5 countries, N\~6,809 children birth to 6\) represents the largest imaging-genetics dataset focused on infancy. Its cross-cultural reach (US, UK, Germany, Singapore, South Africa) revealed that PRS-depression × maternal depression interactions on neonatal amygdala volume **show opposite directions in Asian versus US cohorts** — a finding possible only through cross-population integration.  
* **GUSTO** (Singapore, N=1,358 mother-child pairs across Chinese, Malay, and Indian ethnicities) combines genotyping, DNA methylation, and longitudinal brain imaging at multiple ages. Recent work linked infant screen time to accelerated brain network maturation and altered emotion regulation networks, moderated by parent-child reading (*Psychological Medicine*, 2024).  
* **Drakenstein Child Health Study** (Cape Town, N\~1,000) is one of the only pediatric neuroimaging cohorts in sub-Saharan Africa, demonstrating feasibility of unsedated MRI in 2–3-year-olds with a 77% success rate.  
* **NKI-Rockland Sample II** (N\~600) comes closest to the field's aspirational design by combining fMRI \+ EEG simultaneously, ecological sampling via wearables, and over 300 phenotypic variables — but its sample size is too small for genetic analyses.  
* **Healthy Brain Network** (N\>8,000 target 10,000, ages 5–21) uses a transdiagnostic recruitment design with multimodal MRI, high-density EEG with concurrent eye-tracking, and genetics, but relies heavily on questionnaires rather than behavioral tasks for social-emotional phenotyping.  
* The **Munich Clinical Deep Phenotyping Study** (N=381+) is explicitly aligned with the RDoC framework and uniquely combines neuroimaging, electrophysiology, retinal imaging, blood biobanking, and hiPSC generation in the same cohort — enabling researchers to correlate patient-derived neurons with in-vivo brain data.

The most critical measurement gap across all major cohorts is the absence of any dataset that simultaneously combines **genetics \+ neuroimaging \+ ecological momentary assessment \+ deep cognitive phenotyping** (decomposed executive functions, social cognition tasks, emotion regulation paradigms). NKI-RS II approaches this design but lacks statistical power for genetic analyses. A second gap is that social cognition behavioral tasks (theory of mind, empathy paradigms, social decision-making) are severely underrepresented in genetically-informed cohorts — nearly all rely on questionnaire-based social-emotional measures. Third, non-Western populations remain critically underrepresented: GUSTO, DCHS, BRIGHT, and ORIGINs represent important starts, but sample sizes are small and few combine genetics, cognition, and imaging.

## **Methodological convergence is creating shared computational languages**

Three computational frameworks are now applied simultaneously across traditionally separate domains, creating implicit bridges between research communities that may not attend the same conferences.

**Drift-diffusion models** (DDMs), originally developed for perceptual decision-making, are now applied to social decision-making (fairness judgments, ultimatum games), reinforcement learning (combined RL-DDM models), psychiatric phenotyping (ADHD, depression), and developmental trajectories (applied in ABCD Study analyses). Software tools like HDDM, HSSM, and PyDDM lower implementation barriers. The same DDM parameters — drift rate, boundary separation, non-decision time — are being estimated in attention tasks and social cognition paradigms, enabling direct comparison of computational mechanisms across domains.

**Reinforcement learning models** serve as the most prominent convergence framework. The same Rescorla-Wagner update rules describe prosocial learning (Lockwood), social threat transfer (Lindström), impression formation (Hackel), adolescent self-esteem updating (Will), developmental exploration-exploitation (Hauser), and social media engagement (Lindström). The hBayesDM package (co-developed by Lei Zhang) provides standardized Bayesian hierarchical estimation across these applications.

**Network analysis and graph theory** provide a third convergence language, applied across brain connectomics, genetic pathway networks, psychopathology networks (the network theory of mental disorders), and developmental brain science. In the ABCD Study, researchers now use graph-theoretic brain network measures alongside polygenic risk scores to predict cognitive and behavioral outcomes, bridging neuroimaging, genetics, and developmental science within a single analytic framework.

Novel measurement tools adopted across domains include **ecological momentary assessment** (bridging cognitive neuroscience, digital phenotyping, and psychiatric genetics), **connectome-based predictive modeling** (Rosenberg's approach, extending from attention to social cognition and clinical prediction), and **mobile brain/body imaging** (MoBI, used in NKI-RS II, combining ambulatory EEG with motion capture). The **affective Posner task combined with DDM** (from the Pérez-Edgar lineage) represents a paradigm-level bridge, decomposing latent cognitive processes linking attention disengagement from threat to social anxiety in youth. Intensive longitudinal designs — such as 100-occasion daily inhibitory control assessment via mobile Stroop tasks — enable person-specific modeling of how daily cognitive fluctuations relate to social-emotional behavior.

## **Training pipelines reveal where the next generation is being forged**

The training backgrounds of early-career bridge-builders reveal distinct institutional lineages that function as cross-disciplinary "schools."

For **genetics × cognition**, CU Boulder's Institute for Behavioral Genetics (Friedman lab) is the dominant training ground, producing Hatoum, Gustavson, Morrison, and Sam Freis. The T32 training grant at IBG explicitly supports cross-disciplinary training. Queen Mary University of London (Malanchini, Michelini) is emerging as a second hub, connected to the broader UK behavioral genetics infrastructure (TEDS, King's College, UCL). The UCLA Neurobehavioral Genetics T32 (directed by Roel Ophoff) bridges genetics and behavioral neuroscience on the US West Coast.

For **computational × social/developmental neuroscience**, training flows through four major hubs. **UCL** (Ray Dolan, Peter Dayan) produced Hauser and Will. **Oxford** (Rushworth, Apps, Crockett) produced Lockwood and Siegel. **Caltech** (O'Doherty, Adolphs) trained Charpentier. **MIT** (Tenenbaum, Schulz) trained Jara-Ettinger. The **Zurich Computational Psychiatry Course** (running annually since 2014, celebrating its 10th anniversary in 2024\) has trained hundreds of researchers who now populate the field across all of these intersections. The **Leiden Brain and Development Lab** (Eveline Crone) produces researchers bridging computational and developmental social neuroscience, including Will and Hofmans. **Mount Sinai's Center for Computational Psychiatry** (Xiaosi Gu) is producing a cluster including Rhoads and the SPICE training program.

For **attention/EF × social-emotional neuroscience**, the **Penn State / University of Maryland** axis (Pérez-Edgar, Fox) trained Morales, Buzzell, and Ostlund. The **Columbia–UCLA** axis (Ochsner, Silvers) trained Guassi Moreira and other researchers bridging cognitive reappraisal and social development.

The **UCL Wellcome 4-Year PhD in Mental Health Science** (launched 2019, £5M+ Wellcome funding) explicitly integrates molecular genetics, cellular/systems neuroscience, cognitive neuroimaging, epidemiology, and social determinants — and includes a joint UCL-NIMH doctoral training pathway. The **Duke DN-IPT** (Developmental Neuroscience Integrated Postdoctoral Training) combines brain imaging, computational modeling, and digital health for child development research. Carnegie Mellon's **Program in Neural Computation** (joint with Pittsburgh) continues to produce researchers fluent in both computational modeling and experimental neuroscience.

## **Institutional investment signals where the field is heading**

The period 2020–2026 shows unprecedented institutional commitment to cross-disciplinary integration, driven by massive philanthropic gifts, NIH strategic pivots, and European funding reforms.

The **Wu Tsai Institute** at Yale (directed by Nicholas Turk-Browne) co-locates the Department of Psychology with the Department of Neuroscience and organizes around three centers — Neurodevelopment and Plasticity, Neurocognition and Behavior, and Neurocomputation and Machine Intelligence — that structurally require cross-domain collaboration. Harvard's **Kempner Institute** ($500 million from CZI over 15 years) bridges natural and artificial intelligence, supporting 10 new faculty appointments and a dedicated supercomputing cluster. The **UC San Diego Verge Center** ($12.7 million from CIRM) uses stem cell and brain organoid technologies to study how genetic mutations influence brain development, correlating patient-derived neurons with clinical cognitive-neural phenotypes of the same patients — directly linking genetics to cell biology to neuroscience to behavior.

NIMH's **IMPACT-MH** program (launched 2023\) is arguably the single most important US federal funding signal. Part of the $50 million Precision Psychiatry Initiative introduced by Director Joshua Gordon, IMPACT-MH requires using behavioral measures AND computational methods to define novel clinical signatures for individual-level prediction — structurally mandating the kind of cross-domain integration this report maps. Related RDoC-aligned funding opportunities (PAR-23-305 for computationally-defined behaviors, PAR-23-307 for computational approaches to dimensional constructs) further incentivize the convergence.

The **Wellcome Trust's Mental Health Awards** requiring both computational and experimental neuroscience components and research at multiple scales of analysis (molecular, cellular, circuit, systems, behavioral) represent the strongest international signal. SFARI's **Linking Early Neurodevelopment to Neural Circuit Outcomes** RFA explicitly bridges developmental neurobiology with circuit neuroscience. The BRAIN Initiative's **Theories, Models and Methods** program funds computational theories bridging molecular to behavioral scales, while **BRAIN CONNECTS** ($150 million over five years) maps brain connectivity across species.

Publication signals include Astle, Holmes, Kievit and Gathercole's influential *JCPP* Annual Research Review (2022) arguing that diagnostic categories poorly map onto genetics, neural function, and cognition — a paper that generated follow-up work and institutional response across the transdiagnostic movement. The journal *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience* has become a major outlet for ABCD Study publications that integrate genetic, neural, and cognitive data. The Zurich Computational Psychiatry Course has evolved from a training event into a hybrid course-conference, and CNS 2026 features symposia explicitly combining genetics, AI, and cognitive neuroscience.

## **Conclusion: what the map reveals**

This field map exposes three structural features that are not obvious from reading any single literature.

First, **training lineage matters more than institutional affiliation** for predicting who does genuine cross-domain work. The researchers identified here are not simply people employed at interdisciplinary institutes — they are people who acquired a second discipline's toolkit during a specific training period, typically a postdoc. The CU Boulder IBG → Washington University pipeline, the UCL/Oxford → computational social neuroscience dispersal, and the Penn State → attention-emotion-development network are reproducible patterns, not coincidences. The implication is that cross-disciplinary capacity is built person by person through specific mentorship relationships, not by hiring across departments.

Second, **computational frameworks function as the actual mechanism of convergence**, not merely as tools applied after the fact. Reinforcement learning models do not simply help social neuroscientists analyze data — they redefine what counts as a social neuroscience finding, making it commensurable with findings in attention research, developmental science, and genetics. Genomic SEM does not merely help geneticists model complex traits — it creates a representational space where cognitive facets and psychiatric dimensions can be compared on equal footing. The methodological tail is wagging the theoretical dog.

Third, **the data infrastructure lags behind the researchers**. The people identified in this report have the skills to integrate genetics, computation, neuroimaging, and deep behavioral phenotyping — but no single dataset gives them all of these simultaneously at adequate statistical power. The ABCD Study has genetic and imaging data but shallow cognitive decomposition and no EMA. The UK Biobank has genetic power but no developmental data and minimal social-emotional measures. The most innovative cohorts (NKI-RS II, Munich CDP) have deep phenotyping but small samples. The next major institutional investment that would accelerate this entire field is not another training program or research center — it is a cohort that combines GWAS-scale genetics with decomposed executive function measurement, social cognition behavioral tasks, ecological momentary assessment, and longitudinal neuroimaging in a developmentally and demographically diverse sample.
