# **Digital media, generational cohorts, and personality: what the measurement evidence actually shows**

**The short answer to all three measurement questions is sobering for the popular narrative: between-cohort mean differences in personality are small and have not clearly grown in the digital era; within-cohort variance data barely exist; and the Big Five factor structure appears largely stable across cohorts.** No published study has demonstrated that digital media adoption causally changed the magnitude of between-cohort personality trait differences. The strongest, most recent evidence — a 2025 global meta-analysis of 546,225 participants across 55 countries — finds narcissism *declining*, not rising. Meanwhile, the most methodologically rigorous studies consistently report small or null cohort effects once measurement invariance is controlled and age effects are properly separated. The empirical literature on variance and covariance structure across digitally differentiated cohorts is thin enough that honest answers to questions 2 and 3 must be heavily qualified.

---

## **1\. Between-cohort mean differences: small, contested, and not clearly growing**

### **The cross-temporal meta-analysis tradition**

The dominant research program here is Jean Twenge's cross-temporal meta-analytic (CTMA) method, which tracks mean scores of same-age samples (typically U.S. college students) completing the same instrument across different years. Key findings from this program include reported increases in anxiety/neuroticism of **d ≈ 1.0** from 1952–1993 (Twenge, 2000; multiple anxiety measures; college and child samples), increases in narcissism of **d \= 0.33** from 1982–2006 (Twenge et al., 2008; NPI-40; k \= 85, N \= 16,475), and increases in self-esteem of **d \= 0.30–0.78** from 1988–2008 depending on age group (Twenge & Campbell, 2008; Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale; combined N ≈ 55,700). These are the largest reported cohort effects in the literature.

However, nearly every one of these findings has been substantially challenged. **Wetzel et al. (2017)** published the single most important corrective study in *Psychological Science* (Vol. 28, pp. 1833–1847). Using three cohorts of college students (1990s: N \= 1,166; 2000s: N \= 33,647; 2010s: N \= 25,412) and the NPI-40, they performed what Twenge's program had not: formal measurement invariance testing across cohorts. Several NPI items were not equivalent across cohorts. After accounting for this non-invariance, they found a **small decline** in overall narcissism (d \= −0.27), with facet-level declines in leadership (d \= −0.20), vanity (d \= −0.16), and entitlement (d \= −0.28). The "narcissism epidemic" reversed direction once measurement artifacts were controlled.

**Oberleiter, Stickel, & Pietschnig (2025)** then delivered what amounts to the definitive analysis: a preregistered, precision-weighted cross-temporal meta-analysis in the *Journal of Personality* (Vol. 93, pp. 884–894) covering **1,105 studies, k \= 1,621 samples, N \= 546,225 participants across 55 countries, 1982–2023**. They found meaningful *negative* cross-temporal associations between data collection year and narcissism scores in virtually all analyses (bs: −0.409 to −0.008; partial η²: \<0.001 to 0.118). No evidence of a narcissism epidemic was found globally, regionally, or by sex. The Global Financial Crisis did not mark a significant inflection point. This study cannot separate age, period, and cohort effects (an inherent CTMA limitation), but it decisively undermines the claim that recent cohorts are more narcissistic.

### **Studies with stronger designs for age–period–cohort separation**

**Brandt et al. (2022)** used data from the **Seattle Longitudinal Study** (*Psychological Science*, Vol. 33, pp. 382–396; N \= 4,732; born 1883–1976; tracked 1956–2012) in a cohort-sequential longitudinal design — the best available approach for partially separating age, period, and cohort effects. At age 56, later-born cohorts showed **lower agreeableness and neuroticism** and **higher extraversion and openness**, with women in later cohorts showing higher conscientiousness. Effect sizes were small to moderate. Critically, they found **little evidence that rates of personality change differ across cohorts** — differences were in levels, not developmental slopes. The interpretation: delayed social investment and maturity milestones, not a fundamental personality restructuring.

**A Romanian hierarchical age–period–cohort (HAPC) analysis** (2022, *Journal of Research in Personality*; N \= 30,722; pooled cross-sectional data 2006–2018; IPIP Big Five) found **no generation-related effects on personality** at either the domain or facet level after controlling for age and period. The authors concluded that broad societal changes exert "a minor influence over the distribution of personality traits."

A **German aviation-selection study** (2023, *Frontiers in Psychology*; N \= 82,147 applicants aged 17–24; born 1965–2002; Temperament Structure Scales) compared Gen X (N \= 20,103), Gen Y (N \= 50,531), and Gen Z (N \= 11,513) using ANCOVAs controlling for age and social desirability. Effect sizes for generational differences were **"extremely small"** (η² values). Within-generation variation vastly exceeded between-generation variation.

**Trzesniewski & Donnellan (2010)** (*Perspectives on Psychological Science*, Vol. 5, pp. 58–75) used the **Monitoring the Future** dataset — a nationally representative U.S. probability sample of **N \= 477,380** high school seniors from 1976–2006 — and found **little evidence of meaningful change** in egotism, self-enhancement, individualism, self-esteem, locus of control, happiness, life satisfaction, or loneliness. Only educational expectations increased and trust/civic engagement decreased. This stands in sharp contrast to Twenge's convenience-sample findings.

**Roberts, Edmonds, & Grijalva (2010)** (*Perspectives on Psychological Science*, Vol. 5, pp. 97–102) demonstrated the key confound: when new data were added to Twenge's narcissism meta-analysis, the increase disappeared. More importantly, **age-related declines in narcissism** (∼8 NPI points across the lifespan) were replicable and far larger than any between-cohort differences (∼2 points). Their title captures the conclusion: "It Is Developmental Me, Not Generation Me."

### **Have digital-era cohort gaps specifically widened?**

**No study provides evidence that between-cohort personality differences have grown larger for digitally saturated cohorts.** The German aviation study (spanning Gen X through Gen Z, including fully digital-native participants) found the smallest effect sizes in the literature. Twenge's own updated narcissism analysis (2021; *Personality and Individual Differences*; k \= 164, N \= 35,095 plus within-campus data from UC Davis N \= 58,287 and U. South Alabama N \= 14,319) acknowledged a non-monotonic pattern: narcissism increased through approximately 2008 and then **declined**, returning to 1980s/1990s levels by 2013–2016. The pandemic-era study by **Sutin et al. (2022)** (*PLOS ONE*; N \= 7,109; Understanding America Study; BFI) found personality disruption of approximately **d \= 0.10** per trait — equivalent to roughly a decade of normative change — with younger adults showing "disrupted maturity" (increased neuroticism, decreased agreeableness and conscientiousness). But this appears to be a **period effect** of the pandemic, not a cohort effect of digital media.

**Smits et al. (2011)** (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Vol. 100, pp. 1124–1138) examined Dutch first-year psychology students aged 18–25 across 13 cohorts from 1982–2007 using the Five Personality Factors Test (70 items). After formally testing and controlling for measurement invariance (identifying 11 biased items for females, 2 for males), they found **small linear increases** in extraversion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness, and **small linear decreases** in neuroticism. No clear pattern for openness. Effect sizes were small. This 25-year span mostly predates smartphone saturation, but the direction of change is consistent with cultural modernization rather than digital disruption.

## **2\. Between-cohort variance differences: a striking empirical gap**

This is where the literature essentially runs dry. **No published study was identified that directly compares within-cohort standard deviations of Big Five or HEXACO personality scores between pre-digital and post-digital cohorts at the same age.** This is the report's most important negative finding.

What exists instead is a set of adjacent literatures that provide indirect evidence. A **2024 meta-analysis of 362 longitudinal studies (N \= 344,270)** examining personality trait variance across the lifespan found **"remarkable stability of personality trait variance"** — people neither become more different from each other nor more alike as they age. The only exceptions were small variance decreases in extraversion, neuroticism, and openness during ages 1–4, and a small neuroticism variance increase in late childhood (ages 5–13). By adolescence, variance has stabilized.

**Mõttus, Soto, Slobodskaya, & Back (2017)** (*European Journal of Personality*, Vol. 31, pp. 313–328) showed that individual differences in personality increase from early childhood to early adolescence and then **plateau** around mid-adolescence, using large parent-report samples from the U.S. and Russia. **Mõttus, Briley, Tucker-Drob, et al. (2019)** (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Vol. 117, pp. 635–658) demonstrated through behavioral genetic designs that these developmental increases in variance are **primarily genetically driven**, not driven by shared environmental influences. This is important: it suggests that cohort-level cultural shifts (including digital media adoption) would not be expected to change within-cohort personality variance through environmental pathways.

Twenge's cross-temporal meta-analyses use within-sample standard deviations as denominators for calculating effect sizes but **never analyze these SDs as outcome variables**. This is a significant missed opportunity. The raw data to answer the variance question likely exist in Twenge's archived datasets, the Gosling-Rentfrow-Swann internet personality samples (N \> 1 million), and large panel studies like the German SOEP, but the analyses have not been conducted.

**Smits et al. (2011)** allowed factor variances to differ across their 13 cohorts in their measurement invariance models — a methodological choice that implies they found some variance differences — but the paper focuses on means and does not report whether variance changed systematically across the 25-year span.

The specific hypothesis that social media creates within-cohort personality convergence (homogenization) or divergence has **no direct empirical test**. Research on homophily in social networks (e.g., Lönnqvist & Itkonen, 2016, on Facebook friendship networks) shows that people with similar personalities cluster together online, but this is a selection effect, not evidence that the platform makes people's personalities more similar.

**Bottom line for question 2: The literature cannot currently answer whether post-digital cohorts are more or less homogeneous in personality. This is an addressable gap — the data exist — but the analyses have not been done.**

## **3\. Covariance structure: mostly stable, with important developmental caveats**

### **The Big Five factor structure holds across cohorts**

**Smits et al. (2011)** is the only study identified that directly tests measurement invariance of a Big Five instrument across birth cohorts (rather than merely across age groups). Across 13 cohorts of same-age Dutch students from 1982–2007, they found the five-factor structure was **largely invariant**. Configural invariance held (the same factors emerged). Metric invariance largely held (factor loadings were comparable). At the scalar level (intercepts), **11 of 70 items showed bias for females and 2 of 70 for males** — meaning roughly 16% of items for women functioned differently across cohorts. This is non-trivial but does not indicate a structural breakdown.

**Soto, John, Gosling, & Potter (2011)** (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Vol. 100, pp. 330–348; N \= 1,267,218; BFI; ages 10–65; internet sample 2003–2009) found personality factors were **"reasonably invariant" across ages**. However, this is a cross-sectional study that confounds age and cohort.

**Zimprich, Allemand, & Lachman (2012)** (*Psychological Assessment*, Vol. 24, pp. 173–186; N \= 3,000; MIDUS personality adjective items; 10 age groups) found **strict measurement invariance** — equal factor structure, loadings, and residual variances — across all age groups. Factor variances and covariances were also equal.

**Shchebetenko et al. (2020)** (*Assessment*, Vol. 27; N \= 2,053; BFI-2; Russian version) found **strict measurement invariance across sex and age** for the most modern Big Five instrument, using random intercept exploratory factor analyses to control acquiescence.

### **Factor coherence changes developmentally but not generationally**

**Soto, John, Gosling, & Potter (2008)** (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Vol. 94, pp. 718–737; large internet sample; BFI; ages 10–20) documented that Big Five self-reports become **more coherent within domains** (items within a domain correlate more strongly) and **better differentiated across domains** (lower cross-domain correlations) from ages 10 to 20\. Acquiescence variability was large in younger participants and distorted psychometric properties. This has an important implication: if researchers compare Gen Z 15-year-olds to Millennial 25-year-olds, they will find structural differences that are entirely developmental, not generational.

**No study has demonstrated that the factor structure of personality has fundamentally changed in post-digital cohorts.** The five factors continue to replicate. However, the absence of evidence is partly due to the absence of targeted research: factor structure analyses comparing, say, 2005 college freshmen to 2020 college freshmen on the same instrument are rare.

### **HEXACO evidence**

**García et al. (2022)** (*Journal of Personality*, Vol. 90, pp. 256–276; HEXACO-60; 18 countries) found configural and metric invariance across countries and no relevant effect of age on factor structure. Scalar invariance was not achieved. The **HEXACO-18** short scale (Jankowsky et al., 2021; *Journal of Personality Assessment*; N \= 6,419; ages 16–90) was specifically designed with scalar measurement invariance across age as a criterion and achieved it — demonstrating that it is possible to engineer age-invariant HEXACO measures, though most existing instruments fall short at the scalar level.

### **A systematic review's bottom line**

**Dong & Dumas (2020)** (*Personality and Individual Differences*, Vol. 160; systematic review of 75 articles, 95 studies) found that **58.82% of age-group measurement invariance studies achieved scalar or strict invariance** for the Big Five, and age-group studies achieved significantly higher invariance levels than cross-cultural studies. The glass is more than half full, but imperfect scalar invariance means that mean comparisons across cohorts carry some risk of bias from differential item functioning.

## **Measurement invariance concerns deserve their own careful treatment**

### **The self-presentation problem**

**Mõttus et al. (2022)** (*Journal of Research in Personality*; self- and informant-reports from Estonia, Germany, and Czech Republic; N \= 5,624) provide perhaps the strongest evidence for concern: age differences in personality were **systematically smaller in informant-reports than in self-reports**, and this discrepancy was larger for traits independently rated as socially desirable. This strongly suggests that age-related increases in socially desirable self-presentation inflate apparent personality maturation in self-report data — and by extension, may confound cohort comparisons.

**Vigil-Colet, Morales-Vives, & Lorenzo-Seva (2013)** (*Psicothema*, Vol. 25, pp. 342–348; N \= 3,773; ages 13–97; OPERAS personality test) found both social desirability and acquiescence **increase by 1–2 standard deviations** between young adulthood and old age, and this increase inflates apparent age-personality relationships, especially for agreeableness and conscientiousness.

No empirical study has tested whether social media–era self-presentation norms specifically alter personality inventory responding across cohorts. The theoretical concern is plausible — Gen Z respondents socialized in curated digital self-presentation may approach self-report items differently — but it remains untested.

### **Reference group effects**

**Heine, Lehman, Peng, & Greenholtz (2002)** (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) demonstrated that people compare themselves to culturally specific reference groups when answering Likert-scale personality items. This is directly relevant to cohort comparisons: if Gen Z respondents compare themselves to Gen Z peers while Boomers compare to Boomer peers, observed mean shifts could be entirely artifactual. A recent study (2024; N \= 1,194) testing reference group instructions on Big Five scores found effects were smaller than expected, but the specific cohort-comparison scenario has **never been directly tested**.

### **The CTMA methodology debate**

The Twenge–Trzesniewski/Donnellan debate crystallized five core concerns with cross-temporal meta-analyses:

* **Convenience sample composition changes**: CTMAs rely on college student samples from different universities across decades. Massive expansion of college access since the 1960s means "college students in 1970" and "college students in 2010" are demographically different populations.  
* **Ecological correlation issues**: Correlating sample means with year while using individual-level SDs for effect sizes mixes levels of analysis.  
* **Context confounds**: Samples from different eras come from different universities, regions, and demographic contexts, all confounded with time.  
* **Publication/selection bias**: Adding a single omitted study (Donnellan et al., 2005; N \= 3,143) to Twenge's narcissism meta-analysis reduced the secular increase by nearly half.  
* **Measurement invariance assumed, not tested**: Prior to Wetzel et al. (2017), no CTMA had tested whether the instrument meant the same thing across cohorts. When invariance was tested, it was partially violated and the direction of the effect reversed.

### **Cross-cultural differential item functioning**

**Church et al. (2011)** (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Vol. 101, pp. 1068–1089) found that **40–50% of NEO-PI-R items** exhibited differential item functioning across U.S., Philippines, and Mexico samples. **Nye, Roberts, Saucier, & Zhou (2008)** found over **60% of Big Five Mini-Markers** showed DIF across American, Greek, and Chinese samples. If cultural differences produce this level of item non-equivalence, generational "subcultures" could plausibly produce smaller but non-negligible DIF — but this has not been systematically studied within the cohort-comparison context.

## **Cross-national evidence: the dog that didn't bark**

**No study has tested whether countries with different digital media adoption timelines show different patterns of personality cohort change.** This is a natural experiment waiting to be exploited — South Korea, Estonia, and Scandinavian countries adopted smartphones and social media earlier and more completely than many African and South Asian nations — but the data linkage has not been made for personality traits.

The closest evidence comes from **Vuorre & Przybylski (2023)** (*Royal Society Open Science*; actual Facebook adoption data linked to Gallup World Poll well-being data across 72 countries; N \= 946,798; 2008–2019), which found **no consistent or measurable associations between well-being and the rollout of social media globally**. A follow-up study (Vuorre & Przybylski, 2024; *Clinical Psychological Science*) examining internet and mobile broadband adoption found only "small and inconsistent changes in global well-being and mental health" not suggestive of consistent negative associations with technology adoption. These studies measured well-being, not Big Five personality traits, but they are the best available cross-national test of the digital media → psychological outcome hypothesis, and the results are null.

The **Schmitt et al. ISDP studies** mapped Big Five traits across 56 nations, finding cross-cultural personality variation, but these are single-timepoint snapshots, not cohort-change studies. **Non-Western longitudinal personality data linked to digital media adoption is essentially absent.** China, Japan, and South Korea have validated Big Five instruments but lack published studies tracking personality cohort change in relation to technology adoption timelines.

**Braghieri, Levy, & Makarin (2022)** (*American Economic Review*) leveraged the staggered introduction of Facebook across U.S. colleges (2004–2006) as a natural experiment with over 400,000 survey responses. They found Facebook rollout **negatively impacted student mental health**, especially depression, with effects appearing 1–2 years post-introduction. This is the strongest quasi-experimental evidence for social media → mental health effects, but it measured depression symptoms, not personality traits.

## **Methodological limitations that constrain everything above**

### **The age–period–cohort identification problem is fundamental, not fixable**

**Rohrer (2025)** (*Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*) provides the most current methodological statement: because Age \= Period − Cohort, these three effects **cannot be statistically separated without untestable assumptions**, regardless of study design or sample size. Cross-temporal meta-analyses hold age constant but cannot distinguish cohort from period effects. Cross-sectional studies confound age and cohort. Even cohort-sequential longitudinal designs (the gold standard) require assumptions about which temporal dimension to constrain. "Bounding analysis" approaches (Fosse & Winship) make assumptions explicit but cannot resolve the fundamental indeterminacy.

### **The literature has a digital-era coverage problem**

Most large-sample personality studies with strong designs were published when Gen Z was still young. The massive Soto et al. (2011) internet sample (collected 2003–2009) does not include Gen Z adults. The Seattle Longitudinal Study tracks cohorts born through 1976 only. The Smits et al. (2011) cohort comparison ends in 2007\. **Fully digital-native cohorts (born after \~2000, smartphone-saturated adolescence after \~2012) are poorly represented in the high-quality literature.** The German aviation study (2023) is the best exception, but it uses a specialized non-Big-Five instrument with a self-selected applicant sample.

### **What "digital media effects" means remains poorly specified**

The literature lacks a clear causal model linking digital media to personality. Twenge's work on smartphones concerns mental health outcomes (depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation), not Big Five traits per se. **Haidt's "great rewiring" hypothesis (2024) contains no personality-specific evidence.** Orben & Przybylski's (2019) specification curve analysis (*Psychological Science*; N \= 17,247) found digital technology explains only **\~0.4% of variability** in adolescent well-being — comparable to the association between well-being and eating potatoes. Even the Twenge & Campbell (2020) rebuttal, re-analyzing the same data to focus on heavy social media use, found associations that are modest by any standard.

No published study has directly measured whether social media use causes within-person Big Five trait change over time. The only experimental evidence — **Drążkowski & Pietrzak (2022)** (N \= 325\) — found that Instagram use temporarily elevated neuroticism *states*, not traits. The distinction matters enormously: repeated state activation could theoretically shift traits over years, but this remains a hypothesis without longitudinal confirmation.

## **What the evidence warrants concluding**

The three measurement questions receive strikingly different levels of empirical support. For **between-cohort mean differences**, the evidence is abundant and points toward a clear conclusion: effects are small (typically d \< 0.30 when properly measured), have not grown in the digital era, and in the best-studied case (narcissism) have reversed direction. The most methodologically careful studies consistently produce smaller effects than less careful ones — a signature of initial overestimation, not of a real phenomenon waiting to be captured more precisely. Age/developmental effects on personality are replicable and large; cohort effects are contested and small.

For **between-cohort variance**, the honest answer is that the literature has not asked the question. The data to answer it likely exist in multiple archived datasets, but no one has analyzed within-cohort SDs as an outcome variable across pre-digital and post-digital cohorts. The adjacent literature on lifespan variance suggests remarkable stability, and the genetic architecture of variance expansion in childhood suggests cultural forces like social media would not be primary drivers. But direct evidence is simply absent.

For **covariance structure**, the Big Five factor structure appears robust across the cohorts studied. The only direct cohort-comparison test (Smits et al., 2011\) found the structure largely intact across 25 years, with a small minority of items showing bias. Developmental changes in factor coherence (increasing from ages 10–20) are well-documented and must be distinguished from cohort changes. No evidence suggests that digital media has restructured personality's factor organization, but targeted research on this specific question is scarce.

The broader implication is that the popular narrative — social media is reshaping the personalities of an entire generation — currently **lacks empirical support at the level of measured personality traits**. This does not mean the hypothesis is wrong; it means it is untested with appropriate designs. The field needs longitudinal studies measuring Big Five traits alongside digital media use across culturally varied cohorts, with pre-registered measurement invariance testing. Until those studies exist, claims about digital media's personality effects remain at the level of plausible speculation, not established science.
