# **Executive function research: where the field stands and where momentum is heading**

**The Miyake & Friedman unity/diversity model remains structurally dominant but is under serious pressure from three directions**: computational decomposition revealing that "Common EF" may reduce to processing speed, network psychometrics reconceptualizing EF as emergent rather than latent, and molecular genetics showing GABAergic—not dopaminergic—pathways drive individual differences. No replacement framework has achieved consensus, but the field is converging on a modified picture: EF structure is age-dependent, the three-factor model is likely overspecified (a two-component solution—common plus shifting-specific—may be more robust), and the boundary between EF and intelligence dissolves genetically in childhood but sharpens by adulthood. The most consequential empirical development since 2019 is the first adequately powered GWAS of executive function (Hatoum et al., 2023; N \= 427,037), which identified 112 loci and implicated fast synaptic transmission rather than dopamine—upending decades of neurochemical assumptions.

This review covers **\~50 empirical papers across six sub-areas**, organized by the questions researchers themselves are trying to answer. Papers were selected for novelty, sample size, cross-disciplinary impact, and mechanistic content. Review papers and meta-analyses were used only as pointers; clinical intervention studies were excluded unless they contained circuit-level or genetic mechanism.

## **1\. The unity/diversity model holds as data reduction but its interpretation is contested**

The Miyake & Friedman bifactor model—Common EF plus updating-specific and shifting-specific factors—remains the default structural framework. But since 2019, three empirical challenges have emerged that collectively suggest the model captures statistical regularity without resolving what the common factor actually is.

### **Paper 1.1**

**Hatoum, A. S., Morrison, C. L., Mitchell, E. C., Lam, M., Benca-Bachman, C. E., Reineberg, A. E., Palmer, R. H. C., Evans, L. M., Keller, M. C., & Friedman, N. P. (2023).** Genome-wide association study shows that executive functioning is influenced by GABAergic processes and is a neurocognitive genetic correlate of psychiatric disorders. *Biological Psychiatry*, 93(1), 59–70. DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2022.06.034

* **Design:** GWAS / molecular genetics. **N \= 427,037** (UK Biobank, European descent). Genome-wide genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** First adequately powered GWAS of a latent Common EF factor; identifies **112 genome-wide significant loci** implicating GABAergic and synaptic pathways rather than dopamine.  
* **Why care:** Validates Common EF as a genetically meaningful entity distinct from intelligence, while overturning the dopamine hypothesis of EF individual differences.

This paper is the single most important EF paper since 2019\. It validates the unity/diversity model at the molecular level—Common EF has a distinct genetic architecture from IQ, with differential associations to psychiatric disorders. The GABAergic finding (fast ionotropic synaptic transmission rather than slow neuromodulatory dopamine) challenges the dominant computational narrative linking EF to dopaminergic gating. The PGS predicted Common EF in an independent Colorado twin sample (β \= .171), though prediction remains well below twin heritability estimates (\~99%), illustrating the persistent missing-heritability gap. The UK Biobank battery was not designed for EF measurement, and only the Common EF factor—not updating-specific or shifting-specific—could be derived.

### **Paper 1.2**

**Feng, X., Zhang, Y., Chen, S., Sheng, J., Ye, Z., Feng, X., Liu, C., Cai, Y., Zhu, Z., Yu, J., Chen, C., Dong, Q., & Xue, G. (2022).** A cognitive neurogenetic approach to uncovering the structure of executive functions. *Nature Communications*, 13, 4588\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-022-32383-0

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / multi-modal (behavior \+ fMRI \+ genetics). **N \= 1,594** (behavioral), **1,100** (fMRI). Genetic polymorphism data present.  
* **Establishes:** A bifactor model with Common EF and shifting-specific components—but **not** updating-specific—showed maximal behavioral, neural, and genetic dissociations across 12 competing CFA models.  
* **Why care:** First study to use integrated gene-brain-behavior evidence as model-selection criterion rather than fit statistics alone.

This is consequential because it suggests the full three-component bifactor model is overspecified: updating-specific variance did not survive multimodal constraints. The two-component solution (common \+ shifting-specific) was more robust. This independently converges with Gustavson et al.'s midlife twin finding that only working-memory-span-specific variance survives beyond Common EF. The methodology—using neural and genetic data to adjudicate between statistically equivalent behavioral models—represents a paradigm shift for structural modeling.

### **Paper 1.3**

**Eisenberg, I. W., Bissett, P. G., Enkavi, A. Z., Li, J., MacKinnon, D. P., Marsch, L. A., & Poldrack, R. A. (2019).** Uncovering the structure of self-regulation through data-driven ontology discovery. *Nature Communications*, 10, 2319\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-019-10301-1

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / data-driven ontology. **N \= 522** adults, 37 behavioral tasks, 22 surveys. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** Task-based EF measures and self-report self-regulation measures show **near-zero empirical relationship**; factor structure within tasks was method-driven rather than replicating the inhibition/updating/shifting decomposition.  
* **Why care:** Poses a fundamental challenge: if EF tasks don't cohere with self-regulation measures or predict real-world outcomes, the theoretical foundation of EF as unified cognitive control is questionable.

This is the most radical challenge to the unity/diversity framework from a measurement perspective. Poldrack's group argues that Miyake factors may be artifacts of shared task-method variance. The paper provoked a direct exchange with Friedman and Banich. It doesn't disprove latent EF constructs, but forces the field to confront that factor structure is measurement-dependent—extending the Hedge et al. (2018) reliability critique.

### **Paper 1.4**

**Löffler, C., Frischkorn, G. T., Hagemann, D., Sadus, K., & Schubert, A.-L. (2024).** The common factor of executive functions measures nothing but speed of information uptake. *Psychological Research*, 88(4), 1092–1114. DOI: 10.1007/s00426-023-01924-7

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / computational modeling (drift-diffusion \+ SEM). **N \= 148**. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** When drift-diffusion modeling separates processing speed from executive-specific variance, Common EF is **entirely explained by speed of information uptake**; no coherent inhibition factor remains.  
* **Why care:** The most technically sophisticated challenge to the unity/diversity framework—arguing "Common EF" is not a control construct but an artifact of shared processing speed.

If Common EF equals processing speed, the vast literature linking EF to psychopathology and life outcomes may actually be about speed rather than control. The sample is small relative to the Miyake twin studies, and whether "drift rate" truly reflects "speed" versus "efficiency" is itself debated. This paper, along with work from Rey-Mermet and Frischkorn, forms a growing Zurich/Heidelberg empirical challenge to the Colorado model.

### **Paper 1.5**

**Hartung, J., Engelhardt, L. E., Thibodeaux, M. L., Harden, K. P., & Tucker-Drob, E. M. (2020).** Developmental transformations in the structure of executive functions. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*, 189, 104681\. DOI: 10.1016/j.jecp.2019.104681

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / developmental / network psychometrics. **N \= 1,019** children aged 7–15 (Texas Twin Project). No molecular genetic data (twin design).  
* **Establishes:** Network analysis reveals that associations among EF tasks **weaken with age**—inhibition's centrality decreases, supporting developmental differentiation from a unitary childhood structure toward adult multidimensionality.  
* **Why care:** First large-scale application of network psychometrics to developmental EF data; provides direct evidence that "inhibition \= Common EF" may be a developmental artifact.

The network approach sidesteps CFA assumptions about latent-to-observed causation. The finding that inhibition's centrality decreases with age maps onto the Miyake & Friedman observation that inhibition collapses into Common EF in adults—perhaps because by adulthood, inhibition is fully integrated into general control. The population-based twin sample and large N lend high credibility.

### **Paper 1.6**

**Menu, S., Bhosale, S., Schneider, D., Jarjat, G., & Barral, J. (2024).** Latent network analysis of executive functions across development. *Journal of Cognition*, 7(1), 33\. DOI: 10.5334/joc.355

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / developmental / combined SEM \+ network \+ latent variable network. **N \= 1,019** (reanalysis of Texas Twin Project data). No molecular genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** All three analytical approaches (SEM, network, latent variable network) converge on developmental differentiation; **seven competing latent variable models produce similar fit after age 10**.  
* **Why care:** Introduces latent variable network models to EF literature and shows that behavioral data alone may be insufficient to adjudicate between structural models—supporting the Feng et al. (2022) argument for multimodal constraints.

### **Paper 1.7**

**Karr, J. E., Rodriguez, J. E., Rast, P., Goh, P. K., & Martel, M. M. (2023).** A network analysis of executive functions in children and adolescents with and without ADHD. *Child Psychiatry and Human Development*, 55(6), 1600–1610. DOI: 10.1007/s10578-023-01518-9

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / clinical comparison / network analysis. **N \= 282** (141 ADHD, 141 controls), ages 13–17. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** Children with ADHD show altered EF *structure*—not just lower levels—with shifting less central and inhibition more dominant, resembling the network topology of younger typically developing children.  
* **Why care:** Demonstrates that EF structure differs in psychopathology, supporting delayed-maturation hypotheses and showing network analysis detects abnormalities invisible to mean comparisons.

### **Synthesis for structural models**

The unity/diversity model is broadly correct as data reduction but its theoretical interpretation is contested. **Three key modifications are emerging**: the full three-factor bifactor model is likely overspecified (two components may suffice); Common EF may partly or wholly reflect processing speed rather than goal maintenance; and EF structure is age-dependent, undifferentiated in childhood, increasingly multidimensional through adolescence. No alternative has achieved the dominance of the unity/diversity framework—the major emerging approaches (network psychometrics, DDM decomposition, gene-brain-behavior integration) complement rather than replace it.

## **2\. Computational phenotyping is the dominant measurement innovation**

The most consequential measurement shift since 2019 is not a new task but a new way of analyzing old tasks: applying formal cognitive models—particularly drift-diffusion models—to extract process parameters that outperform traditional scores for prediction and clinical classification.

### **Paper 2.1**

**Weigard, A., & Sripada, C. (2021).** Task-general efficiency of evidence accumulation as a computationally defined neurocognitive trait. *Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science*, 1(1), 5–15. DOI: 10.1016/j.bpsgos.2021.02.001

* **Design:** Conceptual/empirical synthesis (UCLA Phenomics dataset, **N \= 272** including clinical groups). No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** Proposes **efficiency of evidence accumulation (EEA)**—task-general drift rate from sequential sampling models—as a single computational dimension replacing the inhibition/updating/shifting fractionation.  
* **Why care:** This is the conceptual manifesto for computational phenotyping in EF; argues that subtraction-based conflict scores may be psychometrically inferior to model-derived parameters.

Weigard and Sripada argue persuasively that the unity/diversity framework confounds task variance with latent construct variance, and that drift rate offers a biologically grounded alternative. The implications are profound: if EEA is the real driver, then decades of clinical EF research using difference scores may need reinterpretation.

### **Paper 2.2**

**Weigard, A., Angstadt, M., Taxali, A., Heathcote, A., Heitzeg, M. M., & Sripada, C. (2024).** Flexible adaptation of task-positive brain networks predicts efficiency of evidence accumulation. *Communications Biology*, 7(1), 801\. DOI: 10.1038/s42003-024-06506-w

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / computational \+ fMRI. **N \= 5,198** (ABCD Study \~4,500 youth; Human Connectome Project \~700 adults). Genetic data available in ABCD but not the focus.  
* **Establishes:** Frontoparietal and dorsal attention network **flexibility**—higher activation under high demand, lower under low demand—explains **36–39% of individual variance** in EEA.  
* **Why care:** Bridges EEA from behavioral parameter to neurocomputational trait with identified neural substrates in massive samples.

The "divergent activation" finding—that the most efficient accumulators activate task-positive networks more under demand but *less* under easy conditions—shows that efficiency is appropriately scaled activity, not just more activity. This transforms EEA from a behavioral curiosity into a viable biomarker target.

### **Paper 2.3**

**Servant, M., & Evans, N. J. (2020).** A diffusion model analysis of the effects of aging in the Flanker Task. *Psychology and Aging*, 35(6), 831–849. DOI: 10.1037/pag0000546

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / computational. Standard aging sample (\~40–60 participants). No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** First application of conflict-specific diffusion models (DMC and Shrinking Spotlight) to the flanker paradigm, decomposing the **time-course of attentional selection within individual trials**.  
* **Why care:** The flanker is the most widely used EF task in large-scale studies (NIH Toolbox, ABCD); these models create entirely new phenotypes from the same data—flanker suppression rate, spotlight width and shrinkage speed, nondecision time.

This is a methods breakthrough. Older adults show enhanced peripheral processing (stronger attentional engagement), not just slower processing—a distinction invisible to standard analyses.

### **Paper 2.4**

**Feldman, J. S., & Huang-Pollock, C. (2021).** Slow drift rate predicts ADHD symptomology over and above executive dysfunction. *Child Neuropsychology*, 27(6), 834–855. DOI: 10.1080/09297049.2021.1902490

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / computational. **N \= 201** children aged 8–12. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** When both traditional EF and DDM-derived drift rate are entered into structural equation models predicting ADHD, **drift rate continues to predict ADHD but traditional EF does not**.  
* **Why care:** Direct demonstration that computational decomposition provides clinically meaningful information that standard EF metrics miss.

The methodological sophistication is notable: bifactor model of ADHD, latent variables for both EF and drift rate, and structural equation modeling testing incremental validity. The result—drift rate "knocks out" traditional EF in predicting ADHD—is a striking empirical confirmation of the Weigard & Sripada thesis.

### **Paper 2.5**

**Shen, C., Calvin, O. L., Rawls, E., Redish, A. D., & Sponheim, S. R. (2024).** Clarifying cognitive control deficits in psychosis via drift diffusion modeling and attractor dynamics. *Schizophrenia Bulletin*, 50(6), 1357–1370. DOI: 10.1093/schbul/sbae014

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / computational / family design. **N \= 250** (122 psychosis, 78 first-degree relatives, 50 controls). Family design provides genetic risk information.  
* **Establishes:** DDM parameters **differentiate clinical groups better than conventional behavioral measures** using explainable ML; first-degree relatives show intermediate computational phenotypes (prolonged nondecision times) that may serve as endophenotypes.  
* **Why care:** First head-to-head comparison showing computational parameters beat conventional measures for clinical classification in psychosis.

The family design is key: intermediate computational phenotypes in relatives suggest that prolonged nondecision time indexes genetic liability to psychosis, making DDM parameters candidate endophenotypes.

### **Paper 2.6**

**Dali, G., Poulton, A., Chen, L. P. E., & Hester, R. (2024).** Extended ambulatory assessment of executive function. *Journal of Clinical and Experimental Neuropsychology*, 46(5), 436–448. DOI: 10.1080/13803395.2024.2364396

* **Design:** Longitudinal (4 weeks) / methods-validation. **N \= 283**. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** Validates smartphone-delivered n-Back and Stop-Signal Tasks with **within-person reliability** (ICC .29–.73) across 4 weeks of ambulatory assessment.  
* **Why care:** Creates a fundamentally new phenotype: *within-person variability* in EF across time and context, enabling intensive longitudinal designs impossible with traditional assessment.

The 66–89% compliance rates demonstrate feasibility. The moderate ICCs suggest meaningful within-person variance that could be linked to contextual factors (sleep, stress, substance use). This kind of dense time-series EF data enables dynamic structural equation modeling approaches.

### **Paper 2.7**

**Weizenbaum, E. L., Fulford, D., Torous, J., Pinsky, E., Kolachalama, V. B., & Cronin-Golomb, A. (2021).** Smartphone-based neuropsychological assessment in Parkinson's disease. *Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society*, 28(4), 401–413. DOI: 10.1017/S1355617721000503

* **Design:** Longitudinal (10 days, 5×/day) / methods-validation. **N \= 27** (PD patients; small N justified by clinical population). No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** Real-world contextual factors (location, exercise, medication state, sleepiness) **systematically predict moment-to-moment EF variability** in a clinical population.  
* **Why care:** Transforms EF from a trait-like construct to a dynamic, context-dependent process, enabling "just-in-time" adaptive interventions.

### **Paper 2.8**

**Eng, C. M., Tsegai-Moore, A., & Fisher, A. V. (2024).** Incorporating evidence-based gamification and machine learning to assess preschool executive function. *Brain Sciences*, 14(5), 451\. DOI: 10.3390/brainsci14050451

* **Design:** Within-subjects / methods-validation / feasibility. Preschoolers ages 3–5 (\~20–30). No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** A gamified flanker task with **ML-driven adaptive difficulty** preserves psychometric validity while solving the critical floor-effect problem that makes standard EF tasks unusable with very young children.  
* **Why care:** Solves a measurement problem that has hampered preschool EF research for decades—high error rates and disengagement lead to \>10% data loss in standard tasks.

### **Synthesis for new phenotypes**

Three innovation streams dominate. **Computational decomposition** (Papers 2.1–2.5) applies formal cognitive models to extract process parameters from existing tasks, creating new phenotypes without new stimuli—just better math. **Ambulatory assessment** (Papers 2.6–2.7) captures within-person EF variability and context-dependence impossible to measure in lab settings. **Gamified adaptive assessment** (Paper 2.8) solves floor/ceiling effects in developmental populations. Truly passive sensing (predicting EF from typing patterns, GPS) remains aspirational; the field is in an active smartphone testing phase, not yet a passive digital phenotyping phase.

## **3\. EF and intelligence diverge genetically across development**

The EF–intelligence overlap is one of the most productive debates in differential psychology. The field has converged on a developmental differentiation account: EF and IQ are **genetically near-identical in childhood (rG ≈ .86) but substantially separable in adulthood (rG ≈ .44)**. The updating/working memory component is consistently the primary bridge between the two constructs.

### **Paper 3.1**

**Freis, S. M., Morrison, C. L., Lessem, J. M., Hewitt, J. K., & Friedman, N. P. (2022).** Genetic and environmental influences on executive functions and intelligence in middle childhood. *Developmental Science*, 25(1), e13150. DOI: 10.1111/desc.13150

* **Design:** Twin study (embedded in ABCD). **N \> 11,000** children (9–10 years), including **749 twin pairs**. Classical twin design.  
* **Establishes:** In middle childhood, Common EF and IQ have genetic correlation **rG \= .86, not significantly different from 1.0**—genetically near-indistinguishable despite phenotypic separability.  
* **Why care:** Provides the childhood anchor for the developmental differentiation arc: whatever genes influence EF at age 10 also influence IQ and vice versa.

The phenotypic separability despite genetic unity implicates environmental factors (schooling, specific experiences) in creating the apparent distinctness of EF and IQ in children. This has direct implications for interpreting EF training studies: training may work through environmental differentiation of constructs that share all genetic variance.

### **Paper 3.2**

**Gustavson, D. E., Reynolds, C. A., Corley, R. P., Wadsworth, S. J., Hewitt, J. K., & Friedman, N. P. (2022).** Genetic associations between executive functions and intelligence: A combined twin and adoption study. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 151(8), 1745–1761. DOI: 10.1037/xge0001168

* **Design:** Twin-and-adoption. **N \= 1,238** (170 MZ pairs, 154 DZ pairs, 95 biological sibling pairs, 80 adoptive sibling pairs), mean age 33\. Classical behavioral genetic decomposition.  
* **Establishes:** In adults, Common EF and IQ are phenotypically correlated (r \= .49) and genetically correlated (**rG \= .44**)—substantially lower than the childhood rG of .86. Intelligence correlates most strongly with **Updating-specific** abilities (rG \= .69). Substantial nonadditive genetic effects (d² \= .45–.65) detected.  
* **Why care:** Strongest adult behavioral-genetic evidence that EF and intelligence are genetically distinct, not "g by another name."

The adoption component allows estimation of both additive and dominance genetic effects—a critical advance over twin-only designs. The large dominance genetic variance may explain why GWAS (which captures primarily additive variance) finds lower heritability than twin studies. The developmental pattern—rG declining from \~1.0 in childhood to \~0.44 in adulthood—is among the most important findings in the differential psychology literature.

### **Paper 3.3**

**Draheim, C., Tsukahara, J. S., Martin, J. D., Mashburn, C. A., & Engle, R. W. (2021).** A toolbox approach to improving the measurement of attention control. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 150(2), 242–275. DOI: 10.1037/xge0000783

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / latent-variable psychometric. **\~400** young adults. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** Replacing RT-based difference scores with accuracy-based adaptive attention control tasks yields a coherent latent factor that **fully mediates the WMC–fluid intelligence relationship**.  
* **Why care:** Demonstrates that prior failures to find coherent executive control factors (cf. Rey-Mermet et al., 2019\) were measurement artifacts, not evidence against a unitary attention control construct.

Full mediation—WMC-Gf correlation dropping to nonsignificance after accounting for attention control—suggests attention control may be the "active ingredient" explaining the positive manifold. This is methodologically transformative for the field, though the result depends on task selection and factor specification.

### **Paper 3.4**

**Rey-Mermet, A., Gade, M., Souza, A. S., von Bastian, C. C., & Oberauer, K. (2019).** Is executive control related to working memory capacity and fluid intelligence? *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 148(8), 1335–1372. DOI: 10.1037/xge0000593

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / latent-variable. **\~250** young adults per study. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** **No coherent latent factor** of executive control could be established from accuracy-based measures; WMC and Gf were unrelated to individual executive control measures.  
* **Why care:** The strongest empirical challenge to the EF-intelligence overlap hypothesis, arguing the overlap may be measurement artifact.

This paper and Draheim et al. (2021) are essential reading together. The resolution likely depends on task selection: Draheim's improved accuracy tasks (antisaccade, SACT) tap a construct that traditional accuracy measures do not, suggesting not all accuracy measures are created equal.

### **Paper 3.5**

**Burgoyne, A. P., Mashburn, C. A., Tsukahara, J. S., & Engle, R. W. (2022).** Attention control and process overlap theory. *Intelligence*, 91, 101629\. DOI: 10.1016/j.intell.2022.101629

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / latent-variable with higher-order g. **\~400** young adults. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** Attention control has the **highest loading on g** (.89) in a higher-order model spanning WMC, Gf, sensory discrimination, and processing speed, largely explaining the positive correlations between abilities.  
* **Why care:** Provides an empirical bridge between the executive attention framework (Engle) and process overlap theory (Kovacs & Conway), identifying attention control as the strongest candidate for the process driving the positive manifold.

### **Paper 3.6**

**Wang, T., Li, C., Ren, X., & Schweizer, K. (2021).** How executive processes explain the overlap between working memory capacity and fluid intelligence. *Journal of Intelligence*, 9(2), 21\. DOI: 10.3390/jintelligence9020021

* **Design:** Cross-sectional / latent-variable with fixed-links modeling. **N \= 215**. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** Executive processes account for **\~88% of the WMC-Gf overlap**, but the **Common EF factor**—not inhibition or shifting specifically—does the heavy lifting, partially contradicting process overlap theory's multi-process prediction.  
* **Why care:** First direct empirical test of whether POT's mechanism (multiple overlapping processes) or a unitary common EF factor better explains the WMC-Gf relationship.

### **Synthesis for EF–intelligence boundary**

Three theoretical camps remain active with no convergence: **(a) shared mechanism** (Engle/Burgoyne: attention control is the domain-general process underlying both EF and g), **(b) process overlap** (Kovacs/Conway: g is emergent from overlapping executive process sampling), and **(c) developmental differentiation** (Friedman group: EF and intelligence start genetically unified and separate through environmental experience). The updating/WMC component is consistently the primary bridge between EF and intelligence across twin, behavioral, and molecular genetic studies. Inhibition and shifting contribute far less. Measurement quality proves decisive: improvements in attention control measurement reveal full mediation of the WMC-Gf relationship, but this finding is contested.

## **4\. GABAergic pathways and nonadditive genetics reshape EF's molecular landscape**

The genetic architecture of EF has undergone a transformation since 2019\. The central findings are that **latent-variable GWAS dramatically outperforms single-task GWAS**, that EF's molecular biology runs through fast synaptic transmission rather than dopamine, and that large nonadditive genetic effects may explain the persistent missing-heritability gap.

### **Paper 4.1**

**Hatoum et al. (2023).** (See Paper 1.1 above for full citation.) The landmark GWAS. Key additional genetic findings: cEF was negatively genetically associated with ADHD, MDD, and schizophrenia even after controlling for IQ and speed. PGS predicted Common EF in an independent sample (β \= .171, partial r \= .14). SNP heritability was substantially below twin heritability, pointing to the missing heritability problem.

### **Paper 4.2**

**Harden, K. P., Engelhardt, L. E., Mann, F. D., Patterson, M. W., Grotzinger, A. D., Savicki, S. L., Thibodeaux, M. L., Freis, S. M., Tackett, J. L., Church, J. A., & Tucker-Drob, E. M. (2020).** Genetic associations between executive functions and a general factor of psychopathology. *Journal of the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry*, 59(6), 749–758. DOI: 10.1016/j.jaac.2019.05.006

* **Design:** Multivariate twin study. **N \= 1,913** child/adolescent twins (mean age 13.1), Texas Twin Project. No molecular genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** General EF (h² \= 86–92%) is robustly **negatively genetically correlated with a transdiagnostic p-factor** of psychopathology (genetic r \= −.20 to −.38).  
* **Why care:** Key paper linking EF genetics to the p-factor in youth, motivating the entire line of work asking whether EF is a cognitive mechanism for transdiagnostic psychiatric risk.

By modeling EF and g separately, Harden et al. show both index genetic risk for general psychopathology but through partially distinct pathways. The p-factor model outperforms specific-disorder models for capturing the EF–psychopathology genetic overlap.

### **Paper 4.3**

**Friedman, N. P., Hatoum, A. S., Gustavson, D. E., Corley, R. P., Hewitt, J. K., & Young, S. E. (2020).** Executive functions and impulsivity are genetically distinct and independently predict psychopathology. *Clinical Psychological Science*, 8(3), 519–538. DOI: 10.1177/2167702619898814

* **Design:** Multivariate twin study, two samples. **N \= 749 and N \= 761** with EF data (Colorado twin samples). Classical twin design.  
* **Establishes:** EFs and self-reported impulsivity are only **modestly genetically correlated (rA \= −.44 to .04)** and **independently predict externalizing psychopathology**. Common EF predicts externalizing but not internalizing; urgency predicts both.  
* **Why care:** Resolves a key conceptual confusion: laboratory EF and self-reported impulsivity are genetically separable, meaning molecular genetic studies need both measures.

This dissection of the "self-control" construct has major implications for psychiatric genetics: the finding that Common EF predicts externalizing but not internalizing psychopathology while urgency predicts both points to distinct genetic pathways from self-regulation to mental health.

### **Paper 4.4**

**Arnatkeviciute, A., Lemire, M., Morrison, C., Mooney, M., Ryabinin, P., Roslin, N. M., ... & Bellgrove, M. A. (2023).** Trans-ancestry meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies of inhibitory control. *Molecular Psychiatry*, 28(10), 4175–4184. DOI: 10.1038/s41380-023-02187-9

* **Design:** Trans-ancestry GWAS meta-analysis. **N \= 14,877** across 8 sites and four ancestries. Stop-signal task measures; GWAS array.  
* **Establishes:** First trans-ancestry GWAS of inhibitory control. **No genome-wide significant hits**, but SNP heritability \~8.2%. ADHD PRS predicted GoRT variability across ancestries.  
* **Why care:** Demonstrates that single-task EF GWAS need \>100,000 participants—latent-variable GWAS dramatically outperforms single-task approaches.

The null GWAS results at N \~15,000 starkly contrast with Hatoum et al.'s 112 loci at N \= 427,000 using a multi-task factor score. The trans-ancestry design is commendable and rare in cognitive GWAS.

### **Paper 4.5**

**Perry, L. C., Chevalier, N., & Luciano, M.; CHARGE Consortium. (2025).** GenomicSEM modelling of diverse executive function GWAS improves gene discovery. *Behavior Genetics*, 55(2), 71–85. DOI: 10.1007/s10519-025-10214-4

* **Design:** GenomicSEM factor analysis \+ GWAS on latent factors. Summary statistics from **11 EF GWAS** across 5 studies (effective N up to \~266,000).  
* **Establishes:** Factor analysis of diverse EF GWAS summary statistics recovered a **bifactor structure (Common EF \+ Working Memory–Specific)** at the molecular genetic level. Identified **20 new genomic risk loci** for Common EF and 4 for WM beyond constituent GWAS. Stop-signal showed no significant genetic relationship with other EF measures.  
* **Why care:** Demonstrates that GenomicSEM can combine heterogeneous EF GWAS—using different tasks, samples, and ages—to recover the unity/diversity structure at the molecular level and boost gene discovery power.

This is arguably the most methodologically sophisticated EF genetics paper to date. The failure of stop-signal to load on Common EF genetically is striking—it may reflect developmental instability of the task's genetic architecture. The WM-specific loci mapping to educational attainment but not general cognitive ability suggest a WM-specific pathway to educational outcomes.

### **Paper 4.6**

**Gustavson, D. E., Morrison, C. L., Mallard, T. T., Jennings, M. V., Fontanillas, P., Elson, S. L., Palmer, A. A., Friedman, N. P., & Sanchez-Roige, S. (2024).** Executive function and impulsivity predict distinct genetic variance in internalizing, externalizing, thought disorders, and compulsive disorders. *Clinical Psychological Science*, 12(4), 660–682. DOI: 10.1177/21677026231207845

* **Design:** GenomicSEM using GWAS summary statistics. Hatoum et al. (N \= 427,037) for Common EF; Sanchez-Roige et al. (N \> 100,000) for impulsivity; multiple psychiatric GWAS.  
* **Establishes:** At the molecular level, Common EF and impulsivity are only **modestly genetically correlated (r \= .13)**. Low EF is uniquely genetically associated with all four psychopathology factors, with the **strongest association to thought disorders (β \= .38)**.  
* **Why care:** Molecular genetics capstone confirming that EF and impulsivity are genetically distinct constructs with differential pathways to psychopathology.

The strong association between low EF and thought disorders echoes the cognitive deficit literature in psychosis. The differential patterns across psychopathology factors—EF broadly associated with all, urgency specifically with internalizing, sensation seeking with externalizing—provides an actionable genetic decomposition for psychiatric genomics.

### **Paper 4.7**

**Engelhardt, L. E., Briley, D. A., Mann, F. D., Harden, K. P., & Tucker-Drob, E. M. (2015).** Genes unite executive functions in childhood. *Psychological Science*, 26(8), 1151–1163. DOI: 10.1177/0956797615577209

* **Design:** Multivariate twin study. **N \= 505** twins/triplets (grades 3–8), Texas Twin Project. Classical twin design.  
* **Establishes:** Common EF was **100% heritable** in childhood; correlations among four EF domains are entirely attributable to shared genetic etiology.  
* **Why care:** First comprehensive multivariate behavioral genetic analysis of EF in childhood; established that the unity of EF is genetically mediated from middle childhood onward.

### **Synthesis for genetic architecture**

The field has established that **(1)** EF-specific GWAS works and identifies distinct biology from intelligence; **(2)** GABAergic/fast synaptic pathways, not dopamine, drive individual differences; **(3)** EF and IQ share virtually all genetic variance in childhood but diverge by adulthood; **(4)** the unity/diversity model holds at the molecular genetic level; **(5)** EF is a stronger transdiagnostic genetic correlate of psychopathology than IQ after controlling for shared variance; **(6)** EF and impulsivity are genetically distinct with independent pathways to mental illness; and **(7)** large nonadditive genetic effects may explain the missing-heritability gap.

## **5\. Circuit dissection moves far beyond "PFC does EF"**

The most exciting neuroscience of EF has moved to circuit-level specificity—identifying which projection types, neuromodulatory interactions, and non-neuronal cells contribute to which EF operations. The mediodorsal thalamus has emerged as a central player, and the dopamine-centric story is giving way to a multi-neuromodulator, multi-cell-type picture.

### **Paper 5.1**

**Lam, N. H., Mukherjee, A., Wimmer, R. D., Nassar, M. R., Chen, Z. S., & Halassa, M. M. (2024).** Prefrontal transthalamic uncertainty processing drives flexible switching. *Nature*, 637(8044), 127–136. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-024-08180-8

* **Design:** Animal model (tree shrews \+ mice), electrophysiology \+ computational modeling. Small N (species-appropriate).  
* **Establishes:** The mediodorsal thalamus independently represents **distinct sources of uncertainty** (cue vs. rule) and drives prefrontal reconfiguration during cognitive flexibility via a **transthalamic cingulate→MD→PFC pathway**.  
* **Why care:** Strongest demonstration that the thalamus actively "demixes" cortical signals, providing a low-dimensional communication channel for cognitive flexibility—not a passive relay.

This substantially advances the MD thalamus from "PFC helper" to independent computational node. The transthalamic pathway concept offers a new wiring diagram for cognitive flexibility: cingulate cortex routes through MD thalamus to reconfigure PFC.

### **Paper 5.2**

**Mukherjee, A., Lam, N. H., Wimmer, R. D., & Halassa, M. M. (2021).** Thalamic circuits for independent control of prefrontal signal and noise. *Nature*, 600(7887), 100–104. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-021-04056-3

* **Design:** Animal model (mice), optogenetic \+ chemogenetic \+ electrophysiology. Genetically targeted MD thalamic projections.  
* **Establishes:** Two molecularly distinct MD thalamic projections to PFC: a **D2-expressing projection amplifies signal** when inputs are sparse; a **GRIK4-expressing projection suppresses noise** when inputs conflict.  
* **Why care:** First demonstration that the brain has separate hardware for uncertainty-from-low-signal versus uncertainty-from-high-noise.

The D2-receptor enrichment of one projection provides a direct mechanism for how dopaminergic drugs might differentially affect signal amplification versus noise suppression in cognitive control—connecting thalamic and neuromodulatory literatures.

### **Paper 5.3**

**Mederos, S., Sánchez-Puelles, C., Esparza, J., Valero, M., Ponomarenko, A., & Perea, G. (2021).** GABAergic signaling to astrocytes in the prefrontal cortex sustains goal-directed behaviors. *Nature Neuroscience*, 24(1), 82–92. DOI: 10.1038/s41593-020-00752-x

* **Design:** Animal model (mice), optogenetics \+ genetic ablation \+ electrophysiology. C57BL/6 and GABA\_B receptor conditional knockout mice.  
* **Establishes:** PFC **astrocytes** receive GABAergic signaling via GABA\_B receptors that sustains low-gamma oscillations and supports working memory; optogenetic stimulation of astrocytes rescues WM deficits.  
* **Why care:** Astrocytes are now active computational participants in executive function, not passive support cells—opening an entirely new non-neuronal therapeutic target.

This fundamentally expands the circuit diagram for working memory by including glia as active regulators. The GABA\_B-astrocyte pathway provides a previously unknown inhibition-to-excitation conversion mechanism in PFC, connecting to the GABAergic theme from Hatoum et al.'s GWAS.

### **Paper 5.4**

**Pagnier, G. J., Asaad, W. F., & Frank, M. J. (2024).** Double dissociation of dopamine and subthalamic nucleus stimulation on effortful cost/benefit decision making. *Current Biology*, 34(3), 655–660.e3. DOI: 10.1016/j.cub.2023.12.045

* **Design:** Human DBS patients / pharmacological \+ computational. Within-subject manipulation of DA medication × DBS on/off in Parkinson's disease patients.  
* **Establishes:** Clean **double dissociation**: dopaminergic medication alters sensitivity to benefits versus effort costs; STN-DBS lowers decision threshold (speed-accuracy tradeoff). Two basal ganglia interventions affect **separable computational parameters**.  
* **Why care:** First within-subject demonstration validating Frank's computational basal ganglia models—DA changes what you value, STN changes how much evidence you need before acting.

This resolves confusion about why both DA drugs and DBS produce impulsivity—they do so through entirely different computational mechanisms, with direct clinical relevance for managing impulsivity in Parkinson's.

### **Paper 5.5**

**Gangal, H., Xie, X., Huang, Z., et al. (2023).** Drug reinforcement impairs cognitive flexibility by inhibiting striatal cholinergic neurons. *Nature Communications*, 14, 3886\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-023-39623-x

* **Design:** Animal model (mice \+ rats), optogenetic \+ chemogenetic \+ rabies tracing \+ electrophysiology. Multiple experiments.  
* **Establishes:** Direct-pathway MSN axon collaterals that project to SNr for reinforcement **also inhibit local cholinergic interneurons** that mediate cognitive flexibility—shared hardware linking reward and control.  
* **Why care:** Reveals that reinforcement and cognitive flexibility are mechanistically linked through the same neurons, providing a neural architecture explanation for why addiction impairs executive function.

### **Paper 5.6**

**De Risi, M., Cavezza, D., Torromino, G., et al. (2025).** Cortico-striatal circuit mechanisms drive the effects of D1 dopamine agonists on memory capacity. *Nature Communications*, 16(1), 2615\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-025-57788-5

* **Design:** Animal model (mice), optogenetic \+ pharmacological \+ intracellular signaling. C57BL/6J \+ schizophrenia mouse model.  
* **Establishes:** The **inverted-U dopamine dose-response** for working memory arises from dose-dependent shifts between circuit nodes: low doses enhance striatal WMC via cAMP/PKA; high doses over-activate mPFC, which paradoxically shuts down striatum via PV interneuron recruitment.  
* **Why care:** First systems pharmacology account of the inverted-U, showing it's a circuit-level phenomenon rather than a single-region mechanism.

### **Paper 5.7**

**Kikumoto, A., Bhandari, A., Shibata, K., & Badre, D. (2024).** A transient high-dimensional geometry affords stable conjunctive subspaces for efficient action selection. *Nature Communications*, 15, 8513\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-52777-6

* **Design:** Human EEG \+ computational modeling / neural decoding. Healthy humans performing context-dependent action selection.  
* **Establishes:** Successful flexible action selection requires a transient **expansion of representational dimensionality** just before the response, creating a high-dimensional neural state that conjunctively encodes rules, stimuli, and responses. Entry into this state predicts trial accuracy.  
* **Why care:** Operationalizes what "control" looks like in neural population activity: not just PFC activation but a specific geometric expansion enabling linear separability of context-appropriate actions.

This bridges computational theory (high-dimensional representations, neural state spaces) with empirical data on cognitive control. The forced-response paradigm probes the temporal evolution of control representations, offering a new metric for control quality applicable across disorders.

### **Paper 5.8**

**Galvin, V. C., Yang, S., Lowet, A. S., Datta, D., Duque, A., Arnsten, A. F. T., & Wang, M. (2021).** M1 receptors interacting with NMDAR enhance delay-related neuronal firing and improve working memory. *Current Research in Neurobiology*, 2, 100016\. DOI: 10.1016/j.crneur.2021.100016

* **Design:** Animal model (rhesus macaques \+ rats), single-neuron iontophoretic pharmacology \+ behavioral pharmacology. Single-neuron recording in dlPFC.  
* **Establishes:** Muscarinic M1 receptors have **permissive interactions with NMDA receptors** in dlPFC layer III working memory circuits; M1 PAMs prevent NMDA blockade effects on persistent firing and WM behavior.  
* **Why care:** Identifies a specific neuromodulatory interaction (ACh M1 × glutamate NMDA) at the exact cellular locus (layer III dlPFC recurrent circuits) that maintains working memory, providing mechanistic rationale for M1 agonist therapy in schizophrenia.

### **Synthesis for circuits**

The field has moved decisively beyond "PFC does EF." **The mediodorsal thalamus** is now established as an independent computational node for cognitive flexibility with molecularly distinct projections for signal amplification versus noise suppression (Halassa lab). **Astrocytes** are active regulators of working memory through GABAergic signaling (connecting to the GWAS GABA finding). **Basal ganglia gating** has been mechanistically validated in human DBS patients (Frank lab). The **inverted-U dopamine function** is a circuit-level phenomenon, not a single-region effect. And **representational geometry**—high-dimensional conjunctive coding—provides a new computational framework for understanding what control actually is at the neural population level.

## **6\. A canonical developmental trajectory emerges, but component-level timing remains debated**

The most important developmental EF finding since 2019 is a massive multi-dataset demonstration that EF follows a canonical nonlinear trajectory—rapid development during ages **10–15**, smaller gains at **15–18**, and stabilization by **18–20**—that appears domain-general rather than component-specific.

### **Paper 6.1**

**Tervo-Clemmens, B., Calabro, F. J., Parr, A. C., Fedor, J., Foran, W., & Luna, B. (2023).** A canonical trajectory of executive function maturation from adolescence to adulthood. *Nature Communications*, 14(1), 6922\. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-023-42540-8

* **Design:** Multi-dataset (2 longitudinal, 2 cross-sectional). **N \= 10,766**, ages 8–35, **23 measures from 17 tasks**.  
* **Establishes:** EF follows a **canonical nonlinear trajectory** reproducible across datasets and assessments; age effects are captured by domain-general processes rather than separable subcomponents.  
* **Why care:** The most comprehensive charting of EF maturation to date; provides a normative "growth chart" for cognitive development with implications for juvenile justice, education, and psychiatric vulnerability.

The finding that EF subcomponents do not develop at significantly different rates challenges the expectation from adult structural models that inhibition, updating, and shifting would have distinct developmental timelines. This aligns with the childhood data showing a more unitary EF structure that differentiates only later.

### **Paper 6.2**

**Baum, G. L., Cui, Z., Roalf, D. R., et al. (2020).** Development of structure-function coupling in human brain networks during youth. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 117(1), 771–778. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1912034117

* **Design:** Cross-sectional \+ longitudinal. **N \= 727** (ages 8–23), including **294 longitudinal** (Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort). Diffusion-weighted imaging \+ resting-state fMRI.  
* **Establishes:** Structure–function coupling in transmodal cortex (especially rostrolateral PFC) **increases with age** and partially mediates age-related improvements in executive performance.  
* **Why care:** Provides a neural mechanism—white matter to functional connectivity coupling—through which adolescent brain maturation supports EF gains.

### **Paper 6.3**

**Xie, C., Xiang, S., Shen, C., et al. (2023).** A shared neural basis underlying psychiatric comorbidity. *Nature Medicine*, 29(5), 1232–1242. DOI: 10.1038/s41591-023-02317-4

* **Design:** Longitudinal (IMAGEN cohort, adolescence to young adulthood). Validated in ADHD-200 and Stratify clinical samples.  
* **Establishes:** A neuropsychopathological factor underlying psychiatric comorbidity represents **unified, genetically determined, delayed PFC development** leading to poor EF.  
* **Why care:** Bridges behavioral, neuroimaging, and genetic evidence showing that delayed PFC maturation → poor EF → psychiatric comorbidity, unifying developmental trajectory and clinical outcome.

This paper elegantly connects the developmental and clinical literatures. The NP factor is reproducible across multiple developmental periods and generalizable across task-based and resting-state connectomes, providing a neural-developmental bridge to the p-factor of psychopathology.

### **Paper 6.4**

**Broomell, A. P. R. & Bell, M. A. (2022).** Longitudinal development of executive function from infancy to late childhood. *Cognitive Development*, 63, 101229\. DOI: 10.1016/j.cogdev.2022.101229

* **Design:** Longitudinal (6 time points: 5 months to 9 years). **N \= 410**. No genetic data.  
* **Establishes:** Despite heterotypic measurement challenges, **toddler EF (from \~24 months) is a moderately stable predictor** of later EF through age 9; infant measures show weaker prediction.  
* **Why care:** One of the longest-spanning developmental EF studies from infancy; demonstrates meaningful individual differences are measurable and stable from toddlerhood.

### **Paper 6.5**

**Freis et al. (2022)** and **Engelhardt et al. (2015)** (see Papers 3.1 and 4.7 above) provide the genetic developmental foundation: Common EF is 100% heritable from middle childhood, genetically near-identical to IQ at age 9–10, but differentiating by adulthood.

### **Paper 6.6**

**Harden et al. (2020)** (see Paper 4.2 above) establishes that EF indexes genetic risk for transdiagnostic psychopathology in youth, with the critical developmental implication that the **10–15 age window** of rapid EF development (Tervo-Clemmens et al.) overlaps with the period of maximal psychiatric vulnerability.

### **Synthesis for development**

The developmental picture is increasingly clear in its broad strokes: **rapid nonlinear EF development from 10–15**, stabilization by 18–20, high heritability throughout, and a domain-general trajectory rather than component-specific timelines. The genetic architecture shifts from EF-IQ indistinguishability in childhood to clear separation by adulthood. PFC structure-function coupling maturation provides a neural mechanism, and delayed PFC maturation links directly to psychiatric comorbidity via the p-factor. Sensitive periods remain hypothesized but not yet precisely delineated.

## **7\. The labs and researchers shaping the field**

The executive function research landscape is dominated by a remarkably interconnected set of labs, most linked through training lineages or collaborative networks. The field's center of gravity sits at the intersection of behavioral genetics, developmental psychology, and computational neuroscience.

**Naomi Friedman** (University of Colorado Boulder, Institute for Behavioral Genetics) is the single most influential active researcher. With Miyake, she developed the unity/diversity model; she has since extended it to behavioral genetics (showing \~100% heritability), molecular genetics (the landmark GWAS with Hatoum), and the psychopathology interface. Her lab's work spans structural modeling, twin studies, GWAS, and GenomicSEM—an unusual methodological range.

**K. Paige Harden and Elliot Tucker-Drob** (University of Texas Austin) run the Texas Twin Project and have produced the key papers connecting EF genetics to the p-factor of psychopathology, developmental differentiation, and gene-environment interplay. Their students—**Laura Engelhardt, Jeanette Hartung, Samantha Freis**—are emerging as independent voices.

**Alexander Hatoum** (Washington University St. Louis, formerly Colorado) conducted the landmark EF GWAS and has extended this to addiction genetics and the psychopathology interface. **Daniel Gustavson** has produced the key adult twin/adoption work on EF-intelligence separation and the GenomicSEM extension to psychopathology.

**Andrew Grotzinger** (University of Colorado Boulder) developed Genomic SEM applications to psychiatric genetics that are now standard tools in the field.

**Beatriz Luna** (University of Pittsburgh) leads the developmental cognitive neuroscience of EF, with Tervo-Clemmens producing the canonical trajectory paper.

**David Badre and Michael Frank** (Brown University) anchor the computational neuroscience of cognitive control—Badre on representational geometry and hierarchical PFC organization, Frank on basal ganglia computational models validated in human DBS patients.

**Michael Halassa** (MIT) has established the mediodorsal thalamus as a genuine computational node for cognitive flexibility, with two *Nature* papers rewriting the thalamic contribution to EF.

**Amy Arnsten** (Yale) continues to produce the most mechanistically precise primate neurophysiology on neuromodulatory control of PFC working memory circuits.

**Randolph Engle** (Georgia Tech, now emeritus) and his students—**Christopher Draheim, Jason Tsukahara, Alexander Burgoyne**—have driven the attention control measurement revolution and the EF-intelligence boundary debate from the psychometric side.

**Alexander Weigard and Chandra Sripada** (University of Michigan) are building the computational phenotyping framework (EEA) that could ultimately replace the inhibition/updating/shifting taxonomy.

**Klaus Oberauer and Alodie Rey-Mermet** (University of Zurich) represent the most persistent empirical challenge to EF as a coherent individual-differences construct, arguing from the European psychometric tradition.

Cross-disciplinary impact is highest for papers combining genetics with cognitive structure (Hatoum, Friedman, Harden) and for circuit work that validates computational predictions (Halassa, Frank). The **ABCD Study** is the most heavily used dataset, appearing in structural, genetic, developmental, and neuroimaging analyses of EF. The **UK Biobank** powers the molecular genetics. **GenomicSEM** (Grotzinger/Tucker-Drob) is the methodological innovation enabling multi-trait, multi-GWAS integration.

## **Conclusion: five things this field is actually figuring out**

First, **the unity of EF is real and genetic**—Common EF is heritable, genetically coherent, and molecularly distinct from intelligence—but its theoretical interpretation remains uncertain. Whether Common EF reflects goal maintenance, processing speed, or evidence accumulation efficiency is the field's central unresolved question.

Second, **the neurochemical story has shifted from dopamine to GABA**. The largest GWAS implicates fast synaptic transmission pathways, astrocytic GABAergic signaling sustains PFC working memory, and the thalamic circuits that organize cognitive flexibility rely on molecularly distinct cell types rather than diffuse neuromodulation. Dopamine remains important at the circuit level (inverted-U, basal ganglia gating) but is no longer the primary molecular genetic story.

Third, **EF and intelligence undergo developmental differentiation**: genetically indistinguishable at age 10, substantially separable by age 33\. This is among the most important findings in differential psychology and implies that environmental experience progressively differentiates these constructs from a common genetic foundation.

Fourth, **computational phenotyping is replacing the inhibition/updating/shifting taxonomy** at the measurement level. Drift-diffusion model parameters—especially evidence accumulation efficiency—outperform traditional EF scores for clinical prediction, show neural correlates in massive samples, and offer a biologically grounded alternative to subtraction-based conflict scores.

Fifth, **EF is emerging as the strongest cognitive genetic correlate of transdiagnostic psychopathology**. After controlling for intelligence, Common EF is uniquely negatively genetically associated with virtually all major psychiatric disorders, with the strongest link to thought disorders. This positions EF genetics at the center of psychiatric genomics, not as a peripheral cognitive curiosity but as a core mechanism linking neurocognitive variation to mental health.
