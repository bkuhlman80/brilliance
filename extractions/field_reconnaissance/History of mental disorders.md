# **Historical phenomenology of mental disorders: a systematic reconnaissance**

**The phenomenological record of mental disorder is neither uniformly ancient nor uniformly modern.** Three disorder clusters — psychosis, melancholia, and hysteria/conversion — have continuous behavioral descriptions spanning at least 3,500 years across multiple civilizations, while others (autism-like social deficits, antisocial personality) have no clear phenomenological precursor before the 19th century. The evidence establishes a rough hierarchy: disorders with strong neurobiological substrates show cross-cultural and transhistorical stability in their behavioral descriptions, while disorders whose identification depends on social norms or subjective self-report emerge as recognizable entities only when the relevant conceptual and institutional frameworks develop. This finding holds regardless of whether one interprets it through a realist or constructionist lens — the phenomenological record itself is what it is.

This report synthesizes primary historical sources, specialist historiography, epidemiological time-series data, cross-cultural psychiatric studies, and archaeological evidence. It applies the anti-confirmation-bias firewall specified in the research brief: reporting what the evidence shows, treating absence of evidence as a finding, and distinguishing phenomenological descriptions from the theoretical frameworks contemporaries used to explain them.

## **1\. Phenomenological continuity across the diagnostic landscape**

### **The ancient triad: psychosis, melancholia, and hysteria**

Three disorder clusters have **unbroken descriptive traditions from antiquity to the present**, though the specific symptom groupings within each have shifted at identifiable transition points.

**Psychosis and thought disorder.** The earliest known phenomenological descriptions of psychotic symptoms appear in Babylonian diagnostic cuneiform texts from the First Babylonian Dynasty (\~1800–1600 BCE), published by Köcher (BAM series, 1964\) and analyzed by Reynolds and Kinnier Wilson (Brain, 2014). These texts describe paranoid delusions (fear of persecution by "god, king, superiors and city elders"), visual hallucinations (seeing dogs, gazelles), disorganized behavior, hyposexuality, and religiosity. Critically, Babylonian descriptions are **entirely behavioral and observational** — they contain no subjective accounts of inner experience, rumination, or feelings. This constitutes a genuine phenomenological difference from modern descriptions, not merely a gap in the record. The Hippocratic Corpus (\~5th–4th century BCE) introduced the tripartite classification of phrenitis (acute febrile psychosis/delirium), mania (chronic afebrile agitation with delusions), and melancholia. Aretaeus of Cappadocia (1st–2nd century CE) described auditory hallucinations, behavioral disinhibition, and emotional flattening under the broad category of "mania." Galen (2nd century CE) provided case histories of isolated hallucinations in otherwise cognitively intact individuals. The Indian Charaka Samhita (\~2nd century BCE–2nd century CE) described *unmada* (psychosis) with incoherent speech, thought blocking, loosening of associations, echolalia, and false perceptions across five subtypes. Chinese sources (Huangdi Neijing, \~3rd century BCE–2nd century CE) distinguished *dian* (depressive psychosis) from *kuang* (manic/wild behavior). Islamic Golden Age physicians, especially al-Razi (9th century) and Avicenna (980–1037), classified permanent psychosis (*al-junun al-thābet*) as distinct from symptomatic/organic mental disorders and described hallucinations and delusions as core features.

A major contested question is whether schizophrenia — as a specific syndromal cluster distinct from mania and melancholia — existed before the 19th century. Individual psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized behavior) were continuously described. The specific Kraepelinian grouping of chronic deteriorating psychosis (dementia praecox) was genuinely new in 1899 — not merely a relabeling. Berrios and others have argued that accounts meeting strict modern criteria for schizophrenia are rare before 1800, though the first unequivocal case description (James Tilly Matthews, by John Haslam) dates to 1810\. This is the single most debated phenomenological continuity question in the field (see Section 2 for the historiographic debate).

**Confidence: HIGH** for continuity of individual psychotic symptoms across millennia; **CONTESTED** for whether schizophrenia as a specific syndrome existed before the 19th century.

**Melancholia and depression.** Melancholia shows the most continuous descriptive tradition of any disorder cluster. A Babylonian medical tablet (BAM 234, \~2nd millennium BCE) describes what Reynolds and Kinnier Wilson (JRSM, 2013\) interpret as "agitated depression with biological features including insomnia, anorexia, weakness, impaired concentration," plus fear (*puluhtu*) and possibly suicidal tendencies (*zikurrudû*, though Assyriologist Al-Rashid argues this term may refer to a form of magic rather than suicide). The Hippocratic Corpus codified the pairing of "fear and despondency" lasting a long time as the hallmark of melancholia (Aphorisms VI.23). Aretaeus described melancholia as "a disorder of the spirit without fever, in which the mind is always fixed on one thought and adhered to it with sorrow and grief," including paranoid features. Rufus of Ephesus described characteristic delusions in melancholiacs (believing oneself to be a pot, believing one's skin was parchment). Ibn Imran (\~900 CE) wrote the oldest surviving monograph on melancholia, providing the clearest Islamic-era phenomenology including catatonic forms and seasonal patterns. Avicenna (11th century) distinguished early-phase melancholia (suspicions, causeless fear, quick anger, dizziness, tinnitus) from chronic-phase (moaning, deep suspicion, profound sadness, delusions).

**One critical oscillation** runs through the entire history: melancholia's defining feature alternated between affective symptoms (fear and sadness) and cognitive symptoms (fixed delusions). At Pinel's time (\~1800), melancholia was redefined as primarily delusional/cognitive rather than affective. Only in the late 19th–early 20th century did the affective component return as the defining feature. The medieval period introduced a major interpretive shift when sadness was reframed as *acedia* (spiritual sloth), a vice rather than illness, by Evagrius Ponticus (4th century) and Pope Gregory I.

**Confidence: HIGH** — direct primary source quotations survive from multiple independent traditions. The chain from Hippocrates through Islamic physicians through Burton's *Anatomy of Melancholy* (1621) to modern nosology is unbroken.

**Hysteria, conversion, and dissociation.** The Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus (\~1900 BCE, Egypt) is the oldest medical text describing behavioral abnormalities including chronic fatigue, difficulty seeing, choking sensations, mutism, and paralysis in women — all attributed to a wandering uterus. The Ebers Papyrus (\~1550 BCE) described seizure-like episodes and a sensation of suffocation (later called *globus hystericus*). The Hippocratic Corpus first used the term "hysteria" and described anxiety, suffocation, tremors, convulsions, and paralysis especially in "virgins, widows, and sterile women." Soranus of Ephesus (1st–2nd century CE) rejected the wandering uterus theory while maintaining the same symptom descriptions — a key demonstration that **phenomenological descriptions remained stable even as theoretical frameworks changed**. Galen further rejected the wandering uterus, attributed hysteria to retained fluids, and crucially argued that men could also suffer hysterical symptoms. Celsus differentiated epileptic seizures from hysterical pseudoseizures — a remarkably modern distinction.

The etiological framework shifted from wandering uterus to retained fluids to brain/nervous system (Thomas Willis, 1670s) to psychogenic mechanisms (Charcot, Janet, Freud), but the core phenomenological description — unexplained convulsions, paralysis, altered consciousness, suffocation sensation — remained remarkably stable across **nearly 4,000 years**. The medieval period reinterpreted these symptoms as demonic possession, but the behavioral descriptions (fits, altered consciousness, speaking in voices) persisted unchanged.

**Confidence: HIGH** — the most phenomenologically stable cluster across history.

### **Mania and the bipolar connection**

Mania as medical concept (distinct from mythological fury) begins with the Hippocratic Corpus: chronic afebrile agitation with poor appetite, sleeplessness, irritability, and sometimes delusions. Ancient mania was **broader than modern mania** — it encompassed various states of psychomotor agitation and psychosis. Aretaeus made the pivotal observation that "melancholia is the commencement and a part of mania," linking the two as alternating phases of one illness — the first phenomenological description of what we now call bipolar cycling. Celsus described "unprovoked cheerfulness, laughter, and sudden bursts of incautious fury" with "grandiose beliefs" and insomnia. Avicenna described mixed states including "involuntary laughter, and even grandiose thoughts like imagining that one is king" in melancholic patients, and recognized anger as a transitional state between melancholia and mania. Falret (1851) and Baillarger (1854) named circular insanity as a distinct entity. Kraepelin (1899) unified all mood disorders under "manic-depressive insanity."

**Confidence: HIGH** for continuity. The mania-melancholia link was first described by Aretaeus in the 1st–2nd century CE and has been continuously recognized since.

### **Anxiety: embedded in melancholia until modernity**

Anxiety was **not a separate diagnostic category** in the ancient or medieval world. Fear and worry were consistently described as components of melancholia from Hippocrates through Galen through Islamic medicine. Avicenna classified phobias as a distinct category within his medical taxonomy — an early partial separation. But anxiety as an independent clinical entity emerged only in the 18th–19th centuries, with Freud's distinction of anxiety neurosis from neurasthenia (1895) serving as a late milestone. Babylonian texts describe *puluhtu* (fear) and *hīp libbi* ("breaking of the mind," interpreted by Reynolds and Kinnier Wilson as panic attacks), but these appear within broader descriptions of melancholic/depressive states, not as standalone conditions.

**Confidence: HIGH** that anxiety phenomenology was continuously described; **HIGH** that it was not conceptualized as independent of melancholia until the modern era.

### **Disorders that emerged late: antisocial behavior, autism, and the limits of retrospective diagnosis**

**Antisocial behavior and psychopathy** has **no clear continuous pre-modern medical tradition**. Theophrastus (371–287 BCE) described antisocial character types in *Characters*, but these are literary/philosophical sketches, not medical descriptions. Philippe Pinel's *manie sans délire* (1801) — violence and rage without psychosis — is the foundational clinical description, but historian F.A. Whitlock argued there is "not the remotest resemblance" between Pinel's and Prichard's examples and modern psychopathy. The modern construct crystallized only with Cleckley's *The Mask of Sanity* (1941). The phenomenology requires a post-Enlightenment distinction between intellect and affect that did not exist in ancient/medieval frameworks.

**Autism-like social deficit** has the **weakest pre-modern evidence of any major category**. No clear pre-modern medical descriptions exist. Retrospective interpretations of medieval "changeling" stories, Russian "holy fools" (*yurodivy*), and the Wild Boy of Aveyron (1798–99) are speculative. Samuel Gridley Howe's 1848 report to the Massachusetts legislature described behaviors "breathtakingly recognizable" as autism among individuals classified as "idiots," but the first true clinical description was Grunya Sukhareva's 1925 paper on "Schizoid Psychopathy in Childhood" (recently recognized as preceding Kanner and Asperger). Many historians argue autism genuinely lacks a pre-modern phenomenological precursor because the concept requires modern frameworks of typical social development, theory of mind, and developmental norms.

**Intellectual disability** has a continuous **legal** tradition from Roman law (5th century BCE, *furiosus* vs. permanently incapable) through English common law (*Prerogativa Regis*, c. 1290s, distinguishing "born fools" from "lunatics"), but thin medical/phenomenological descriptions before the 19th century. Historian Irina Metzler's *Fools and Idiots?* is the first book devoted to intellectual disability in the pre-modern period. She found no hagiographic texts describing a "born fool" cured by saintly miracle — a striking absence suggesting the condition was recognized as permanent.

**Substance dependence** has rich ancient phenomenological descriptions for alcohol — Aristotle described *akrasia* (acting against better judgment) applied to drinking, and Pliny the Elder (1st century CE) provided vivid descriptions of tremulous hands, disturbed sleep, and compulsive drinking. But the concept of **loss of control** as the defining feature emerged only in the 17th century with Dutch physician Nicolaes Tulp (1641), adapting Calvinist theological models.

**Eating pathology** presents the most actively contested continuity question. The case of Blaesilla (d. 384 CE) — fatal self-starvation under Jerome's mentorship — and 181 documented cases of "holy fasting" women between the 13th–17th centuries (including Catherine of Siena, who began restricting food at 16, induced vomiting, and died at 32 from malnutrition) present striking behavioral parallels to modern anorexia nervosa. However, the scholarly **majority view** (Bynum 1987, Brumberg 1988, Vandereycken and Van Deth) holds that medieval *anorexia mirabilis* was a distinct phenomenon driven by religious motivations fundamentally different from modern body-image concerns. Richard Morton (1689) provided the first medical case report; Gull and Lasègue independently named "anorexia nervosa" in 1873\. Body image disturbance was not a diagnostic criterion until 1970\.

**Compulsive/repetitive behavior (OCD-like)** has a distinctive double tradition: sparse early medical descriptions (Aretaeus possibly described compulsive handwashing; Plutarch, 1st century CE, possibly described scrupulosity) alongside a rich **religious documentation** of scrupulosity from the 7th century onward. John Climacus (7th century) recorded a monk plagued by constant intrusive thoughts of blasphemy. Abu Zayd al-Balkhi (9th century) described obsessional disorders matching modern DSM-5 criteria with "transcultural diagnostic consistency." Medieval theologians (Gerson, 14th century; Antoninus of Florence, 15th century) described scrupulosity extensively; Antoninus notably recognized it sometimes had a "physical cause," recommending "medicine or other physical remedies." The transition from religious to medical framing occurred in the 17th–18th centuries; Esquirol classified OCD-like symptoms as "monomania" in 1838\.

### **What happened to the disappeared diagnoses**

Several historical diagnostic categories do not map onto any current DSM/ICD entity and reveal distinct fates:

* **Acedia** (4th century CE, Evagrius Ponticus): listlessness, undirected anxiety, inability to concentrate, urge to flee one's obligations. Not simply equivalent to depression — it lacks distinctive low mood and involves active turning away from recognized good rather than inability to perceive good. Better mapped to modern "avolition" than clinical depression. A **genuinely distinct phenomenological category with no exact modern equivalent**.  
* **Nostalgia** (coined 1688 by Johannes Hofer): severe, potentially lethal homesickness. Dismantled by advances in bacteriology and psychiatric classification; the phenomena **stopped being reported as pathological** once the conceptual framework changed.  
* **Neurasthenia** (George Beard, 1869): "nervous exhaustion" with fatigue, headaches, depression, digestive problems. Its symptoms were **fragmented** across chronic fatigue syndrome, generalized anxiety disorder, depression, and somatoform disorders. Still recognized in ICD (F48.0) and commonly diagnosed in China and Japan.  
* **Drapetomania** (Samuel Cartwright, 1851): the "mental illness" of enslaved people fleeing captivity. Universally recognized as pseudoscience — the "symptoms" were normal human behavior. The clearest case of a diagnosis that was entirely a product of social control.  
* **Hysteria** itself was **fragmented** into conversion disorder, somatization disorder, histrionic personality disorder, and dissociative disorders when the theoretical unifying framework (wandering uterus, then Freudian repression) was abandoned.

**Null finding worth reporting:** I searched for historical phenomenological descriptions that would map onto modern ADHD before the 20th century and found none that are clearly distinguishable from descriptions of childhood misbehavior, mania, or intellectual disability. George Still's 1902 Goulstonian Lectures are widely cited as the first recognizable ADHD description, though Sir Alexander Crichton (1798) described a condition involving "incapacity of attending with a necessary degree of constancy to any single object."

## **2\. The historiographic landscape and its fault lines**

### **The field's foundational texts**

The history of mental disorder phenomenology (as distinct from the history of psychiatry as an institution) centers on a small number of landmark works. **German E. Berrios's *The History of Mental Symptoms: Descriptive Psychopathology since the Nineteenth Century*** (Cambridge UP, 1996\) is universally regarded as the foundational reference. Roy Porter called it "a tour de force of multilingual erudition." The book traces descriptive categories from classical antiquity through modern operational criteria, organized by symptom type (disorders of perception, thought, delusions, obsessions, cognition, memory, consciousness, anxiety, affect, will, fatigue, catatonia, personality, self-harm) with an 80-page bibliography drawing on French, German, Italian, Spanish, and British sources. Its central argument — that mental symptoms are **"hybrid objects"** blending inchoate biological signals with cultural configurators rather than natural kinds — represents the "Cambridge model" of symptom formation. The book's primary limitation is its density, heavy European focus, and relatively thin coverage of pre-19th-century material.

**Karl Jaspers's *General Psychopathology*** (1913; 7th edition 1959\) is the founding document of phenomenological psychopathology. Jaspers distinguished *Verstehen* (empathic understanding of subjective experience) from *Erklären* (causal explanation), insisted on systematic first-person descriptions, and stressed that some psychotic experiences are fundamentally "un-understandable." Its influence persists through the EASE scale, the ipseity-disturbance model of schizophrenia, and the current Wellcome Trust–funded "Renewing Phenomenological Psychopathology" project.

**Henri Ellenberger's *The Discovery of the Unconscious*** (1970) demolished the "hero myth" around Freud by reinstating the full scientific and cultural context of dynamic psychiatry, tracing it from primitive healing through mesmerism to Janet, Freud, Adler, and Jung. The *British Journal of Psychiatry* (2014) called it "exemplary in showing the value of skepticism about received narratives."

Among historians of psychiatry as a discipline, **Andrew Scull** (*Madness in Civilization*, 2015\) provides the most comprehensive single-volume treatment, emphasizing sociological and critical perspectives. Scull dismisses purely constructionist views as "romantic nonsense" while insisting that madness exists as a phenomenon beyond medicine's explanatory reach. **Edward Shorter** (*A History of Psychiatry*, 1997\) offers a fiercely partisan biological-progressivist narrative, celebrating psychopharmacology's triumph over psychoanalysis. **Roy Porter** (*Madness: A Brief History*, 2002; *Mind-Forg'd Manacles*, 1987\) pioneered patients'-voice social history.

**Michel Foucault's *Madness and Civilization*** (1961) remains hugely influential despite near-universal criticism from empirical historians for factual errors, selective source use, and the exaggerated "Great Confinement" thesis. Jan Goldstein acknowledged Foucault's works hold "a special and central place in the historiography of psychiatry" while Colin Gordon (1990) argued most criticisms were based on the severely abridged English translation (missing 300 pages from the French original).

**Ian Hacking** developed the most philosophically sophisticated framework for understanding diagnostic categories through his concepts of **"looping effects"** (classifications interact with classified individuals, who change behavior in response, which changes the classification) and **"transient mental illnesses"** (disorders like dissociative fugue that appear only when a specific "ecological niche" of cultural conditions exists). His framework is now standard vocabulary in philosophy of psychiatry, though criticized for overstating the interactive/indifferent kinds distinction (Cooper 2004, Khalidi 2010\) and lacking an adequate account of the self (Tekin 2014).

### **The discovery-versus-construction debate**

The central historiographic fault line concerns whether modern diagnostic categories were **discovered** (they always existed and we finally named them) or **constructed** (they are products of specific social and medical contexts). This is not a simple binary — the major scholars occupy distinct positions along a continuum.

Berrios's Cambridge model holds that symptoms are constructed in specific historical-cultural contexts (19th-century alienism created the descriptive categories still in use) but insists there is a neurobiological substratum — symptoms are neither pure natural kinds nor pure social fictions. Hacking's "looping effects" framework occupies similar middle ground. Shorter and Torrey lean strongly toward realism (disorders as brain diseases that always existed). Foucault and his followers lean toward construction (psychiatric categories as instruments of social control).

The debate crystallizes around specific cases. The **schizophrenia recency hypothesis** (Edward Hare, BJP, 1988; expanded by E. Fuller Torrey) proposed that schizophrenia is a recent disease that emerged after \~1800, citing the rarity of clear pre-modern descriptions and the dramatic increase in institutionalized insanity during the 19th century. Hare himself acknowledged the evidence was "somewhat frail." Critics counter that absence of descriptions reflects diagnostic/conceptual limitations rather than true absence, and that WHO studies demonstrate schizophrenia at consistent prevalence (**0.07–0.14 per 1,000** using narrow criteria) across all cultures, including isolated indigenous groups. The debate remains unresolved; Robin Murray has stated "I expect to see the end of the concept of schizophrenia soon," reflecting growing recognition that the category may be heterogeneous.

The **anorexia continuity debate** pits Rudolph Bell (*Holy Anorexia*, 1985; 261 cases of female religious starvation 1206–1934) against Caroline Walker Bynum (*Holy Feast and Holy Fast*, 1987\) and Joan Jacobs Brumberg (*Fasting Girls*, 1988). The **majority view** among historians favors discontinuity — medieval food refusal was religiously motivated and lacked the body-image concerns central to modern anorexia nervosa. However, structural parallels (perfectionism, need for control, amenorrhea, sometimes fatal outcome) prevent clean resolution.

### **Standard periodization historians use**

Historians of psychiatry commonly employ the following epochs, though interpretive frameworks differ sharply (Scull: critical-sociological; Shorter: biological-progressivist; Foucault: discontinuity/rupture):

| Period | Approximate dates | Defining features |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Antiquity | \~500 BCE – \~500 CE | Hippocratic/Galenic humoral naturalism; madness as bodily imbalance |
| Medieval | \~500 – \~1500 | Religious/supernatural frameworks dominant; monastic care; Islamic hospital care |
| Renaissance/Early Modern | \~1500 – \~1700 | Emerging naturalism; Willis relocates hysteria to brain; Locke on mind |
| Classical Age / Great Confinement | \~1650 – \~1800 | Mass institutionalization; madness grouped with vagrancy; decline of humoralism |
| Asylum Era / Birth of Psychiatry | \~1790 – \~1900 | Pinel, Tuke, psychiatric professionalization; Kraepelin's nosology; Jaspers |
| Dynamic Psychiatry | \~1890 – \~1960 | Freud, Janet; psychoanalytic dominance (especially US) |
| Biological Revolution | \~1950 – present | Chlorpromazine (1952); DSM-III revolution (1980); psychopharmacology; neuroscience |

Key transitions marking these epochs include: the shift from religious to naturalistic frameworks (\~16th–18th centuries), Kraepelinian nosology organized by course and outcome (\~1890s), psychoanalytic dominance (\~1920s–1960s, predominantly American), and the DSM-III neo-Kraepelinian revolution (1980).

## **3\. Diagnostic adoption curves reveal distinct epidemiological signatures**

### **The shape of prevalence growth after diagnostic introduction varies dramatically by diagnosis**

Empirical prevalence data reveals that diagnostic adoption does **not** follow a single universal curve. Instead, at least five distinct patterns emerge:

**Exponential growth (no plateau yet): Autism.** CDC ADDM Network data shows prevalence in 8-year-olds rising from **1 in 150** (2000) to **1 in 31** (2022) — a 4-fold increase in measured prevalence over 22 years. Before ADDM tracking, pre-DSM-III prevalence was estimated at 2–5 per 10,000, implying a **60- to 150-fold increase** in identified prevalence since the 1970s. Growth has been approximately 10–15% biennially with no sign of plateauing. Key inflection points correspond to DSM-III (1980, introducing "infantile autism"), DSM-IV (1994, adding Asperger's disorder and broadening PDD-NOS), and the 1990 IDEA Act (adding "autism" as a special education category, producing a 25-fold increase in educational classification from 15,556 children in 1992 to 406,957 in 2011).

**S-curve with plateau: ADHD.** Parent-reported ever-diagnosed ADHD in U.S. children rose from **6.1%** (1997–98) to **11.0%** (2011), then plateaued at approximately **10–11%** through 2022\. Adult ADHD continues rising (6.0% current diagnosis among U.S. adults per 2023 NCHS data). Peak growth rate occurred 2003–2011, approximately 23–31 years after DSM-III introduction.

**Step function tied to external events: PTSD.** Civilian lifetime prevalence has remained relatively stable at **\~6.1%** since establishment. Military prevalence surges are tied to specific conflicts (Vietnam: 15.2% male theater veterans in original NVVRS; Iraq/Afghanistan: 4–17%). The pattern resembles a step function in military populations rather than a smooth curve.

**Stable flat line: Borderline personality disorder.** Community prevalence has remained at approximately **1–3%** since DSM-III (1980) introduction, with no significant epidemic pattern. BPD represents a diagnosis that was introduced and maintained stable prevalence — a useful comparison case.

**Rise-and-partial-fall (inverted U): Bipolar disorder in youth.** Outpatient visits with bipolar diagnosis in youth increased **40-fold** from 1994–95 (25 per 100,000) to 2002–03 (1,003 per 100,000). Rates then **substantially decreased** after mid-2000s, coinciding with concerns about overdiagnosis and DSM-5's introduction of Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder as an alternative. This is a rare case of a diagnostic "fad" that was partially corrected. Adult bipolar II shows slower, steadier uptake with **1.1–1.6%** lifetime prevalence.

**Gradual linear rise: Major depression.** Diagnosed prevalence nearly doubled from **6.1%** (1996) to **10.4%** (2015) in the U.S., tracking closely with SSRI availability (fluoxetine approved 1987). Social anxiety disorder underwent a parallel transformation from "a relatively rare and neglected condition" to "the third most prevalent psychiatric disorder" (7–13% lifetime) after renaming from "social phobia" in DSM-IV (1994) and FDA approval of paroxetine for the condition in 1999\.

### **What predicts how fast a diagnosis takes off**

**Pharmaceutical treatment availability is the strongest documented predictor.** SSRIs drove the expansion of depression and social anxiety disorder diagnoses; stimulant availability (especially long-acting formulations in the 1990s–2000s) accompanied ADHD growth; atypical antipsychotics enabled the pediatric bipolar epidemic. Allen Frances (DSM-IV chair) identified "3 market-driven diagnostic fads" in which ADHD tripled, bipolar doubled, and autism increased 20-fold despite DSM-IV's intended conservatism.

Other documented factors include: **school accommodation systems** (IDEA's 1990 addition of autism as a special education category was followed by a 25-fold increase in educational classification); **patient advocacy** (Vietnam veterans' advocacy directly drove PTSD's inclusion in DSM-III 1980); **media coverage** (ADHD media articles increased from 5,775 to 25,080 between comparable periods in 2014 and 2024); and **insurance/reimbursement coding** (a diagnosis must have a billing code to be reimbursable, creating immediate incentives upon DSM inclusion).

The typical **time lag from formal introduction to peak prevalence growth rate** ranges from **8–31 years**, with faster uptake when pharmaceutical treatments are available. Bipolar disorder in youth showed the fastest peak (\~8–10 years after DSM-IV); autism and ADHD showed the slowest (\~24–31 years after DSM-III), consistent with diagnoses requiring infrastructure development (screening programs, specialist training, school systems).

### **Diagnoses that failed and why**

Several proposed diagnostic categories were introduced but never gained traction: **Masochistic/Self-Defeating Personality Disorder** (DSM-III-R appendix 1987, dropped from DSM-IV after feminist criticism that it pathologized domestic violence victims); **Sadistic Personality Disorder** (also dropped); **Passive-Aggressive Personality Disorder** (relegated to appendix); **"Gross Stress Reaction"** (DSM-I 1952, dropped in DSM-II 1968, leaving a 12-year gap before PTSD). DSM-5 Section III categories that remain under study include Attenuated Psychosis Syndrome, Internet Gaming Disorder, and Nonsuicidal Self-Injury Disorder. Failed diagnoses share common features: political controversy, overlap with existing categories, or lack of a pharmaceutical treatment creating market demand.

A key meta-analytic finding challenges simple narratives of diagnostic inflation. Haslam et al. (Clinical Psychology Review, 2020; 123 studies, 476 risk ratios) found an **average risk ratio of exactly 1.00** comparing diagnostic rates across consecutive DSM editions — no overall diagnostic inflation from DSM-III to DSM-5 at the criteria level. However, specific disorders did inflate (ADHD and autism from DSM-III to DSM-III-R; eating disorders and GAD from DSM-IV to DSM-5). The distinction between **criteria-driven inflation** (modest/absent per meta-analysis) and **practice-driven inflation** (substantial, driven by awareness, marketing, and incentives) is critical.

### **Diagnostic substitution is well-documented for specific category pairs**

**Autism and intellectual disability.** Shattuck (Pediatrics, 2006\) found that growing autism prevalence in IDEA data (1994–2003) was associated with corresponding declines in mental retardation and learning disability classifications. Coo et al. (2008) found that switching from another classification to autism accounted for **51.9%** of the increase in educational records studied longitudinally. Bishop (2008) applied contemporary autism criteria to adults with history of developmental language disorder and found 32% qualified for ASD — direct evidence of diagnostic reclassification.

**Neurosis to specific disorders.** DSM-III's 1980 elimination of "neurosis" as a category and replacement with specific anxiety, mood, and somatoform disorders was the single largest act of diagnostic substitution in modern psychiatry.

**Hysteria to multiple successors.** DSM-III split hysteria into conversion disorder, somatization disorder, histrionic personality disorder, and dissociative disorders — a diagnostic fragmentation.

**Bipolar and depression/ADHD in youth.** The 40-fold increase in youth bipolar diagnoses (1994–2003) likely reflected reclassification of irritable/aggressive children previously diagnosed with ADHD, conduct disorder, or mood disorder NOS. DSM-5 introduced DMDD specifically to reverse this substitution.

## **4\. Cross-cultural evidence reveals a hierarchy of universality**

### **Psychosis is the most robustly universal phenotype**

The WHO International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia (IPSS, 1973; 1,202 patients, 9 countries) and Determinants of Outcome of Severe Mental Disorders study (DOSMeD, 1980s–92; 1,379 patients, 12 centers, 10 countries) established that schizophrenia is identifiable using standardized methods in all centers studied. Using a narrow definition, incidence rates were **0.07–0.14 per 1,000** across all settings — the variation was statistically **non-significant** (Jablensky et al., 1992). This is the strongest evidence for any mental disorder's universality. Symptom content varies culturally (delusion themes reflect local concerns), and subtype frequencies differ (catatonic subtype was more common in developing countries), but the core syndrome is recognized everywhere.

The "outcomes paradox" — **37% complete remission in developing countries vs. 15.5% in developed countries** — has been attributed to cultural factors (family support, social integration), though the finding has been critiqued for high attrition rates and selection bias. The International Study of Schizophrenia's 15–25 year follow-up found that only half of developing-country patients who initially had "best outcomes" maintained them.

### **Depression is universal but culturally reshaped**

Arthur Kleinman's landmark work (1977, 1982\) found that 87 of 100 neurasthenia patients in Hunan, China met DSM-III criteria for Major Depressive Disorder but presented with somatic complaints (fatigue, dizziness, pain) rather than psychological symptoms, and 70% responded to antidepressant medication. He proposed that Chinese patients "somatize" depression while Western patients "psychologize" it. However, more recent work by Ryder et al. (2008) complicated this narrative, finding that Chinese outpatients endorsed **both** more somatic and more cognitive symptoms when appropriately measured — challenging the simple somatization/psychologization dichotomy. BDI-II multigroup confirmatory factor analysis found strong measurement invariance across Chinese-heritage and European-heritage students, with Chinese-heritage students scoring **higher on cognitive symptoms** — directly contradicting the somatization hypothesis.

### **The hierarchy from most to least universal**

No single study produces a definitive ranking, but the evidence supports the following approximate hierarchy:

**Highly universal** (present in all cultures studied with consistent core features): psychosis/schizophrenia, intellectual disability, substance dependence, severe mood dysregulation (mania and melancholia). These share clear neurobiological substrates.

**Universal with major cultural reshaping** (identifiable globally but with significant variation in symptom emphasis, threshold, and meaning): depression, PTSD-like trauma responses (re-experiencing/intrusion symptoms appear universal; avoidance/numbing symptoms are more culturally variable per Hinton and Lewis-Fernández, 2011), ADHD (worldwide pooled prevalence **5.29%** per Polanczyk et al. 2007 meta-analysis of 102 studies; variability largely explained by methodology, not geography).

**Moderately culture-shaped** (identified cross-culturally but with significant variation in prevalence and presentation): autism (global prevalence \~1 in 100–127 per WHO/GBD 2021, but \>80% of studies from high-income countries and massive detection variation), anxiety disorders (specific phobia most prevalent disorder in WMH surveys at 6–12% lifetime; but expression and conceptualization vary — taijin kyofusho shows how social anxiety's "object" can be culturally reshaped), OCD.

**Substantially culture-bound** (prevalence and presentation strongly shaped by cultural context): anorexia nervosa (present globally but without "fat phobia" in some non-Western settings per Lee et al.; non-fat-phobic presentations driven by religious fasting or idiosyncratic nutritional beliefs), bulimia nervosa (Keel and Klump argued it is a **Western culture-bound syndrome**; in non-Western countries where it emerges, populations have been heavily exposed to Western thinness ideals), personality disorders (very few systematic cross-cultural studies; the concept of "personality disorder" is arguably itself Western).

DSM-5 (2013) formally acknowledged this complexity by dropping the term "culture-bound syndrome" entirely, replacing it with three concepts: **cultural syndromes**, **cultural idioms of distress**, and **cultural explanations of distress**. The glossary was reduced from 25 to 9 entries, and some culture-specific symptoms were integrated into main diagnostic categories (e.g., taijin kyofusho's "fear of offending others" added to social anxiety disorder criteria).

### **Symptom space is carved differently across cultures**

Cross-cultural factor analytic studies demonstrate that different cultures **do** group symptoms differently. Factor analysis of the CES-D in Chinese versus American samples found different center-periphery patterns: Chinese respondents made clearer discrimination between affective/interpersonal and somatic items, while Americans conflated them. An exploratory factor analysis across English, Spanish, Chinese, Russian, and Arabic speakers found both similarities and noteworthy differences — suicide-related items clustered separately in most cultures but loaded with worthlessness for Latin Americans; core depression and physical symptoms loaded together except in the Russian group.

However, large-scale studies using the Achenbach System (CBCL/YSR/TRF) across 20–44 societies found that the **8-syndrome factor structure fit well** across diverse societies, supporting dimensional universality of broad psychopathology domains. The emerging consensus is a **middle position**: broad dimensions of psychopathology (internalizing, externalizing, thought disorder) show cross-cultural stability, but the specific symptom configurations within these dimensions, the thresholds for dysfunction, and the meanings attributed to symptoms are culturally shaped.

## **5\. The prehistory question: what can and cannot be inferred**

### **Trepanation is widespread but its connection to mental illness is uncertain**

Trepanation is the oldest surgical procedure with archaeological evidence, dating to at least **6500 BCE** (Ensisheim burial, Alsace, France). Over **1,500 trepanned Neolithic skulls** have been found globally, with survival rates of 50–70% in some groups (evidenced by bone healing). The practice appeared on every inhabited continent. A significant proportion of trepanned skulls show **no prior injury**, suggesting non-traumatic motivations. Among the Kissii of Kenya, who practice trepanation today, motives include treatment for headaches, epilepsy, and "mental illness." Paul Broca (19th century) concluded that prehistoric trepanation was performed for conditions like epilepsy attributed to demonic possession. However, the attribution to mental illness treatment is largely inference from ethnographic analogy — we cannot definitively determine the motivation behind any specific prehistoric trepanation.

**Null finding: skeletal evidence for mental disorders is essentially absent.** Paleopathology can identify infectious diseases, tumors, trauma, and nutritional deficiencies from skeletal remains, but mental disorders leave virtually no skeletal signature. Burial anomalies (deviant positioning, exclusion from normal cemeteries) have been tentatively linked to disability or social exclusion, but such interpretations are highly speculative — atypical burial could reflect foreign origin, criminal status, ritual sacrifice, or epidemic death.

David Lewis-Williams's neuropsychological model of cave art (1988, 2002\) proposes that Upper Paleolithic art (\~40,000–10,000 BCE) reflects shamanic trance experiences, with geometric patterns corresponding to entoptic phenomena during altered states of consciousness and therianthropic figures (human-animal hybrids) representing shamanic transformation. The model has been critiqued for methodological problems in using 20th-century San ethnography to interpret art made by different peoples tens of thousands of years earlier. Crucially, **altered states of consciousness are not equivalent to mental illness** — as one Inuit stated to Jane Murphy: "when the shaman is healing he is out of his mind, but he is not crazy."

**Realistic conclusion:** We can say that trepanation was widely practiced and may have been used to treat behavioral disturbance in some cases, and that altered states of consciousness were likely important in Upper Paleolithic culture. We **cannot** make specific claims about the prevalence or nature of mental disorders in prehistoric populations from archaeological evidence alone. Mental states don't fossilize.

### **Contemporary hunter-gatherer studies yield conflicting signals**

Jane Murphy's 1976 *Science* study ("Psychiatric Labeling in Cross-Cultural Perspective") compared mental illness concepts in Yupik Eskimos (Bering Sea island; fieldwork 1954–55; n=499) and Yoruba of Nigeria. Both cultures had indigenous concepts for what Western psychiatry calls mental illness. The Yupik recognized *nuthkavihak* (talking to oneself, screaming at nonexistent persons, believing oneself to be an animal, refusing to eat) — strikingly parallel to schizophrenia. Both cultures distinguished pathological states from normal behavioral variation **and** from shamanic trance. Both had concepts corresponding to "psychopath": the Yupik term *kunlangeta* ("his mind knows what to do but he does not do it") described chronic lying, cheating, stealing, and sexual exploitation. When Murphy asked what was traditionally done with a *kunlangeta*, the response was: "Somebody would have pushed him off the ice when nobody else was looking."

This study challenged extreme cultural relativism by demonstrating cross-cultural convergence in recognizing core psychotic and antisocial symptoms. Its limitation is that both groups, while non-Western, were not completely isolated from Western influence.

Regarding depression in small-scale societies, the evidence is conflicting. Anthropologist Edward Schieffelin studied \~2,000 Kaluli of New Guinea highlands and found **only one person** approaching Western criteria for clinical depression. However, a study using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale among 23 Hadza women found **52%** scored above the threshold for probable depression — "very high in comparison with industrialised populations." These contradictory findings likely reflect both genuine variation and measurement problems (Western instruments may miss culturally specific expressions of distress while generating false positives through somatic items). The empirical base is too small for confident conclusions.

### **The "civilization disease" hypothesis has partial support for some disorders**

Several evolutionary psychiatry frameworks (notably Randolph Nesse's, articulated in *Good Reasons for Bad Feelings*, 2019, and *World Psychiatry*, 2023\) propose mismatch between evolved psychological mechanisms and modern environments. The evidence is strongest for **ADHD**: genomic analysis (Esteller-Cucala et al., *Scientific Reports*, 2020\) found ADHD-associated allele frequency has been decreasing for \~35,000 years, suggesting negative selection since the Paleolithic. Among nomadic Ariaal in Kenya, the DRD4 7R allele (linked to ADHD and novelty-seeking) was associated with better nourishment and social standing; among settled Ariaal, it was associated with worse outcomes — consistent with the mismatch hypothesis. A 2024 study found ADHD individuals departed foraging patches sooner and collected more berries, suggesting a possible foraging advantage.

The evidence for **anorexia nervosa as substantially culture-modulated** is strong: it is virtually absent in traditional societies, strongly associated with affluence and Western beauty ideals, and increases with Westernization/urbanization, though Hoek et al. (1998) found comparable incidence on Curaçao, complicating pure cultural causation.

The strongest version of the civilization disease hypothesis — that **schizophrenia is entirely modern** — is not well-supported given its apparent universality across all studied cultures at consistent prevalence. However, the possibility that its incidence or expression has been modulated by civilization-associated factors (infections, urbanization, diet, inflammation) remains plausible. Inflammation-based arguments (Rook, 2008\) proposing that reduced microbial exposure perturbs immunoregulatory circuits have correlational support (elevated inflammatory markers in schizophrenia and depression; prenatal inflammation associated with increased offspring schizophrenia risk), but the causal chain remains unestablished.

**Critical gap:** Very few systematic psychiatric epidemiological studies exist for hunter-gatherer populations. As a Cambridge University Press evolutionary psychiatry volume acknowledges: "Very few studies have examined the prevalence of these pathologies among contemporary hunter-gatherer populations; thus, empirical support for such diseases of modernity hypotheses is lacking." This absence of data should be treated as a finding — the evidence base for claims about mental disorder in prehistory is fundamentally thin.

## **6\. Key sources: the essential bibliography**

### **German E. Berrios, *The History of Mental Symptoms* (Cambridge UP, 1996\)**

**Type:** Secondary historical synthesis. **Key contribution:** Foundational reference for tracing descriptive psychopathological categories from antiquity through modern operational criteria. Organized by symptom type with coverage of perception, thought, delusions, obsessions, cognitive impairment, memory, consciousness, anxiety, affect, will, fatigue, catatonia, personality, and self-harm. Argues mental symptoms are "hybrid objects" — neither natural kinds nor pure social constructions. **Limitations:** Extremely dense; primarily covers 19th century onward; heavily European (especially Continental) sources; thin on non-Western material. **Does NOT cover:** Epidemiological data, cross-cultural phenomenology, or diagnostic adoption curves.

### **Reynolds and Kinnier Wilson, "Mental Illness in Ancient Mesopotamia" (*Brain*, 2014; *JRSM*, 2013\)**

**Type:** Primary historical analysis of cuneiform medical texts. **Key contribution:** Earliest known phenomenological descriptions of psychotic and depressive symptoms (\~1800–1600 BCE). Demonstrated that Babylonian descriptions were entirely behavioral/observational with no subjective content. **Limitations:** Interpretations of cuneiform texts have been critiqued by Assyriologists (Al-Rashid) as potentially over-reading modern categories into ancient texts. **Does NOT cover:** Non-Mesopotamian ancient sources.

### **Jablensky et al., "Schizophrenia: Manifestations, Incidence and Course in Different Cultures" (*Psychological Medicine*, 1992\)**

**Type:** Epidemiological (WHO DOSMeD study). **Key contribution:** Established that narrow-definition schizophrenia incidence (0.07–0.14/1,000) is remarkably consistent across 12 centers in 10 countries. Documented the outcomes paradox (better recovery in developing countries). **Limitations:** High attrition; Western-derived assessment instruments; long-term follow-up (ISoS) partially challenged the durability of the outcomes finding. **Does NOT cover:** Historical phenomenology or diagnostic adoption curves.

### **Murphy, "Psychiatric Labeling in Cross-Cultural Perspective" (*Science*, 1976\)**

**Type:** Primary anthropological. **Key contribution:** Demonstrated that Yupik and Yoruba cultures have indigenous concepts for psychosis and psychopathy that parallel Western categories, challenging extreme cultural relativism. **Limitations:** Only two cultures studied; neither fully isolated from Western contact. **Does NOT cover:** Prevalence estimates, historical trajectory, or non-psychotic disorders.

### **Hacking, *Mad Travelers* (Virginia UP, 1998\) and "The Looping Effects of Human Kinds" (1995)**

**Type:** Philosophy of psychiatry / historical epistemology. **Key contribution:** Framework of "transient mental illnesses" requiring specific ecological niches, and "looping effects" between classifications and classified individuals. **Limitations:** Interactive/indifferent kinds distinction may be overdrawn (Cooper, Khalidi); lacks adequate account of the self (Tekin). **Does NOT cover:** Empirical epidemiology or cross-cultural data.

### **Kleinman, *Rethinking Psychiatry* (Free Press, 1988\) and "Neurasthenia and Depression" (*Culture, Medicine and Psychiatry*, 1982\)**

**Type:** Cross-cultural psychiatric research. **Key contribution:** Demonstrated somatization of depression in Chinese populations; introduced "category fallacy" concept (applying categories from one culture to another). **Limitations:** Somatization/psychologization dichotomy has been challenged by subsequent work (Ryder et al., 2008). **Does NOT cover:** Historical phenomenology before the modern period.

### **Haslam et al., "Dimensions Over Categories" (*Clinical Psychology Review*, 2020\)**

**Type:** Meta-analysis (123 studies, 476 risk ratios). **Key contribution:** Found no overall diagnostic inflation across DSM editions (average risk ratio \= 1.00), while documenting specific diagnosis-level inflation and deflation. Distinguishes criteria-driven from practice-driven inflation. **Limitations:** Compares consecutive DSM editions only; does not capture practice patterns. **Does NOT cover:** Historical phenomenology or cross-cultural variation.

### **Shattuck, "The Contribution of Diagnostic Substitution to the Growing Administrative Prevalence of Autism" (*Pediatrics*, 2006\)**

**Type:** Epidemiological. **Key contribution:** Used IDEA data (1994–2003) to demonstrate association between autism prevalence increases and corresponding declines in mental retardation and learning disability classifications. **Limitations:** Administrative data (educational classifications, not clinical diagnoses); ecological design. **Does NOT cover:** Clinical validity of autism diagnosis or historical phenomenology.

### **Scull, *Madness in Civilization* (Princeton UP, 2015\)**

**Type:** Secondary historical synthesis. **Key contribution:** Most comprehensive single-volume history of madness, emphasizing sociological and critical perspectives. **Limitations:** Predominantly Western European and American focus (despite "civilization" title). **Does NOT cover:** Detailed symptom-level phenomenology; epidemiological data.

### **Polanczyk et al., "The Worldwide Prevalence of ADHD" (*American Journal of Psychiatry*, 2007\)**

**Type:** Meta-analysis (102 studies, 171,756 subjects). **Key contribution:** Established worldwide pooled ADHD prevalence of 5.29%, with variability explained by methodology rather than geographic location. **Limitations:** Assessment instrument heterogeneity; predominantly Western study populations. **Does NOT cover:** Historical trajectory, diagnostic adoption curves, or phenomenological analysis.

## **7\. Active scholars and research groups**

### **German E. Berrios and Ivana S. Marková (Cambridge / University of Hull)**

**Focus:** Epistemology of psychiatry and mental symptoms; historical epistemology of descriptive psychopathology. **Recent work:** "The Epistemology of Psychiatry and Mental Symptoms: The Cambridge View" in *Phenomenological Neuropsychiatry* (Springer, 2024); "Historical Epistemology: A Research Approach in Psychiatry" (*Integrative Psychological and Behavioral Science*, 2021). **Methodology:** Historical-epistemological analysis of psychiatric concepts; the "Cambridge model" of hybrid symptom-formation arguing that culture configures pre-linguistic biological signals into recognizable symptoms.

### **Giovanni Stanghellini and Matthew Broome (Chieti-Pescara / Birmingham)**

**Focus:** Renewed phenomenological psychopathology; abnormal bodily, spatial, and temporal experiences in schizophrenia and depression. **Recent work:** *The Oxford Handbook of Phenomenological Psychopathology* (2019); TATE instrument (2022); empirical studies on body/space disturbances in psychosis (*Schizophrenia Bulletin*, 2020). **Methodology:** Combines Jaspersian phenomenological description with empirical interview-based research; Wellcome Trust–funded "Renewing Phenomenological Psychopathology" project.

### **Josef Parnas (University of Copenhagen)**

**Focus:** Phenomenology of schizophrenia spectrum disorders; ipseity/self-disturbance model. **Recent work:** EASE scale development and validation; imagination anomalies in schizophrenia (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2024); co-edited *Philosophical Issues in Psychiatry II: Nosology* (2012, with Kendler). **Methodology:** Phenomenologically-informed semi-structured interviews combined with quantitative outcomes research; argues that self-disturbance is the core phenotype of schizophrenia spectrum.

### **Jonathan Y. Tsou (Iowa State University)**

**Focus:** Philosophy of psychiatric classification; natural kinds debate; history of the DSM. **Recent work:** *Philosophy of Psychiatry* (Cambridge UP, 2021); "Natural Kinds, Psychiatric Classification and the History of the DSM" (*History of Psychiatry*, 2016); "DSM-5 and Psychiatry's Second Revolution" (2015). **Methodology:** Naturalistic philosophy of science applied to classification; argues some mental disorders (schizophrenia, depression) are biological/natural kinds with stable biological bases, against Hacking's interactive kinds thesis.

### **Laurence Kirmayer (McGill University)**

**Focus:** Cultural psychiatry; cross-cultural validity of diagnostic categories; critique of global mental health movement. **Recent work:** "Toward a New Architecture for Global Mental Health" (2014, with Pedersen); "Cultural Concepts of Distress and Psychiatric Disorders" (2019, with Lewis-Fernández); DSM-5 Cultural Formulation Interview development. **Methodology:** Integrates cultural biology, discursive psychology, and attention to globalization; argues that "all forms of distress are locally shaped, including the DSM disorders."

## **Conclusion: what the evidence establishes and where it runs out**

The phenomenological record of mental disorder reveals a striking asymmetry. **Three clusters — psychosis, melancholia, and hysteria/conversion — have behavioral descriptions continuous across at least 35 centuries**, remarkably stable even as etiological theories changed from wandering uteruses to humoral imbalances to brain pathology. Individual psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized behavior), core depressive features (persistent sadness, withdrawal, insomnia, anorexia), and conversion symptoms (unexplained paralysis, seizures, altered consciousness) were described with recognizable fidelity in Babylonian cuneiform, Greek medical texts, Islamic medical treatises, and Indian Ayurvedic sources. This transhistorical, transcultural stability strongly suggests a neurobiological substrate that constrains the phenotypic expression of these conditions.

At the opposite extreme, **autism-like social deficits have no clear phenomenological precursor before the late 19th century**, and antisocial personality as a medical concept emerges only with Pinel in 1801\. These categories depend on conceptual frameworks — developmental norms, the intellect/affect distinction — that did not exist in pre-modern medicine. Whether the phenomena themselves are new or merely newly visible is the central unresolved question.

Between these poles lies the most intellectually productive territory: disorders whose phenomenology was continuously described but **differently carved**. Anxiety was embedded within melancholia until the 18th century. OCD existed as religious scrupulosity for centuries before being medicalized. Mania subsumed what we now divide into bipolar disorder and some psychotic states. The historical record shows that symptom boundaries are not stable — the same behavioral phenomena get grouped and regrouped across centuries, driven by changing theoretical frameworks, institutional needs, and available interventions.

The epidemiological data on modern diagnostic adoption reveals a complementary insight: **how a diagnosis is received depends less on its phenomenological validity than on whether pharmaceutical treatments exist, whether school systems create classification incentives, and whether patient advocacy movements generate demand**. The shape of adoption curves varies from exponential (autism, still rising) to S-curve (ADHD, plateaued) to inverted U (youth bipolar, partially corrected), with typical time lags of 10–31 years from introduction to peak growth. Diagnostic substitution is well-documented — the rise of autism corresponds partly to the decline of intellectual disability classifications; the death of "neurosis" birthed a dozen specific anxiety and mood disorders.

The deepest gap in this entire research domain is **the almost complete absence of systematic psychiatric epidemiology in hunter-gatherer and small-scale societies**. We have Murphy's study of two groups, scattered anthropological observations, and an enormous inferential void. Until this gap is addressed, claims about which disorders are "diseases of civilization" will remain more hypothesis than evidence. The archaeological record, for its part, offers trepanned skulls and suggestive burial practices but no direct window into prehistoric minds. Mental states don't fossilize, and the honest answer to the prehistory question is that we know remarkably little.
