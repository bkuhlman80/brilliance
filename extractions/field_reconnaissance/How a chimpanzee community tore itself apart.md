# **How a chimpanzee community tore itself apart**

The Ngogo chimpanzee community fission — documented in Sandel et al. (2026, *Science*) — is the most completely observed permanent division of a primate society in the scientific record, and only the second known chimpanzee community fission after Gombe's Kasakela-Kahama split in the 1970s. **The best available evidence points to a multi-causal cascade**: an unprecedentedly large community (\~200 individuals) exceeded the capacity for social relationship maintenance, pre-existing spatial substructure hardened into permanent division after the deaths of key bridging individuals, and the resulting polarization escalated through reproductive isolation, territorial patrolling, and sustained lethal aggression that has killed at least 24 former community members since 2018\. Individual-difference dimensions (boldness, stress reactivity, affiliation, dominance) are plausible but essentially untested causal contributors — no personality data exist for Ngogo, no hormonal data span the fission period, and no analytical framework has been applied to connect temperament to fission dynamics at any chimpanzee site. The empty cells here are genuine: the current evidence base cannot distinguish between individual differences playing a meaningful causal role and being entirely epiphenomenal.

## **Section 1: Causal narrative**

### **The community that grew too large**

Ngogo sits in the heart of Kibale National Park, Uganda — mature, species-rich rainforest with minimal human disturbance. Continuous research began in 1995 under John Mitani (Michigan) and David Watts (Yale). The community was already the largest known chimpanzee group and continued growing, reaching approximately **200 individuals including over 30 adult males** by the mid-2010s. Typical chimpanzee communities number 30–80 individuals. Ngogo's extraordinary size reflected favorable ecology: abundant fruit trees, low predation pressure, and high infant survivorship. Between 1999 and 2008, the community killed 21 members of neighboring groups. In 2009, it expanded its territory by **22% (6.4 km²)** into the decimated range of a northeastern neighbor. Wood et al. (2025, *PNAS*) showed that this expansion more than doubled female fertility and reduced infant mortality from 41% to 8% — the first direct evidence linking coalitional killing to reproductive fitness gains.

The success fed itself. More territory meant more food, more surviving infants, and continued population growth. Mitani described the community as "victims of their own success." Dunbar's social brain hypothesis predicts a cognitive group-size limit of roughly **53 individuals** for chimpanzees based on neocortex ratio. Ngogo was nearly four times that figure. Chimpanzees partially solve this problem through fission-fusion dynamics — they don't travel as a single group but instead form temporary foraging parties of variable size. But even fission-fusion dynamics have limits. With 200 individuals and 30+ adult males, the number of dyadic relationships requiring maintenance vastly exceeded any individual's capacity, and feeding competition intensified despite abundant habitat. Fruit production at Ngogo varies across space and time, and **heightened feeding competition** in a group this large likely imposed real costs even in rich forest.

### **Pre-existing fault lines masked by social bridges**

The Ngogo community was **never a homogeneous social unit**. Network analysis using the Leiden algorithm reveals detectable clusters throughout the 1998–2024 study period. Males and females formed spatial "neighborhoods," with a Western cluster and a Central/Eastern cluster consistently identifiable. But critically, **cluster membership was fluid**: approximately 29% of individuals switched clusters year to year, and between 2004 and 2014, **44% of infants were conceived by parents belonging to different clusters** (n \= 57 genetically assigned parentages). The community functioned as a single reproductive and political unit despite its spatial substructure.

What maintained this unity? The network data point to specific individuals who maintained cross-cluster social ties — males who groomed, associated, and formed coalitions with partners in both the Western and Central neighborhoods. These "bridging" individuals kept the network connected. Their role became visible only in retrospect, after their removal shattered the connections they had sustained.

### **2014–2015: The cascade begins**

**In 2014, five adult males and one adult female died.** Causes were not definitively established; two showed clinical signs of illness. These deaths represented more than 10% of the mature male population. The deceased males were later identified as key bridges connecting the Western and Central clusters. Jacob Negrey (Arizona) described them as "very central... to have lost them in a very short time was very significant because they had strong ties throughout the community." Joseph Feldblum (Duke), who identified a parallel pattern in the Gombe fission, compared them to "avuncular figures friendly with everyone who play this bridging social role."

Simultaneously, **the dominance hierarchy underwent a major transition**. A male named Jackson deposed the established alpha in 2015\. Jackson had previously been part of the Western cluster before rising through the hierarchy and associating primarily with Central males — making him, too, a cross-cluster bridge. His ascension to alpha status may have altered the coalition landscape, though published details on specific coalition shifts remain sparse.

The tipping point arrived on **June 24, 2015**. Aaron Sandel was observing when Western chimpanzees approached Central chimpanzees near the center of their shared range. "Rather than reuniting in typical fission-fusion fashion, the Western chimpanzees ran away, and the Central chimpanzees chased them." Nothing like this had been observed before. Six weeks of sustained avoidance followed — the longest recorded separation at the site.

Three independent statistical methods converge on **2015 as the structural breaking point**:

* **Modularity** (Leiden algorithm) increased most sharply from 2014 to 2015  
* **Change-point analysis** using separable temporal exponential-family random graph models (STERGMs) flagged 2015 as the strongest structural transition across 24 years of network data  
* A **degree-corrected stochastic block model** showed male proximity networks shifting from a single block to multiple blocks in 2015, stabilizing at two by 2018

Reproductive isolation preceded complete spatial separation. The last infant conceived by parents from different post-fission clusters dates to **March 2015**. Cross-cluster mating ceased entirely.

### **2016–2017: Territorial hardening and epidemic shock**

In **2016**, Western males initiated the first territorial patrol directed at Central chimpanzees. Two Central males initially accompanied them; after that, patrols were exclusively within-cluster. Spatial overlap decreased sharply — by 2016, 95% kernel density estimates showed two distinct, non-overlapping home ranges.

From December 31, 2016 to February 8, 2017, a **devastating respiratory epidemic** swept Ngogo. Caused by human rhinovirus C (a zoonotic pathogen), the outbreak infected 43.8% of observed chimpanzees and killed **25 individuals (12.2%)**, including four adult males and ten adult females (Negrey et al. 2019). Among the dead was one of the last males connecting the two emerging factions. Central males conducted their first patrol toward Western chimpanzees in 2017\. During one encounter, Western chimpanzees collectively attacked alpha male Jackson, severely injuring him, though he survived.

By the end of 2017, **two fully distinct groups had formed** with separate territories and no remaining affiliative connections.

### **2018–2026: Lethal violence and its escalation**

The first killing came in **2018**, when Western males killed a young adult Central male. Between 2018 and 2024, all observed lethal attacks were initiated by the Western group:

* **7 adult/mature males killed** (observed or inferred with high confidence)  
* **17 infants killed** (infanticides beginning in 2021, approximately 2 per year)  
* **14 additional Central males disappeared** between 2021 and 2024 with no signs of illness — suspected victims

Killing rates **"far exceeded those estimated for intergroup aggression in chimpanzees and in small-scale human societies"** (Sandel et al. 2026). The conflict remains ongoing — Sandel confirmed further attacks in 2025 and 2026 beyond the paper's data cutoff.

The demographic consequences are stark. At fission (\~2018), the Western group numbered approximately **83 individuals** and the Central group approximately **107**. By December 2025, these numbers had reversed: **Western \= 108, Central \= 76**. The smaller, initially disadvantaged group has systematically eliminated its former community members.

### **Causal weight assessment**

The factors driving the Ngogo fission can be ranked by evidentiary strength:

**Strong evidence**: (1) Group size exceeding the capacity for social relationship maintenance, creating persistent substructure vulnerable to disruption. (2) Deaths of bridging individuals in 2014 removing the social glue connecting clusters. (3) Network dynamics — the progressive increase in modularity, loss of cross-cluster ties, and emergence of two disconnected components, all documented with 24 years of quantitative data.

**Moderate evidence**: (4) The January 2017 respiratory epidemic accelerating polarization by killing additional bridge individuals. (5) The alpha male transition in 2015 disrupting coalitional alliances. (6) Reproductive competition — reproductive isolation preceded spatial separation, suggesting mate competition contributed to hostility. (7) Ecological pressure — feeding competition in a group four times typical size, even in rich habitat.

**Plausible but underdetermined**: (8) Cognitive limits on relationship maintenance (social brain hypothesis applied to chimpanzees). (9) Reduction of external threats following the 2009 territorial expansion, weakening the incentive for community-wide cohesion. (10) Demographic stochasticity — the specific individuals who died may have mattered more than the number who died.

**Speculative or absent from evidence**: (11) Individual-difference dimensions (personality, temperament, hormonal profiles) playing a causal role in determining which individuals joined which cluster, who initiated violence, or how the fission proceeded. The data to test this do not currently exist.

### **Why the smaller group won**

The most counterintuitive finding is that the **smaller Western group initiated all lethal attacks**. This refines rather than contradicts Wrangham's (1999) imbalance-of-power hypothesis, which predicts violence when one party can attack at low cost. The Western group achieved **local numerical superiority** through coordinated raiding parties that ambushed isolated Central individuals, typically 5–10 attackers against a single victim. Global community size was less important than the cohesion and coordination of the attacking party. Sandel et al. argue that "persistent offensive success by Western males suggests that cohesion supported by enduring relationships can outweigh numeric disadvantage." The Western cluster's tighter social bonds, some forged years before the split, gave them a cooperative advantage the larger but more diffuse Central group could not match.

### **The Gombe comparison clarifies what is general**

The Kasakela-Kahama fission at Gombe (1972–1978) shares striking structural parallels with Ngogo: pre-existing spatial subgroups, deaths of bridging males preceding polarization, alpha male instability during the fracture, and systematic lethal aggression against former community members. Feldblum et al. (2018) showed that Gombe's split sharpened in 1971–1972 during a male-biased sex ratio and dominance struggle, with post-split membership predicted by prior association patterns.

Key differences sharpen the interpretation. Gombe involved food provisioning (bananas), which critics argued artificially inflated group size and competition. Ngogo was **entirely unprovisioned**, eliminating this confound. Gombe's Kasakela group (\~20 adults) was far smaller than Ngogo (\~200 individuals). At Gombe, the **larger** Kasakela attacked the smaller Kahama; at Ngogo, the **smaller** Western group attacked the larger Central group. And at Gombe, the Kahama community was annihilated within four years; at Ngogo, the conflict has persisted for eight years and counting, with the attacking group growing while the defending group shrinks. The convergence of the two cases — despite different ecologies, scales, and provisioning regimes — strengthens the argument that community fission followed by lethal violence reflects a general chimpanzee capacity rather than an artifact of specific conditions.

The bonobo contrast is equally instructive. Brooks (2026, *Science* Perspective) notes that bonobos at Wamba, DRC, experienced a permanent community fission approximately 50 years ago (E1 and E2 groups), but the daughter communities coexist peacefully to this day. Whatever drives post-fission lethal aggression in chimpanzees, bonobos lack it — pointing toward species-level differences in male coalition structure, female social power, or both.

## **Section 2: Annotated source list**

**1\. Sandel, A. A., He, Y., Ren, J., et al. (2026). "Lethal conflict after group fission in wild chimpanzees." *Science*, 392, 216–220.** \[core\] The landmark paper analyzing 24 years of social network data (1998–2024), 10 years of GPS ranging, and 30 years of demographic records. Employs three independent network methods (Leiden algorithm, STERGM change-point analysis, degree-corrected stochastic block models) to identify 2015 as the structural tipping point. Documents 24+ killings by the Western group between 2018 and 2024\. Methodological strength: unprecedented temporal depth and multi-method convergence. Limitation: observational data cannot definitively establish causation among correlated factors (bridging deaths, alpha transition, epidemic).

**2\. Brooks, J. (2026). "Civil war among wild chimpanzees." *Science* (Perspective), same issue as Sandel et al.** \[core\] Companion commentary contextualizing the Ngogo fission within broader evolutionary theory. Highlights the bonobo contrast (Wamba fission without violence) and the "relational dynamics hypothesis" — that social networks alone can fracture communities without cultural markers like ethnicity or ideology. Useful for theoretical framing; not primary data.

**3\. Sandel, A. A. & Watts, D. P. (2021). "Lethal coalitionary aggression associated with a community fission in chimpanzees at Ngogo." *International Journal of Primatology*, 42, 26–48.** \[core\] The first published account of lethal post-fission violence at Ngogo, documenting two killings (2018–2019) in detail. Names victims (Erroll, Basie) and describes attack dynamics. Notes that Basie had previously groomed and formed coalitions with his attackers — demonstrating that long-term familiarity provided no protection. Important precursor to the 2026 *Science* paper; limited by smaller temporal scope.

**4\. Feldblum, J. T., Manfredi, S., Gilby, I. C., & Pusey, A. E. (2018). "The timing and causes of a unique chimpanzee community fission preceding Gombe's 'Four-Year War.'" *American Journal of Physical Anthropology*, 166, 730–744.** \[core\] Retroactive network analysis of the Gombe fission using digitized historical field notes. Confirmed that Kasekela-Kahama split from a single community, with subgrouping increasing sharply in 1971–1972 during male-biased sex ratios and dominance instability. Post-split membership predicted by prior association patterns. Methodological strength: demonstrates that historical data can be reanalyzed with modern network tools. Limitation: Gombe data are sparser than Ngogo's continuous records.

**5\. Wood, B. M., Watts, D. P., Langergraber, K. E., & Mitani, J. C. (2025). "Female fertility and infant survivorship increase following lethal intergroup aggression and territorial expansion in wild chimpanzees." *PNAS*, 122, e2524502122.** \[core\] First direct evidence that coalitional killing yields fitness benefits at Ngogo — fertility doubled and infant mortality dropped from 41% to 8% after the 2009 territorial expansion. Closes the theoretical loop between Wrangham's coalitional killing hypothesis and reproductive payoffs. Limitation: documents pre-fission intergroup aggression against external neighbors, not the post-fission intragroup violence.

**6\. Wilson, M. L., Boesch, C., Fruth, B., et al. (2014). "Lethal aggression in *Pan* is better explained by adaptive strategies than human impacts." *Nature*, 513, 414–417.** \[supporting\] Meta-analysis of 152 killings across 18 chimpanzee and 4 bonobo communities over five decades. Killing rates predicted by community size and population density, not human disturbance. Attackers outnumbered victims at a median 8:1 ratio. Foundational for establishing that chimpanzee lethal aggression is an adaptive strategy, not an artifact. Methodological strength: largest cross-site dataset; Limitation: aggregates heterogeneous populations.

**7\. Wrangham, R. W. (1999). "Evolution of coalitionary killing." *Yearbook of Physical Anthropology*, 42, 1–30.** \[supporting\] The foundational theoretical paper articulating the imbalance-of-power hypothesis: coalitional killing evolves when intergroup hostility exists and power asymmetries allow low-cost attacks. The Ngogo case refines this model — local encounter-level superiority matters more than global community size. Remains the primary theoretical framework for interpreting chimpanzee lethal aggression.

**8\. Negrey, J. D., Reddy, R. B., Scully, E. J., et al. (2019). "Simultaneous outbreaks of respiratory disease in wild chimpanzees caused by distinct viruses of human origin." *Emerging Microbes & Infections*, 8, 139–149.** \[core\] Documents the January 2017 respiratory epidemic at Ngogo (human rhinovirus C, 25 deaths) and simultaneous but distinct outbreak at Kanyawara (metapneumovirus, no deaths). Critical for the fission timeline: the epidemic killed remaining bridge individuals and accelerated complete separation. Methodological strength: pathogen identification via molecular diagnostics.

**9\. Langergraber, K. E., et al. (2014). "How old are chimpanzee communities? Time to the most recent common ancestor of the Y-chromosome in highly patrilocal societies." *Journal of Human Evolution*, 69, 1–7.** \[supporting\] Source of the \~500-year fission frequency estimate, based on Y-chromosome STR coalescent analysis across eight communities. TMRCA estimates ranged from 125 to 2,625 years with a median of \~500. Large confidence intervals limit precision, but the qualitative conclusion — fission is extremely rare — is robust.

**10\. Watts, D. P. (2018). "Male boundary patrols at Ngogo." *PNAS*.** \[supporting\] Twenty-year study of patrol participation at Ngogo. Males with more offspring, higher rank, and better physical condition patrolled more. Individual participation averaged 42% with 2.5-fold variation. Demonstrates that patrol behavior has both adaptive (reproductive stake) and dispositional (individual consistency) components. The data predate the fission but could retrospectively illuminate individual variation in aggression.

**11\. Weiss, A., Feldblum, J. T., Altschul, D. M., et al. (2023). "Personality traits, rank attainment, and siring success throughout the lives of male chimpanzees of Gombe National Park." *PeerJ*, 11, e16047.** \[individual differences\] The strongest demonstration of personality-fitness links in wild chimpanzees: higher Dominance and lower Conscientiousness predicted both rank attainment and siring success in 28 males over 37 years at Gombe. Uses the Hominoid Personality Questionnaire rated by long-term field assistants. Methodological strength: longitudinal design with genetic fitness data. Limitation: Gombe only; no data from Ngogo or other sites.

**12\. King, J. E. & Figueredo, A. J. (1997). "The Five-Factor Model plus Dominance in chimpanzee personality." *Journal of Research in Personality*, 31, 257–271.** \[individual differences\] Established the six-factor personality structure for chimpanzees (Extraversion, Conscientiousness, Agreeableness, Neuroticism, Openness, plus Dominance) using keeper ratings of 100 zoo-housed individuals. The Dominance factor — characterized by boldness, fearlessness, and competitive prowess — is the closest published analog to a "risk tolerance" dimension. Foundational but limited by captive population.

**13\. Sueur, C. & Maire, A. (2014). "Modelling animal group fission using social network dynamics." *PLOS ONE*, 9, e97813.** \[supporting\] Dynamic social network model simulating how repeated temporary fissions, with accompanying changes in social relationships, can lead to irreversible permanent fission. Key parameters: group size, relative influence of nutritional vs. social needs. Shows that the same species can take months or years before irreversible splitting depending on network feedback dynamics. Directly applicable to the Ngogo case but developed for cercopithecines rather than chimpanzees.

**14\. Wolf, M. & McNamara, J. M. (2012). "On the evolution of personalities via frequency-dependent selection." *The American Naturalist*, 179, 679–692.** \[individual differences\] Theoretical paper showing that frequency-dependent selection can explain behavioral consistency and cross-context trait correlations (i.e., personalities) when physiological architecture is incorporated into evolutionary models. Relevant to the question of why personality variation is maintained in populations — a necessary theoretical foundation for any individual-difference account of fission dynamics. Purely theoretical; no empirical chimpanzee data.

## **Section 3: Lab and research group mapping**

### **Ngogo Chimpanzee Project**

The primary research group documenting the fission, based at **Kibale National Park, Uganda**, with data collection continuous since 1995\. The project possesses 30 years of focal behavioral data, 24 years of annual social networks, 10 years of GPS ranging data, complete genetic parentage assignments, and non-invasive hormonal sampling infrastructure.

**Current co-directors (as of 2024\)**: Aaron Sandel (UT Austin), Kevin Langergraber (Arizona State), Rachna Reddy (Utah), Jacob Negrey (Arizona). **Founding co-directors**: John Mitani (Michigan, emeritus) and David Watts (Yale). Key recent publications: Sandel et al. (2026, *Science*), Wood et al. (2025, *PNAS*), Sandel & Watts (2021, *IJP*), Negrey et al. (2019, *Emerging Microbes*). The group has the only complete dataset on a well-documented chimpanzee community fission in an unprovisioned population. Notably, **no systematic personality assessments have been conducted at Ngogo**.

### **Gombe research consortium**

The **60-year Gombe dataset** is now managed through Arizona State University's Institute of Human Origins. **Ian Gilby** (ASU) directs the Gombe Chimpanzee Database; **Anne Pusey** (Duke, emerita) and **Joseph Feldblum** (Duke) maintain active research programs using the archive. **Michael Wilson** (Minnesota) contributes intergroup aggression analysis. **Alexander Weiss** (Edinburgh) has collected personality ratings for Gombe chimpanzees in collaboration with this group. Key publications: Feldblum et al. (2018, *AJPA*), Weiss et al. (2023, *PeerJ*), Feldblum et al. (2021, *iScience*). The consortium possesses personality data, long-term behavioral records, and genetic data — making it the only site where personality-fission dynamics could theoretically be examined retrospectively (for the 1970s Kasakela-Kahama split).

### **Chimpanzee personality researchers**

**Alexander Weiss** (Edinburgh) is the leading figure, having validated the chimpanzee personality structure in captive and wild populations and demonstrated personality-fitness links at Gombe. Collaborators include **Drew Altschul** (Edinburgh, statistical methods), **Miho Inoue-Murayama** (Kyoto, molecular genetics of personality), and **William Hopkins** (Emory/Yerkes, neurobiology). **James King** (Arizona, retired) developed the original Chimpanzee Personality Questionnaire. This group has access to Gombe long-term data through collaboration but not to Ngogo data. No published work connects personality to intergroup aggression, coalition dynamics, or community fission.

### **Primate social-network analysis**

**Lauren Brent** (Exeter) leads the largest primate SNA program, primarily using Cayo Santiago rhesus macaques, with ERC funding for the "Friend Origins" project linking social connections to brain structure and fitness. **Julia Ostner and Oliver Schülke** (Göttingen/German Primate Center) have developed causal Bayesian frameworks for inferring drivers of social network structure from time-series data — directly applicable to the Ngogo network dataset. **Cédric Sueur** (Strasbourg/CNRS) has built the most relevant computational models, simulating how temporary fissions lead to irreversible group splitting through network feedback dynamics. **Gabriel Ramos-Fernández** (CIDIR-IPN, Mexico) studies fission-fusion dynamics in spider monkeys using network approaches.

### **Coalitional violence and intergroup aggression theory**

**Richard Wrangham** (Harvard, emeritus) remains the dominant theorist, having formulated the imbalance-of-power hypothesis and co-authored the cross-site meta-analysis. **Michael Wilson** (Minnesota) maintains the largest comparative database on chimpanzee lethal aggression. **Martin Muller** and **Melissa Emery Thompson** (both New Mexico) have the strongest hormonal dataset from Kanyawara, neighboring Ngogo. **Roman Wittig and Catherine Crockford** (formerly MPI Leipzig, now ISC-CNRS Lyon) lead the Taï Chimpanzee Project, with extensive hormonal data on cortisol, oxytocin, and testosterone during intergroup encounters.

### **Key bridging figures across research programs**

**Kevin Langergraber** (ASU) bridges Ngogo genetics with the Gombe archive, both housed at ASU's Institute of Human Origins. **Joseph Feldblum** (Duke) bridges Gombe fission analysis with modern network methods. **Liran Samuni** (German Primate Center, Emmy Noether Programme) bridges Taï intergroup dynamics with network and cooperation research. These individuals represent potential nodes for integrating individual-difference approaches with the existing fission and aggression research programs.

## **Section 4: Evidence gaps**

### **What exists but hasn't been analyzed for individual differences**

The Ngogo dataset contains **30 years of individually identified behavioral data** on 219 chimpanzees, including focal follows recording party association, proximity, grooming, coalitions, dominance interactions, patrol participation, and hunting. Watts (2018) demonstrated **2.5-fold individual variation in patrol participation rates** at Ngogo, and Watts & Mitani (2001) showed that males preferentially patrolled with close social partners. These data could support retrospective analysis of individual behavioral consistency — whether the same males were consistently frequent or infrequent patrollers, groomers, or coalition partners across years. If behavioral consistency can be documented, it constitutes a personality-like construct even without formal questionnaire ratings.

**The Gombe personality-fission analysis is already feasible.** Weiss et al. (2023) collected personality ratings for Gombe males using long-term field assistants, and Feldblum et al. (2018) characterized the network structure of the 1970s Kasekela-Kahama fission. Combining these datasets could test whether personality dimensions predicted which cluster males joined during fission — a direct test of the individual-difference hypothesis using the only chimpanzee fission for which personality data exist. This analysis does not appear to have been published.

### **What hormonal and physiological data exist or could be collected**

Non-invasive urine collection has been conducted at Ngogo since at least the early 2000s (Sobolewski 2012, Muehlenbein et al. 2004/2006, Negrey et al. 2023). **Pre-fission testosterone and cortisol data exist** from the Sobolewski dissertation (\~2002–2010). However, **no hormonal data from the fission period (2014–2018) or the subsequent lethal violence (2018–2024) have been published**. If archived urine samples exist from these periods, retrospective hormonal analysis could test whether cortisol or testosterone profiles differed between individuals who joined the Western versus Central clusters, whether stress hormones spiked during the polarization, and whether testosterone predicted participation in lethal raids. Wittig et al.'s (2016) social buffering paradigm and Samuni et al.'s (2019) intergroup encounter cortisol data provide methodological templates.

Creatinine-corrected lean muscle mass data (Negrey et al. 2023\) could also be examined for pre-fission differences between clusters — were Western males in better physical condition, which might have enabled their subsequent aggressive dominance?

### **What analytical approaches would be informative**

Five specific analytical approaches could advance the individual-difference question:

* **Retrospective behavioral consistency analysis**: Using the 30-year Ngogo focal follow dataset, calculate repeatability (intraclass correlation) of patrol participation, aggression rates, grooming reciprocity, and association breadth across years for individual males. High repeatability would establish personality-like consistency. Then test whether these stable individual traits predicted post-fission cluster membership.

* **Network position–behavior coupling**: Test whether males who occupied high-betweenness-centrality positions (bridges) differed behaviorally from those who were embedded within a single cluster. Were bridges more affiliative, higher-ranking, older, or more socially versatile? The 24-year annual network data support this analysis.

* **Hormonal trajectory modeling**: If archived urine samples exist, measure cortisol and testosterone trajectories for identified individuals across the 2012–2020 window spanning the fission. Use mixed-effects models to test whether individual hormonal profiles predicted cluster membership and whether fission-related social disruption produced detectable stress responses.

* **Personality questionnaire collection (prospective)**: Deploy the Hominoid Personality Questionnaire (Weiss et al. 2009\) at Ngogo using long-term Ugandan field staff, as was done at Gombe (Weiss et al. 2017). With the two post-fission communities now functioning as separate entities for over eight years, current personality ratings could be linked to both retrospective behavioral data and ongoing intergroup dynamics.

* **Agent-based modeling**: Extend Sueur & Maire's (2014) network fission model to incorporate individual heterogeneity in boldness, affiliative tendency, and stress reactivity. Test whether models with personality variation better reproduce the observed fission dynamics (timing, cluster composition, direction of aggression) than models with homogeneous agents.

### **The fundamental constraint**

The core limitation is that **no personality assessment was conducted at Ngogo before the fission**. Without pre-fission personality data, any association between individual traits and cluster membership is confounded by post-fission experience — eight years of intergroup warfare have likely altered behavior, stress physiology, and social tendencies in ways that obscure pre-existing dispositions. The Gombe dataset, where personality ratings and historical fission data both exist, offers the only current path to a direct test. If personality predicted Kasekela-versus-Kahama membership at Gombe, this would provide strong motivation for prospective data collection at Ngogo and other large communities.

The honest assessment is that the current evidence base supports a predominantly **structural and ecological explanation** for the Ngogo fission: large group size created persistent substructure, stochastic deaths removed bridging individuals, and network dynamics did the rest. Individual differences may have influenced who became a bridge, who joined which cluster, and who led the violence — but these propositions are currently conjecture, not findings. The machinery for testing them exists in principle. The data, in most cases, do not.

## **Conclusion**

The Ngogo fission reveals community fracture as a multi-stage cascade rather than a single-cause event. No lone factor — group size, bridging deaths, alpha instability, disease — is sufficient; the interaction among them is what proved irreversible. The most novel empirical contribution is the demonstration that **social cohesion, not numerical superiority, determined which daughter group became the aggressor** — a meaningful refinement to the imbalance-of-power framework that has dominated theory since Wrangham (1999). The "relational dynamics hypothesis" advanced by Sandel et al. — that social networks can fracture and generate collective violence without cultural identity markers — carries implications for understanding the evolutionary origins of human intergroup conflict, though the bonobo contrast cautions against treating this as an inevitable outcome of group living.

For the individual-difference question that motivates this reconnaissance: the field is characterized by a striking disciplinary gap. Personality researchers and behavioral ecologists have worked in parallel for decades with almost no integration. The tools, frameworks, and long-term datasets needed to bridge this gap all exist — but they have not yet been connected. Whether individual-difference dimensions are causal contributors to fission dynamics or merely downstream consequences of structural forces is, as of 2026, genuinely unknown. That is the most important finding of this review.
