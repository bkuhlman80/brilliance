# **Mania in the 2020s: a construct between mood, psychosis, and reward**

Mania is not where clinicians once placed it. The convergence of dimensional psychopathology, genomic structural modeling, and computational neuroscience in the 2020s has repositioned mania as an **interstitial construct**—sitting between the internalizing and thought disorder spectra in HiTOP, loading onto a shared schizophrenia-bipolar genetic factor in Genomic SEM, and showing **71% of its genetic variance is specific** to the manic syndrome rather than shared with depression. This matters because it challenges the longstanding Kraepelinian separation of affective and psychotic illness while simultaneously undermining any simple equation of mania with "extreme happiness" or "the opposite of depression." The empirical picture that emerges is of a multidimensional phenotype—comprising euphoric activation, irritable dysphoria, psychomotor acceleration, grandiosity, and circadian disruption—whose component dimensions likely distribute across different structural positions in psychopathology. The field is moving toward decomposing mania at the symptom level, with network analyses identifying motor activity/energy as the most central feature, and latent class studies consistently recovering 4–5 subtypes that cut across the DSM's categorical boundaries.

## **Section 1: Descriptive and explanatory models**

### **The factor structure of mania has been replicated as 3–5 dimensions**

The categorical DSM-5-TR definition of a manic episode—elevated/expansive/irritable mood plus increased energy for ≥1 week with ≥3 of 7 additional symptoms—remains the clinical standard but is increasingly viewed as empirically inadequate. A meta-review by Peralta et al. (2020, *European Psychiatry*, reviewing 12 factor-analytic studies, N \= 3,039 manic patients) found that **hyperactivity and pressured speech** had equal or greater salience than mood elevation, contradicting DSM's primacy of the mood criterion. The most replicated factor structures identify hedonic activation (elevated mood, grandiosity, hyperactivity), irritable/dysphoric mania (irritability, hostility, aggression), psychomotor activation (pressured speech, flight of ideas), psychotic features (delusions, poor insight), and a depressive-anxious dimension that is present even during manic episodes.

Latent class analyses have reinforced this multidimensionality. Pignon et al. (2020, *Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease*, N \= 1,846) identified five manic subtypes: irritable (29%), irritable-psychotic (16%), expansive-hyperactive (13%), manic-psychotic (15%), and classical manic (27%). Arathimos et al. (2022, *British Journal of Psychiatry*) applied LCA in UK Biobank and the PROTECT study, finding subtypes with differential associations with genetic liability and comorbid diagnoses. These data-driven typologies consistently reveal more heterogeneity than DSM acknowledges, particularly regarding the irritable-dysphoric variant that represents roughly a third of manic presentations and is associated with poorer prognosis and higher comorbidity.

### **Reward hypersensitivity remains the most-tested psychological model**

The Behavioral Approach System (BAS) dysregulation model, developed primarily by Sheri Johnson, Lauren Alloy, and Charles Carver, posits that bipolar-vulnerable individuals have a hypersensitive approach-motivation system that, when triggered by reward-relevant life events, escalates into manic symptoms. A systematic review by Glas et al. (2024, *Bipolar Disorders*) examined 84 experimental studies of psychological mechanisms in bipolar disorder and found BAS/reward sensitivity was the most frequently investigated theory. Kwan et al. (2020, *Journal of Affective Disorders*, N \= 76 euthymic BD patients, 2-year follow-up) found BAS reward sensitivity was the only significant predictor of time to manic recurrence. Experience sampling studies have refined the model: a 2025 study in *Psychiatry Research* (N \= 497, dynamic SEM over one month) showed that **day-to-day BAS variability** predicted hypomanic mood and behavior more strongly than trait BAS levels, supporting the "dysregulation" rather than "sensitivity" formulation.

The neural evidence is more complex than the behavioral data. A 2022 meta-analysis in *Translational Psychiatry* (21 fMRI studies) found **hypo**activation in bilateral angular gyrus and right inferior frontal gyrus during reward anticipation in BD versus controls—partially contradicting a simple "hyperactive reward system" account. The model's limitations include difficulty explaining irritable/dysphoric mania, reliance on self-report measures, and limited specificity (reward sensitivity is also elevated in ADHD and substance use disorders). The Reward-Circadian Rhythm (RCR) integrated model (Alloy, Nusslock, & Boland) represents the most mature attempt to address these limitations by combining BAS dysregulation with circadian instability.

### **Circadian disruption shows converging multi-level evidence**

The social zeitgeber theory and circadian models have accumulated strong evidence across genetics, digital phenotyping, pharmacology, and animal models. Tonon et al. (2024, *Psychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences*) reviewed evidence that BD patients exhibit eveningness chronotype even during euthymia, with clock gene variants (*PER1*, *BMAL1*, *PER3*, *TIMELESS*) associated with BD risk. Song et al. (2024, *eBioMedicine*, N \= 139, \>40,000 person-days of wearable data) used mathematical modeling and transfer entropy analysis to demonstrate **causal directionality** from circadian disruption to mood symptoms—a critical advance over cross-sectional work. Walker et al. (2020, *Translational Psychiatry*) established that jet lag triggers bipolar episodes in susceptible individuals, with phase-advance (west→east travel) preferentially triggering mania. The ClockΔ19 mutant mouse, developed by Roybal et al. (2007, *PNAS*), displays a behavioral profile strikingly similar to human mania—hyperactivity, decreased sleep, increased reward sensitivity—that is reversed by lithium. This model directly connects circadian gene disruption to dopaminergic hyperactivity in the ventral tegmental area.

### **Dopamine evidence strengthens with the Yatham PET study**

The dopaminergic model received its strongest direct support from Yatham et al. (2022, *JAMA Psychiatry*, N \= 26 BD patients \+ 21 controls), a PET study using \[¹¹C\]methylphenidate that found **42–45% lower striatal dopamine transporter (DAT) binding** in currently manic patients versus controls, with DAT levels normalizing upon remission. The correlation between YMRS scores and DAT binding was remarkably strong (r \= −0.93 in right striatum). Ashok et al. (2017, *Molecular Psychiatry*) provided a comprehensive review establishing converging pharmacological and imaging evidence for hyperdopaminergia in mania. The PGC4 GWAS (O'Connell et al., 2025, *Nature*) identified **DRD2 among 36 credible causal genes**, providing genomic validation of dopamine's role. The model's primary limitation remains small imaging samples and medication confounds—almost all PET studies have N \< 30\.

### **Predictive processing accounts remain largely theoretical for mania**

Computational psychiatry has generated elegant theoretical accounts of mania as aberrant precision weighting—the brain assigning excessive confidence to active, exploratory policies and reward-predicting priors. Shaw, Sumner, and Berndt (2025/2026, *Frontiers in Psychiatry*) explicitly modeled bipolar disorder within a predictive coding framework, proposing that a time-varying precision parameter κ increases excessively in mania (producing overconfident updates) and crashes in depression. Haarsma et al. (2021, *Molecular Psychiatry*, N \= 59 healthy \+ 74 including 20 first-episode psychosis) provided mechanistic evidence that dopamine mediates precision weighting of prediction errors, which is impaired in psychosis. However, a systematic review by Qela et al. (2025, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*) confirmed that **bipolar disorder is severely underrepresented** in empirical predictive coding research relative to schizophrenia. No mania-specific computational modeling studies with clinical samples were identified. This remains the model with the highest theoretical promise but lowest empirical traction for mania specifically.

### **Emerging models target neuroinflammation, mitochondria, and the gut**

Neuroinflammatory and metabolic models are gaining momentum. Gardea-Resendez et al. (2022, *Molecular Psychiatry*) provided quasi-causal evidence that antidepressants increasing mitochondrial energetics elevate treatment-emergent mania risk (OR \= 2.21, p \= 0.000009), supporting a mitochondrial-energy model. An iPSC-derived cerebral organoid study (2025, *Translational Psychiatry*) showed BD organoids had mitochondrial impairment and increased NLRP3 inflammasome activation, with an NLRP3 inhibitor rescuing mitochondrial function—linking neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction mechanistically. Gut-brain axis research is the most nascent: Guo et al. (2024, *Translational Psychiatry*, N \= 65 unmedicated BD-II \+ 58 controls) found decreased gut microbial alpha diversity, elevated cytokines, and altered cerebellar connectivity in BD-II. These emerging models share a common limitation: cross-sectional designs predominate, effect sizes are modest, and findings lack disorder specificity.

Network neuroscience models of prefrontal-limbic-striatal dysconnectivity have moderate-to-strong support. Hu et al. (2023–2024, *Psychological Medicine*) found strengthened default mode network connectivity in hypomanic patients that tracked mood state longitudinally. However, very few neuroimaging studies capture patients in acute mania, most scan euthymic patients, and network findings overlap substantially with schizophrenia, limiting diagnostic specificity.

## **Section 2: Where mania sits in dimensional psychopathology**

### **HiTOP designates mania as interstitial between internalizing and thought disorder**

The Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) places mania in a unique structural position. Watson et al. (2022, *World Psychiatry*) stated explicitly that "mania is currently an interstitial construct in HiTOP, with important connections to both internalizing and thought disorder." Several structural studies place bipolar I within the thought disorder spectrum, while others link mania to the internalizing distress subfactor. Critically, Watson et al. noted that mania "subsumes several distinct symptom dimensions, including **emotional lability, euphoric activation, hyperactive cognition, reckless overconfidence, and irritability**" and predicted that "more fine-grained analyses will likely reveal that they are located in different HiTOP spectra."

Kotov et al. (2020, *World Psychiatry*) reported that bipolar I disorder was linked to thought disorder in three structural studies and to internalizing in one, but cautioned that several studies linking mania to internalizing did not include a thought disorder factor and therefore could not test the alternative placement. The genetic evidence strongly favors spectrum-differentiated placement: **rg between BD-I and schizophrenia is 0.66–0.70**, whereas between BD-I and MDD it is only 0.34–0.39. Conversely, **BD-II shows the opposite pattern** (rg with MDD \= 0.66–0.68 versus rg with schizophrenia \= 0.54–0.61). This genetic architecture suggests that BP-I is structurally aligned with the thought disorder spectrum while BP-II aligns with internalizing—the bipolar "spectrum" may literally span two HiTOP spectra.

The HiTOP Thought Disorder Measure development (Cicero, Jonas et al., \~2023, consortium publication) found that mania scales—grandiosity, euphoric mood/excessive energy, hyperactive cognition, emotional lability, recklessness, increased goal-directed activity, decreased need for sleep—all loaded with the positive psychosis symptom factor in EFA, forming a two-factor structure of positive symptoms (including mania) versus negative symptoms. However, internalizing items were not included in this data collection, so the cross-loading hypothesis could not be directly tested. The research-ready HiTOP-SR (released December 2023\) includes mania scales, but empirical data on their structural distribution have not yet been published.

### **The p-factor debate reveals mania's paradoxical generality**

Mania occupies a paradoxical position in bifactor models of psychopathology. In the foundational study by Caspi et al. (2014, *Clinical Psychological Science*, Dunedin cohort, N ≈ 1,000), mania loaded **0.97 on the general psychopathology (p) factor**—the highest loading of all indicators. This occurred because the Thought Disorder specific factor collapsed (showing negative variance), with its variance absorbed into the general factor.

Heinrich et al. (2023, *Assessment*) demonstrated that this result is a methodological artifact rather than a substantive finding. In Caspi et al.'s data, factor scores for the p-factor and the Thought Disorder factor from the correlated-factors model correlated at **0.997**—they were the same thing. What Caspi et al. called "general psychopathology" was essentially the Thought Disorder factor relabeled. The bifactor-(S−1) framework resolves this by designating one domain as a reference factor, yielding interpretable residual specific factors. Forbes (2024, *Biological Psychiatry*) reinforced this critique, showing that the p-factor's meaning changes depending on which indicators are included: when internalizing items dominate, p reflects distress; when thought disorder items dominate, p reflects psychosis. Mania's extreme loading simply reflects the latter scenario. Caspi and Moffitt (2024, *Clinical Psychological Science*) responded by arguing that p performs similarly regardless of how it is modeled and that its meaning should come from criterion-validation studies rather than model comparison. The debate remains active but the bifactor-(S-1) perspective has substantially weakened claims that mania uniquely indexes "general" psychopathology.

### **Network models identify energy and sleep as structural hubs**

Network analyses of psychopathology position mania and depression as spatially segregated communities connected by bridge symptoms. Zavlis, Matheou, and Bentall (2023, *Bipolar Disorders*, N \= 36,557 from the Canadian Community Health Survey) identified four bridge symptoms: **sleep disturbances, anhedonia, suicidal ideation, and impulsivity**. Within the manic cluster, hyperactivity/energy-related symptoms were the most central nodes. Weintraub, Schneck, and Miklowitz (2020, *Bipolar Disorders*, N \= 272 treatment-seeking adolescents) found that **irritability and mood lability** served as bridge symptoms, while energy-related symptoms (fatigue, hyperactivity) had the highest centrality. These findings converge with DSM-5's addition of "increased energy or activity" as a gate criterion—energy dysregulation appears to be the structural core of mania rather than mood elevation per se.

The comorbidity patterns predicted by mania's structural position are well-supported. Shared membership in the thought disorder spectrum explains the high overlap with psychotic disorders. Cross-loading onto internalizing explains the co-occurrence with anxiety and depression. Bridge features of impulsivity and reward sensitivity connect to substance use (disinhibited externalizing). The fact that bipolar disorder has among the highest comorbidity rates of any psychiatric condition is a predictable consequence of its interstitial position.

## **Section 3: What mania is and is not**

### **Mania versus ADHD: episodicity is the primary discriminator**

ADHD and mania share impulsivity, distractibility, psychomotor activation, racing thoughts, and emotional lability. Measurement instruments themselves struggle to separate the constructs—Barden et al. (2023, *Practice Innovations*) found the BASC-3 Mania Scale accounted for over 54% of variance in ADHD total symptoms, with correlations of r \= .70–.75 between hyperactivity and mania scales. Kitsune et al. (2016, *Journal of Affective Disorders*, N \= 60 adult women) found that ADHD interview measures achieved \~90% sensitivity and \~95% specificity for distinguishing ADHD from BD, but only when using temporal framing—current-symptom self-report measures could not differentiate the groups.

The key dissociators are **episodic course** (mania involves discrete change from baseline; ADHD is chronic and lifelong), **grandiosity** (core to mania, absent in ADHD), and **decreased need for sleep with sustained energy** (cardinal for mania; ADHD sleep disruption produces fatigue, not energy). Prada et al. (2023, *Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment*) identified a cognitive double dissociation: hypomania produces **phonemic-based** verbal fluency patterns (puns, alliterations), whereas ADHD produces **semantic-based** word search. They also found, counterintuitively, that racing thoughts are more enhanced in ADHD than during hypomania—challenging the assumption that racing thoughts are pathognomonic of mania. The comorbidity rate is approximately 18% (Schiweck et al., 2021 meta-analysis), underscoring that these are overlapping but separable constructs.

### **Mania versus narcissistic grandiosity: an empirically thin distinction**

Manic and narcissistic grandiosity share inflated self-concept, goal-directed activity, reward-seeking, and hubristic pride. Nagel et al. (2023, *Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy*) provided the most comprehensive recent review and found considerable overlap in reward-seeking, harm avoidance, and social functioning domains. The paper challenged the clean episodic-versus-trait distinction by noting that narcissism shows vulnerable oscillations and BD has stable hypomanic traits between episodes. The traditional discriminators—that manic grandiosity is mood-congruent and episodic while narcissistic grandiosity is defensive and pervasive—may be oversimplified.

Kaufman et al. (2018), using the trifurcated model of narcissism (FFNI-SF), located the structural overlap precisely: both manic traits and grandiose narcissism load on **agentic extraversion plus antagonism** (low agreeableness). The dissociation requires invoking dimensions with no narcissism equivalent: decreased need for sleep, psychomotor acceleration, and the cycling into depression. **This is honestly one of the thinnest empirical areas in the review.** There are virtually no studies administering the same grandiosity measures to both BD and NPD samples with adequate power. Direct comparative studies are urgently needed.

### **Positive urgency is a component of mania, not a competing construct**

The relationship between mania and positive urgency (impulsive action during positive mood states) is among the best-documented in the impulsivity literature. Johnson et al. (\~2015, *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*, N \= 91 euthymic BD-I vs. 80 controls) found positive urgency yielded the largest group difference of any impulsivity facet: **partial η² \= 0.316**—a very large effect. Giovanelli et al. (2013, *Cognition and Emotion*, N \= 823 \+ 482\) found that Hypomanic Personality Scale scores were uniquely associated with positive urgency (r ≈ .36) and that positive urgency was linked to amplification of positive moods, suggesting a feedback loop. A 2024 scoping review in *BMC Psychology* (10 studies) found emotional urgency predicted bipolar symptoms, severity, and suicide attempts better than non-emotional impulsivity (r \= .34 vs. r \= .08–.14).

The critical structural insight is that positive urgency appears to be a **constituent facet** of mania rather than a discriminable adjacent construct. The dissociation lies in mania's additional features—grandiosity, decreased sleep, elevated mood generation (not just acting on existing positive mood), and cognitive acceleration—that go beyond the impulsivity-when-happy component. Whether mania reduces to positive urgency plus elevated mood plus grandiosity plus energy changes, or whether a deeper common factor generates all of these, remains an open question.

### **Mania versus extraversion: instability and neuroticism are the keys**

Hypomania-prone individuals score high on Extraversion, Openness, and low on Conscientiousness across self-report, laboratory tasks, and informant reports (Durbin et al., 2009, *Journal of Research in Personality*, N \= 101, multi-method). The meta-analytic association between the Hypomanic Personality Scale and BAS sensitivity is large (g \= 0.74). However, the dissociation is clear and well-established. Meyer (2002, *Personality and Individual Differences*, N \= 224\) demonstrated that HPS predicted manic symptoms **beyond** what Extraversion alone predicted—Extraversion was not a significant predictor once HPS was in the model.

The most important finding comes from Barnett et al. (2023, *Psychological Medicine*): euthymic BD patients actually show **low Extraversion**, along with high Neuroticism, high Openness, low Conscientiousness, and low Agreeableness. The elevated Extraversion associated with mania is state-dependent—it rises during manic episodes and falls during depression. The structural key is that **manic vulnerability combines high approach motivation with high punishment sensitivity** (elevated BIS alongside BAS), whereas normal extraversion involves only high approach with emotional stability. Meta-analytic data confirm: the HPS–BAS association is g \= 0.74, but euthymic BD patients versus controls show g \= 0.20 for BAS and g \= 0.64 for BIS sensitivity. The co-occurrence of high Neuroticism with high reward sensitivity is what differentiates bipolar liability from trait extraversion. Openness to Experience may additionally distinguish BD from unipolar depression (Barnett et al., 2023).

### **Network centrality confirms energy/activity as the structural core**

Across network analysis studies, **motor activity/energy** is consistently the most central manic symptom. Briganti et al. (2021, *Journal of Affective Disorders*, N \= 100 inpatients with acute mania) found elevated mood was the most interconnected symptom at admission, but centrality shifted over treatment, with language-thought disorder becoming more central later. A 2024 study in *Translational Psychiatry* of BD patients in psychiatric emergency departments confirmed "increased motor activity-energy" as the most central manic symptom (highest expected influence), followed by emotional instability. The convergence across studies supports conceptualizing mania as fundamentally an **activation/energy disturbance** with affective and cognitive coloring, rather than a purely emotional phenomenon.

## **Section 4: Genetic architecture places mania at the psychosis-mood boundary**

### **The PGC4 GWAS identified 298 loci across ancestries**

The Psychiatric Genomics Consortium has produced two landmark bipolar disorder GWAS in this period. Mullins et al. (2021, *Nature Genetics*, PGC3: 41,917 cases, 371,549 controls) identified **64 genome-wide significant loci** with enrichment in synaptic and **calcium signaling pathways**, particularly in prefrontal cortex and hippocampal neurons. The top hit was *TRANK1* (P \= 6.6 × 10⁻¹⁹), with *CACNA1C*, *ANK3*, and *FADS2* among the most consistently replicated risk genes. BD-I and BD-II showed high but imperfect genetic correlation (**rg \= 0.85**), with BD-I SNP-heritability estimated at 0.21 and BD-II at 0.11.

O'Connell, Koromina et al. (2025, *Nature*, PGC4: **158,036 cases**, 2,796,499 controls across multiple ancestries) expanded the count to **298 genome-wide significant loci** and identified **36 credible causal genes** through fine-mapping, including *DRD2* (dopamine receptor D2), *FURIN*, *THRA*, and *MED24*. Enrichment was observed in GABAergic interneurons and medium spiny neurons. The convergence of common and rare variant signals—genes prioritized through fine-mapping were enriched for ultra-rare damaging variants in BD cases—provides biological cross-validation of GWAS hits.

No large-scale GWAS has been conducted specifically of manic symptoms as a continuous trait rather than categorical BD diagnosis. This is a significant gap. Twin evidence (McGuffin et al., 2003, *Archives of General Psychiatry*, 67 twin pairs) suggests that **\~71% of the genetic variance for mania is not shared with depression** (genetic correlation between mania and depression within bipolar \= 0.65), implying that a mania-specific GWAS would identify partially distinct biology.

### **Genetic correlations map the bipolar spectrum onto a psychosis-mood continuum**

The genetic correlation structure of bipolar disorder tells a remarkably coherent story. BD shows its strongest overlap with schizophrenia (**rg \= 0.68**), followed by MDD (**rg \= 0.44**), with moderate correlations with ADHD, anorexia, and autism (each rg ≈ 0.20). The subtype-specific analysis is particularly informative:

* BD-I (defined by mania) → schizophrenia: rg \= **0.66–0.70**  
* BD-II (defined by hypomania \+ depression) → MDD: rg \= **0.66–0.68**  
* BD-I → MDD: rg \= **0.34–0.39**  
* BD-II → schizophrenia: rg \= **0.54–0.61**

This pattern means that the more manic the bipolar subtype, the more it genetically resembles schizophrenia; the more depressive, the more it resembles MDD. MiXeR analyses (Hindley et al., 2022, *American Journal of Psychiatry*) estimated \~8,600 causal variants for BD, with 96% of BD variants also influencing schizophrenia and 97% influencing MDD—but with variable concordance of effect direction (80% for SCZ, 69% for MDD). This suggests that BD shares genetic architecture broadly across major psychiatric conditions but that the direction of effects is what differentiates it, particularly from MDD.

Non-psychiatric genetic correlations extend the picture. BD PRS predict membership in artistic societies and creative professions (Power et al., 2015, *Nature Neuroscience*, replicated across Icelandic and European cohorts). Rajagopal et al. (2024, *Psychiatry Research*, N \= 241,736 UK Biobank) found extensive genetic overlap between occupational creativity and psychiatric disorders with mixed effect directions. BD shows positive genetic correlation with **risk-taking behavior** (rg \= 0.21; O'Connell et al., 2021, *Translational Psychiatry*), higher educational attainment, and evening chronotype. BD PRS also associate positively with insomnia, daytime sleepiness, smoking initiation, and problematic alcohol use.

### **Genomic SEM consistently places bipolar on a psychotic factor**

Genomic structural equation modeling has become the primary method for mapping the genetic architecture of psychopathology. Grotzinger et al. (2022, *Nature Genetics*) modeled 11 psychiatric disorders and identified four correlated genomic factors: Neurodevelopmental (ADHD, ASD, Tourette's), Compulsive (anorexia, OCD, Tourette's), **Psychotic (bipolar, schizophrenia)**, and Internalizing (MDD, PTSD, anxiety, alcohol use). Bipolar loaded primarily on the **Psychotic factor** alongside schizophrenia—not with MDD on the Internalizing factor. The updated five-factor model (Grotzinger et al., 2025, *Nature*, CDG3: 1,056,201 cases across 14 disorders) confirmed a **Schizophrenia-Bipolar factor**, with the addition of separate Substance Use and Neurodevelopmental factors. A higher-order genetic p-factor loaded most strongly on the Internalizing factor, which means bipolar's primary genetic affiliation is with the psychosis dimension rather than the general liability dimension.

This genomic placement converges strikingly with HiTOP's phenotypic structure. Both place mania closer to thought disorder/psychosis than to internalizing/depression. However, Waldman et al. (2020, *World Psychiatry*) found that BD's factor placement was somewhat unstable across models—in their 14-disorder CFA, BD loaded on a "thought problems" factor alongside schizophrenia, OCD, and eating disorders, differing from Grotzinger's compulsive factor structure. This instability across models reflects BD's genuinely heterogeneous genetic architecture.

### **Twin heritability is among the highest in psychiatry**

Twin studies consistently estimate BD heritability at **60–93%**, the highest of any major psychiatric disorder. The most cited estimates come from McGuffin et al. (2003: h² \= 0.93), Kieseppä et al. (2004, *American Journal of Psychiatry*: h² \= 0.93, MZ concordance 0.43, DZ concordance 0.06), and Song et al. (2021, *Psychiatry Research*, Swedish Twin Register, N \= 92,408: h² \= 0.60 with survival analysis correction). SNP-heritability from PGC3 captures only \~19% of the phenotypic variance, indicating a substantial "missing heritability" gap. Kieseppä et al. noted that "the phenotype might more precisely be a manic behavior" and that "a manic state \[is\] the key heritable trait"—an observation supported by the McGuffin finding that 71% of genetic variance for mania is specific to the manic syndrome.

### **Animal models converge on circadian-dopamine interactions**

The ClockΔ19 mutant mouse (Roybal et al., 2007, *PNAS*) remains the most cited single-gene animal model of mania: hyperactivity, decreased sleep, increased cocaine and sucrose reward sensitivity, decreased anxiety-like and depression-like behavior—all reversed by chronic lithium. The behavioral abnormalities were rescued by expressing functional CLOCK protein specifically in the VTA, directly linking circadian gene disruption to dopaminergic hyperactivity. Dopamine transporter (DAT) knockdown mice display mania-like exploratory profiles matching the quantified behavioral pattern of manic BD patients in the cross-species Behavioral Pattern Monitor. The Madison (MSN) mouse (Saul et al., 2012, *PLOS ONE*) represents a rarer polygenic model, with gene expression dysregulation in transcripts whose human orthologs are associated with BD. However, **no systematic GWAS-based cross-species polygenic overlap analyses** between human BD GWAS hits and mouse locomotor activity, reward sensitivity, or circadian QTLs have been published. This remains a significant gap.

## **Synthesis: five open questions where the field is concentrating**

The 2020s literature on mania reveals a phenotype that defies simple classification but is yielding increasingly to decomposition. Several open questions concentrate field momentum.

**First, can mania be decomposed at the symptom level across structural dimensions?** HiTOP explicitly predicts that euphoric activation, irritability, emotional lability, grandiosity, and hyperactive cognition will ultimately map to different spectra. No published study has tested this with the HiTOP-SR. This is arguably the most important near-term empirical question in the structural psychopathology of mania.

**Second, will a mania-specific GWAS reveal distinct biology from the categorical BD GWAS?** Given that 71% of genetic variance for mania is specific to the manic syndrome (McGuffin et al., 2003), and that BD-I and BD-II show differential genetic overlap with schizophrenia versus MDD, a dimensional GWAS of manic symptom severity could identify loci invisible to categorical BD GWAS. The PGC has not yet prioritized this, but the infrastructure exists.

**Third, can predictive processing models generate mania-specific empirical predictions?** The theoretical framework is elegant—mania as excessive precision weighting, depression as blunted precision—but no computational task has been designed and validated for manic samples. The field needs paradigms analogous to the beads task in psychosis research, specifically designed to capture the precision dynamics hypothesized to generate manic cognition.

**Fourth, what is the nature of mania's relationship to narcissistic grandiosity?** This is the thinnest empirical area identified in this review. The traditional episodic-versus-trait distinction may be insufficient given that BD has stable hypomanic personality traits and narcissism shows vulnerable oscillations. Direct studies using the same dimensional grandiosity measures in BD and NPD samples are needed.

**Fifth, how do emerging biological models (neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, gut-brain axis) integrate with established psychological models?** The Gardea-Resendez et al. (2022) pharmacological evidence linking mitochondrial energetics to treatment-emergent mania, and the organoid studies linking NLRP3 inflammasome activation to mitochondrial dysfunction, suggest a convergent biological substrate. The critical challenge is connecting these molecular findings to the psychological-level models (BAS dysregulation, circadian disruption) through mechanistic intermediaries—dopamine being the most obvious candidate, given that neuroinflammation affects DAT function and oxidative stress disrupts dopaminergic signaling.

The overarching trajectory is clear: mania is moving from a unitary categorical diagnosis toward a decomposed, multi-level construct whose component dimensions distribute across different structural positions in psychopathology, are served by partially distinct biological substrates, and share genetic architecture primarily with the psychosis spectrum rather than with depression. The clinical implications are substantial—interventions targeting energy/activity dysregulation and circadian disruption may have more structural justification than those targeting mood per se.
