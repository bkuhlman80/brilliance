# **Negative Valence and shame: two literatures studying the same construct in parallel**

**The Negative Valence factor from Tellegen and Waller's Big Seven model and dispositional shame-proneness appear to describe the same psychological phenomenon — global, stable, morally-valenced negative self-evaluation — yet not a single published study has ever tested their relationship directly.** This is the central finding of this reconnaissance. NV has been replicated in at least eight languages, consistently predicts personality disorder variance beyond the Big Five, and fractionates into subfactors (depravity, worthlessness, stupidity) that mirror shame's appraisal structure almost exactly. Meanwhile, the shame literature has independently documented that shame-proneness shows the same non-specific prediction of personality pathology, the same incremental validity beyond Neuroticism, and the same theoretical core of "I am fundamentally bad." The two research communities — lexical personality structure and self-conscious emotion — share no citations, no collaborating researchers, and no overlapping measures. What follows is what the evidence actually says, including where it is absent.

---

## **1\. The Big Seven model: replicated but orphaned**

### **Origins and replication count**

The Big Seven emerged from Tellegen and Waller's 1987 APA presentation, in which **400 adjectives** sampled inclusively from the American Heritage Dictionary (without the evaluative-term exclusions standard in Big Five work) were self-rated by **N \= 543** undergraduates. NV was the largest factor, accounting for **28.2% of common variance**. The full study was not formally published until Tellegen self-published it on his personal website in 2018, three decades later — an extraordinary lag that partly explains NV's marginal status in the field.

Independent replications exist in **at least eight language communities**: English (Tellegen & Waller, 1987/2018, N \= 543; replication N \= 696; Saucier, 1997, JPSP), Hebrew (Almagor, Tellegen, & Waller, 1995, JPSP, 252 descriptors), Spanish (Benet & Waller, 1995, JPSP, N \= 435 native speakers; Benet-Martínez & Waller, 1997, Journal of Personality), Filipino/Tagalog (Church, Katigbak, & Reyes, 1997/1998, N \= 629 and N \= 740), Turkish (Goldberg & Somer, 2000, N \= 631), Greek (Saucier et al., 2005, JPSP, N \= 991, partial — NV merged with Honesty content), Serbian (Smederevac, Mitrović, & Čolović, 2007), and Chinese (Zhou, Saucier, Gao, & Liu, unpublished). Saucier's (2003) Multi-Language Seven (ML7) demonstrated convergence across Filipino and Hebrew structures, relabeling NV as "Social Unacceptability."

**The critical methodological contingency**: NV emerges only when researchers use inclusive variable selection. Studies that pre-exclude evaluative, low-base-rate, or "purely descriptive" terms — the standard in Big Five research — eliminate NV by design. This is not a replication failure; it is a sampling decision. Ashton, Lee, Perugini et al. (2004, JPSP) analyzed six-factor solutions across seven languages and did not find separate NV factors, but they used their preferred restrictive selection criteria. The debate over NV's status is thus substantially a debate over which adjectives should be allowed into the analysis.

### **Lexical content: what actually loads onto NV**

The ten strongest NV markers from Tellegen's original study (loadings .60–.68) include: *lousy, wicked, evil, stupid, detestable, depraved, jackbooted, oafish, squalid,* and *lunatic*. This content spans moral depravity (*evil, wicked, depraved, immoral*), stupidity/incompetence (*pinheaded, oafish*), insanity (*lunatic*), physical-social repulsiveness (*squalid, lousy*), and oppressive cruelty (*jackbooted*). The common thread is **extreme negative social evaluation** — attributes that would make someone the target of contempt, disgust, or moral condemnation.

Benet-Martínez and Waller (2002, European Journal of Personality) provided the definitive internal structure analysis by factor-analyzing 225 evaluative person-descriptors drawn from Norman's (1967) excluded evaluative terms. They found five evaluative dimensions, three of which correspond to NV subfactors: **Depravity** (moral condemnation: *evildoer, damnable*), **Worthlessness** (inadequacy/powerlessness: *inconsequential, inessential*), and **Stupidity** (*wooden-headed, lunkhead*). This fractionation is important: NV is not monolithically about malevolence. It includes self-directed inadequacy content that cannot be reduced to antisocial callousness.

Simms, Yufik, Thomas, and Simms (2008, Journal of Research in Personality) developed the Evaluative Person Descriptors Questionnaire (EPDQ) across four data-collection rounds, treating NV content as a single **Depravity** scale (α \= .88; two-week test-retest r \= .73). Self-informant agreement for Depravity was within the same range as Big Five domains, directly countering claims that NV is merely response bias.

### **Relationship to the Big Five: reducible or residual?**

This is the most contested structural question. Simms (2007, Journal of Personality, N \= 403\) found that in joint factor analyses of Big Seven and Big Five scales, **NV loaded primarily on the Agreeableness factor** — specifically low Agreeableness — leading him to conclude NV "might be best understood as a specific, maladaptive aspect of low agreeableness." However, NV simultaneously showed **incremental prediction** of personality disorder variance beyond all five factors, meaning it captures variance not fully accounted for by Agreeableness alone.

Saucier and Goldberg (1998) investigated 53 potential dimensions and found NV was "reliable and substantially distinct from the FFM." Ashton and Lee (2001, 2002, European Journal of Personality) offered the strongest counterargument: evaluative terms are not personality-descriptive in the same way trait terms are; NV endorsement may reflect "a more global indication of positive or negative self-esteem" rather than specific personality characteristics. They argued this is inconsistent with the logic of the lexical approach.

The counter-rebuttal, advanced by Saucier (1994, 2002\) and Benet-Martínez and Waller (2002), holds that virtually all person-descriptors include both descriptive and evaluative components — the distinction is a false dichotomy. NV's temporal stability and consensual ratability in friendship dyads support its personality-construct status.

**The honest summary**: NV shares substantial variance with low Agreeableness, but it is not fully reducible to it. The residual variance appears clinically meaningful. Whether that residual is "personality" or "evaluation" depends on definitional choices the field has not resolved.

## **2\. NV spreads non-specifically across personality disorders**

### **Strongest evidence: three convergent studies**

Three studies provide the core evidence base for NV's relationship to personality disorders, and they converge on the same conclusion.

**Simms, Yufik, and Gros (2010, Personality Disorders: Theory, Research, and Treatment, N \= 338\)** used three independent markers of NV (IPC-7, EPDQ, IPIP) in hierarchical regressions with Big Five entered first. NV significantly incremented prediction of **9 of 12 SNAP-2 PD scales**: Narcissistic (ΔR² \= 11.7%), Histrionic (10.2%), Dependent (9.2%), Antisocial (5.9%), Schizotypal (3.6%), Paranoid (3.4%), Borderline (3.3%), Avoidant (3.2%), and Schizoid (3.1%). NV also predicted 8 of 15 SNAP-2 trait scales (ΔR² \= 2.8%–9.0%), including Mistrust, Manipulativeness, Self-Harm, and Eccentric Perceptions.

When restricted to core NV markers (IPC-7 and EPDQ only), the pattern narrowed: NV predicted only **Antisocial, Borderline, Paranoid, and Schizotypal** PDs — a more clinically coherent cluster. The broader IPIP NV scale inflated non-specificity by including dependency, exhibitionism, and narcissistic content.

**Durrett and Trull (2005, Psychological Assessment)** used the SIDP-IV structured interview for PD assessment — the only study using clinician-rated PDs rather than self-report. NV was non-specifically related to most personality disorders after controlling for the FFM. The Big Seven model accounted for more PD variance than the FFM "primarily because of the association of negative valence with most PDs."

**Simms (2007, Journal of Personality, N \= 403\)** confirmed NV incrementally predicted Borderline, Antisocial, Schizotypal PDs, and trait dimensions including Suicidality, Aggressive thoughts, and Manipulativeness.

### **General factor or specific profile?**

All three studies converge: NV's prediction pattern is **predominantly non-specific**. Simms et al. (2010) stated explicitly: "The relative non-specificity of NV suggests that individuals with most personality disorders are characterized by negatively valenced self-perceptions that go beyond the broad variance tapped by the Big Five." This pattern is conceptually consistent with a **p-factor-like** general vulnerability to personality pathology. However, **no study has directly tested** whether NV maps onto the p-factor (Caspi et al., 2014). This is a critical untested hypothesis.

### **Null findings to report**

NV did **not** incrementally predict Obsessive-Compulsive PD, Passive-Aggressive PD, or Depressive PD beyond the Big Five. NV also failed to predict several SNAP-2 trait scales: Negative Temperament, Aggression (as a standalone trait), Positive Temperament, Detachment, Impulsivity, Propriety, and Workaholism.

### **What has never been tested**

No published study examines NV's incremental validity **beyond the PID-5 domains**. No study places NV within the **HiTOP hierarchy**. No study uses **clinical/patient samples** rather than undergraduates. No study uses **longitudinal/prospective designs**. No study examines **informant-rated NV** predicting PDs. These are not merely gaps — they represent the entire clinical validation program that would be needed to establish NV as a clinical construct, and it has simply not been conducted.

## **3\. Double dissociations reveal NV's unique ego-dystonic signature**

### **NV versus Antagonism: overlapping low Agreeableness, divergent self-regard**

Both NV and PID-5 Antagonism are rooted in **low Agreeableness** (Simms, 2007; Krueger et al., 2012). NV correlates with SNAP-2 Manipulativeness (r \= .41–.54) and Aggression (r \= .39–.46), content that overlaps Antagonism. But the constructs diverge on a critical axis: **Antagonism is ego-syntonic** (callous exploitation experienced as acceptable or even positive), while **NV is ego-dystonic** (self-description as *evil*, *disgusting*, *deserving to be hated*). NV includes a self-directed moral condemnation component entirely absent from Antagonism. NV predicts internalizing outcomes (Self-Harm, Dependency, Avoidant PD) that Antagonism does not. No study has jointly factor-analyzed NV items alongside PID-5 Antagonism items.

### **NV versus HEXACO Honesty-Humility: one direct test**

A Serbian HEXACO validation study (Međedović, Čolović, Dinić, & Smederevac, approximately 2017\) provides the **only direct empirical evidence**: NV negatively correlated with all four facets of Honesty-Humility (Sincerity, Fairness, Greed-Avoidance, Modesty). The authors concluded "Positive and Negative Valence are both covered by Honesty-Humility." However, low H captures strategic, goal-directed exploitation (*I am entitled; I am clever*) with no self-condemnation. NV captures moral self-loathing (*I am evil; I am worthless*). A person low in H may feel perfectly comfortable with their manipulation; a person high in NV sees themselves as morally reprehensible. Ashton and Lee originally included a "Negative Self-Evaluation" interstitial facet in the HEXACO-PI, loading on low Extraversion and high Emotionality. They later **removed it** from the HEXACO-PI-R, judging that Social Self-Esteem plus Anxiety would capture its variance. This editorial decision may have inadvertently eliminated NV-relevant content from their model.

### **NV versus Dark Triad and D-factor: no direct evidence exists**

**No published study correlates NV measures with Dark Triad instruments** (SD3, Dirty Dozen, MACH-IV, NPI, LSRP). No study examines NV alongside Moshagen, Hilbig, and Zettler's (2018, Psychological Review) Dark Factor of Personality (D). The D-factor literature does not cite the Big Seven, and the Big Seven literature does not cite D. This is a clean case of literature siloing.

Conceptually, D requires **justifying beliefs** — the person high in D believes their exploitation is warranted. NV captures the **moral condemnation label** without requiring behavioral exploitation. NV may be closer to how D is perceived by others (or by the self in moments of moral insight) than to D's motivational core. The Vulnerable Dark Triad provides a partial bridge: Gamache et al. (2023, N \= 1,023) found the general Vulnerable Dark Triad factor was "mostly saturated with borderline symptoms items reflecting self-hatred and worthlessness" — content highly reminiscent of NV.

### **NV versus self-esteem: dissociable but related**

Simms (2007, N \= 403\) found that in a two-factor analysis, Rosenberg Self-Esteem loaded on Factor I alongside PV, Extraversion, and the Narcissistic Personality Inventory, while NV loaded on Factor II alongside Neuroticism and personality pathology traits. **Self-esteem and NV occupy different structural positions.** NV is not simply reversed self-esteem. NV carries specifically moral self-condemnation (*evil, wicked, immoral*) that generic self-esteem measures lack. This moral flavor is precisely what aligns NV with shame rather than with global self-worth.

## **4\. The NV-shame bridge that no one has built**

### **The absence of direct evidence is the finding**

After exhaustive search, **no study was found that measures both Big Seven NV and any shame-proneness instrument** (TOSCA, GASP, ESS, PFQ, or Internalized Shame Scale) in the same sample. This is the single most important finding of this entire review. The conceptual overlap is striking:

NV markers include *evil, wicked, awful, disgusting, deserve to be hated, immoral, worthless*. Tracy and Robins (2004) define shame as involving "negative feelings about the stable, global self" — the attribution pattern "I am bad" rather than guilt's "I did bad." Gilbert describes shame as "associated with a negative self-assessment... for example, 'I am unsuccessful, inadequate, ugly, and worthless.'" Tellegen and Waller themselves described PV and NV as "enduring self-construals of global evaluation." The language of **global negative self-evaluation** is essentially the definition of dispositional shame.

### **The shame/guilt distinction maps directly onto NV content**

Tracy and Robins' (2004) appraisal model distinguishes shame (internal, stable, uncontrollable, **global** attributions) from guilt (internal, unstable, controllable, **specific** attributions). NV content — "I am evil/wicked/worthless/disgusting" — maps onto shame's global self-condemnation, not guilt's behavior-specific self-critique. The Benet-Martínez and Waller (2002) NV subfactor called **Worthlessness** (*inconsequential, inessential*) captures the inadequacy/powerlessness component that is central to shame's experiential phenomenology. The **Depravity** subfactor (*evildoer, damnable*) captures shame's moral dimension.

### **Parallel empirical signatures**

Both NV and shame-proneness show nearly identical empirical profiles: non-specific prediction of personality disorders (especially BPD), incremental validity beyond Neuroticism/Big Five, correlation with but structural independence from low self-esteem, and association with both internalizing and externalizing pathology. Both predict BPD — NV consistently across Simms (2007), Simms et al. (2010), and Durrett and Trull (2005); shame meta-analytically confirmed by Buchman-Wildbaum et al. (2021, 10 studies). Simms et al. (2010) even quoted the DSM observation that BPD patients have "a self-image based on being bad or evil" — shame language — without connecting it to the shame literature.

A recent bifactor study (Mu et al., 2025\) found that the general factor shared by shame and guilt was associated with moral personality traits and inversely with externalizing psychopathology, but was **not** associated with Neuroticism — suggesting the unique moral-evaluative component of shame is distinct from emotional instability. This parallels NV's incremental validity beyond Neuroticism.

### **Why the siloing occurred**

Five factors explain the disconnection. **Methodological traditions**: NV emerged from bottom-up lexical factor analysis; shame-proneness from top-down emotion theory and clinical observation. **Exclusion history**: the Big Five explicitly excluded evaluative terms; when Tellegen and Waller included them, critics (McCrae & Costa, 1995\) attempted to subsume the results rather than asking what psychological construct NV might capture. **Labeling**: calling the factor "Negative Valence" — a statistical descriptor — rather than something psychologically evocative (e.g., "dispositional shame" or "moral self-condemnation") obscured the connection. **Field boundaries**: lexical personality researchers and emotion researchers attend different conferences, publish in different journals, and cite different literatures. **Level of analysis**: NV researchers studied a structural question (how many factors?); shame researchers studied a process question (what causes shame?). Neither group asked whether they were studying the same construct from different angles.

## **5\. Shame resistance, shamelessness, and two routes to low shame**

### **The compass of shame has reasonable empirical support**

Nathanson's (1992) four shame-response scripts (Withdrawal, Attack Self, Avoidance, Attack Other) were operationalized via the **Compass of Shame Scale (CoSS)** by Elison, Lennon, and Pulos (2006, Social Behavior and Personality, N \= 322). Confirmatory factor analysis supported the four-factor model. Subscale reliabilities ranged from **α \= .74 to .91**. Attack Self correlated with internalized shame, low self-esteem, and psychological symptoms; Attack Other correlated with anger and psychopathy; Avoidance showed the fewest pathological correlates. The CoSS has been validated cross-culturally (Schalkwijk et al., 2016, young offenders; Nyström & Mikkelsen, 2012, adolescents). **No study has directly tested** CoSS predictions against specific DSM personality disorder clusters, though the data are suggestive: externalizing shame coping maps onto Cluster B PDs, internalizing onto internalizing PDs.

### **Two routes to low shame: theoretically developed, empirically thin**

The distinction between healthy integrated shame and pathological absent shame is theoretically well-established (Wurmser; Cipriano et al., 2022, Frontiers in Psychology) but empirically underdeveloped. The primary/secondary psychopathy distinction provides partial support: **primary psychopathy** is unrelated to shame-proneness; **secondary psychopathy** is positively related (Prado et al., N \= 739). Campbell and Elison (2005, N \= 305\) found primary psychopathy was negatively related to internalizing shame coping (low withdrawal, low attack-self) while secondary psychopathy was positively related. Both were positively related to externalizing shame coping. **No study has directly compared securely-attached low-shame individuals with psychopathically low-shame individuals.** This remains a critical gap.

### **Psychopathy involves maladaptive shame coping, not absent shame generation**

The evidence increasingly supports a **"maladaptive coping" model** over a "never feeling shame" model. Garofalo and Velotti (2021, Journal of Criminal Justice, N \= 266 incarcerated violent offenders) found externalizing shame coping was positively related to psychopathic traits across all domains, while adaptive coping was negatively related only to affective traits. They concluded psychopathy involves "emotional functioning that is maladaptive *in kind*, rather than deficient *in absolute*." Ribeiro da Silva et al. (2021, N \= 703 youth) tested an evolutionary model: harsh rearing → external shame → maladaptive shame coping → psychopathic traits — supporting the conceptualization of psychopathy as learned shame externalization.

Tangney's longitudinal jail inmate study (2014, Psychological Science, N \= 476\) found shame-proneness predicted recidivism positively via externalization of blame, but when externalization was controlled, shame had a **negative** direct effect on recidivism. The "hide" component of shame was protective. Tangney concluded: "the only people who have no capacity for shame are psychopaths."

### **Shame and narcissism: subtype-dependent in the predicted direction**

The literature robustly distinguishes shame's role by narcissism subtype. **Vulnerable narcissism** is consistently, strongly associated with high shame-proneness (Hyatt et al., 2017; Poless et al., 2018; Ritter et al., 2014, Psychiatry Research, N \= 28 NPD \+ 34 controls). **Grandiose narcissism** is negatively associated with shame when controlling for vulnerability. Garofalo et al. (2020, Journal of Research in Personality, daily diary study) found vulnerable narcissism was positively related to daily shame, while daily grandiose narcissism was mostly negatively related. Schoenleber and Berenbaum (2012, Journal of Abnormal Psychology) proposed shame-regulation as a core feature of all pathological narcissism — grandiose fantasies, perfectionism, aggressive behavior, and social withdrawal are all shame regulation strategies. This is theoretically elegant: grandiose narcissism is the attack-other pole of Nathanson's compass; vulnerable narcissism is the attack-self/withdrawal pole. Both are responses to the same underlying shame.

## **6\. Behavior genetics and cross-cultural evidence are nearly empty**

### **No heritability estimate for NV exists**

Despite NV being proposed in 1987, **no twin study has examined its heritability**. Big Five heritability estimates cluster at approximately **.40–.60** (Jang et al., 1996). Whether NV follows the same genetic architecture is unknown. Given NV's content (extreme negative self-evaluation, low-frequency endorsement, skewed distributions) and its relationship to personality pathology, it is plausible NV would show either lower heritability (more environmental influence from abuse/trauma) or substantial nonadditive genetic variance. Neither hypothesis has been tested. No GWAS has examined NV or close proxies.

The heritability evidence for shame-proneness is almost equally sparse. **Only one twin study exists**: Zahn-Waxler et al. found relatively higher heritability for shame and higher shared environment for guilt in toddlers, but the sample was small and has never been replicated. Bender et al. (2015, PLOS ONE, N \= 271 adolescents) found the first gene-environment interaction for self-conscious emotions: 5-HTTLPR moderated trauma's effect on shame, while BDNF Val66Met moderated guilt. This is suggestive but isolated.

Environmental origins of shame are better documented. A 2025 meta-analysis of 16 studies (N \= 5,306) in *Child Abuse & Neglect* found general child maltreatment was significantly associated with shame (**r \= .40**), with all five subtypes (physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, emotional neglect) contributing. **No study has tested whether NV scores are elevated in maltreatment survivors.** The content overlap (NV's "worthless, disgusting, evil" with internalized shame following abuse) strongly suggests they would be.

### **Cross-cultural NV depends on methodological choices**

NV replicates cross-culturally when inclusive variable selection is used and disappears when restrictive selection is applied. This is not an empirical disagreement — it is a methodological one. In shame/guilt cultures specifically, neural evidence (2025 meta-analysis, 62 neuroimaging studies) shows shame elicits stronger medial cingulate cortex activation in East Asian cultures than Western cultures. Cross-cultural research on cultural values (2022, International Journal of Intercultural Relations) finds Asian values positively related to shame-proneness and European American values positively related to guilt-proneness. **No study examines whether NV's structural position varies across shame versus guilt cultures.** In principle, NV might emerge more readily as a separate factor in cultures where negative self-evaluation is a more differentiated psychological space, but this remains entirely speculative.

## **7\. Active labs and the siloing landscape**

### **NV is effectively orphaned**

**Auke Tellegen died in 2023/2024** (Society for Personality Assessment obituary). The intellectual originator of the Big Seven is gone. **Niels Waller** (University of Minnesota) co-originated the Big Seven but his recent work (2023–2025) is exclusively psychometric methodology. **Leonard Simms** (University at Buffalo) published the key clinical NV papers (2007, 2010\) and developed the EPDQ, but has shifted to HiTOP and Computerized Adaptive Testing of Personality Disorders. His current NIMH grant on "Negative Valence Measures" refers to RDoC Negative Valence Systems (fear, anxiety, frustration), not Big Seven NV — even the most active NV-adjacent researcher has changed terminology. **Gerard Saucier** (University of Oregon) developed the ML7 but now favors a Big Six model. No researcher is currently actively championing NV as a personality dimension.

### **Shame personality research is active but disconnected**

**June Tangney** (George Mason University) remains very active through 2025, currently focusing on shame/guilt in criminal justice populations. **Jessica Tracy** (UBC) continues evolutionary and functional work on pride and shame. **Paul Gilbert** (University of Derby) continues Compassion Focused Therapy development targeting shame. None of these researchers cite the Big Seven or NV. **Thomas Scheff** (UC Santa Barbara), pioneer of sociological shame theory, died in 2023 at age 93\.

### **Dark Core research is thriving but equally disconnected**

**Morten Moshagen** (Ulm University), **Benjamin Hilbig** (RPTU Kaiserslautern-Landau), and **Ingo Zettler** (University of Copenhagen) continue active D-factor research. Their conceptual ancestor is low Honesty-Humility, not NV. They have never referenced the Big Seven.

### **No bridge researchers exist**

**No researcher has explicitly attempted to connect Big Seven NV to dispositional shame.** The closest structural hint is the Serbian HEXACO validation showing NV maps onto low Honesty-Humility, combined with HEXACO's removed Negative Self-Evaluation facet. Benet-Martínez and Waller (2002) analyzed the internal structure of evaluative terms but included no shame measures. The three literatures — NV/personality structure, shame/emotion, and dark personality — operate in near-complete mutual isolation.

## **8\. Papers the reader should read themselves**

The following **five papers** are most essential for anyone pursuing this line of research:

* **Benet-Martínez & Waller (2002)**, "From Adorable to Worthless," *European Journal of Personality*, 16, 1–41. The definitive analysis of NV's internal structure, identifying Depravity, Worthlessness, and Stupidity subfactors. This is where the shame-NV connection becomes most visible in the data, even though the authors never make it.

* **Simms, Yufik, & Gros (2010)**, "Incremental Validity of Positive and Negative Valence," *Personality Disorders: Theory, Research, and Treatment*, 1, 77–86. The strongest empirical demonstration that NV predicts PD variance beyond the Big Five, with the critical operationalization-dependent finding that core NV (depravity) versus broad NV show different specificity patterns.

* **Tracy & Robins (2004)**, "Putting the Self Into Self-Conscious Emotions," *Psychological Inquiry*, 15, 103–125. The appraisal-based model of shame as global self-evaluation. Reading this alongside Tellegen's NV description reveals the constructs are defined in nearly identical language.

* **Garofalo & Velotti (2021)**, "Shame Coping and Psychopathy," *Journal of Criminal Justice*, 76, 101845\. The most rigorous examination of shame processing in psychopathic individuals, using incarcerated violent offenders (N \= 266\) and controlling for general emotion dysregulation.

* **Schoenleber & Berenbaum (2012)**, "Shame Regulation in Personality Pathology," *Journal of Abnormal Psychology*, 121, 433–446. The most theoretically developed framework for how shame regulation strategies map onto different personality disorders.

## **Conclusion: an integrative hypothesis hiding in plain sight**

The evidence reviewed here supports a specific, testable hypothesis that has never been formally articulated: **NV is the lexical fingerprint of dispositional shame as it appears in natural language when evaluative self-descriptions are permitted to enter factor analysis.** The content alignment is near-perfect (global moral self-condemnation), the empirical signatures are parallel (non-specific PD prediction, incremental validity beyond Neuroticism, association with BPD and both internalizing and externalizing pathology), and the subfactor structure of NV (Depravity, Worthlessness, Stupidity) maps onto shame's appraisal dimensions (moral, competence, social).

Three findings complicate this hypothesis. First, NV includes content — particularly the malevolence markers (*evil, cruel, depraved*) — that reads more like other-directed aggression than self-conscious emotion. This content might reflect what a chronically shamed person becomes (the attack-other pole of Nathanson's compass) rather than shame itself. Second, NV's ego-dystonic quality distinguishes it from the dark personality constructs (Antagonism, low H, Dark Triad, D-factor), but it also raises the question of whether high-NV individuals are genuinely reporting stable self-perceptions or performing penitential self-presentation. Third, the near-total absence of behavior genetic data means we cannot determine whether NV and shame-proneness share genetic architecture — a necessary condition for construct identity.

The field's failure to test this hypothesis is not a mystery. It is a predictable consequence of how personality psychology organized itself: by excluding evaluative terms from the Big Five, the field ensured that shame-as-personality would have no structural home. When Tellegen and Waller found it anyway, the result was labeled with a statistical name ("Negative Valence") rather than a psychological one, and critics debated whether it was "real personality" rather than asking what it meant. Meanwhile, shame researchers built their literature without reference to personality structure. The result is two communities, each holding half of the picture, with no one holding both halves at once. The empirical test — administering the EPDQ Depravity scale alongside the TOSCA and GASP in a large, clinically diverse sample — would be straightforward. It has simply never been done.
