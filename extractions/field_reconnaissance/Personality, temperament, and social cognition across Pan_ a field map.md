# **Personality, temperament, and social cognition across *Pan*: a field map**

**Chimpanzees and bonobos share four of six personality dimensions but diverge strikingly on two — and the species differences that seemed sharpest a decade ago are now the most actively contested.** Using the Hominoid Personality Questionnaire across \~154 bonobos and 500+ chimpanzees, researchers have established fair-to-good cross-species congruence for Assertiveness/Dominance, Conscientiousness, Openness, and Agreeableness, but not for Attentiveness (found only in bonobos) or Extraversion (structurally different in each species). Experimental work has documented replicated species differences in risk preference, patience, and spatial memory, with chimpanzees scoring higher on all three. But the core narrative that bonobos are categorically more tolerant, less aggressive, and more empathic than chimpanzees has been substantially complicated by recent evidence: wild male bonobos show **2.8× more aggressive interactions** than chimpanzees, sanctuary bonobos and chimpanzees console at equal rates, and a partial replication of the foundational co-feeding tolerance study found only a trend-level species difference. The field is small, captive-dominated, and sample-limited for bonobos — the entire personality-assessed bonobo population numbers roughly 170–200 individuals, compared to 650+ chimpanzees.

## **Section 1: Comparative summary**

### **1\. Personality factor structure: four shared dimensions, two divergent**

The foundational chimpanzee study (King & Figueredo, 1997; N=100 zoo chimpanzees) identified six factors accounting for 72.4% of variance: **Dominance, Extraversion, Conscientiousness, Agreeableness, Neuroticism, and Openness**. This six-factor structure has been replicated in Japanese zoo chimpanzees (Weiss et al., 2009; N=157) and, with some variation, in multiple captive populations. In laboratory-housed chimpanzees, Neuroticism sometimes fails to emerge as a distinct factor, merging instead with a broader Reactivity/Undependability dimension (Weiss et al., 2007, N=175 at Yerkes; Freeman et al., 2013, N=99 at MD Anderson).

The primary bonobo study (Weiss et al., 2015; N=154 captive bonobos from 16 US and European facilities, representing \~80% of the captive population) also extracted six factors, explaining **56% of variance**: **Assertiveness, Conscientiousness, Openness, Attentiveness, Agreeableness, and Extraversion**. Cross-species comparison via orthogonal targeted Procrustes rotations with Tucker's congruence coefficients (φ) yielded these results against three chimpanzee reference samples:

| Bonobo factor | ChimpanZoo (N=100) | Yerkes (N=175) | Japan (N=157) |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Assertiveness | **.95** (good) | .85 (fair) | **.90** (fair) |
| Conscientiousness | **.95** (good) | **.96** (good) | **.92** (fair) |
| Openness | **.95** (good) | .81 (poor) | **.91** (fair) |
| Attentiveness | .66 (poor) | .79 (poor) | .78 (poor) |
| Agreeableness | **.91** (fair) | **.91** (fair) | **.93** (fair) |
| Extraversion | .85 (fair) | .69 (poor) | .78 (poor) |

Interpretation thresholds follow Lorenzo-Seva & ten Berge (2006): below .85 indicates no similarity; .85–.94 is fair; ≥.95 is good. **Four factors — Assertiveness/Dominance, Conscientiousness, Openness, and Agreeableness — meet or approach the fair-similarity threshold across most comparisons.** Two factors clearly fail: Attentiveness (φ \= .66–.79) has no chimpanzee analogue, and Extraversion (φ \= .69–.85) is structurally reconfigured across species.

The relabeling of chimpanzee Dominance as bonobo **Assertiveness** was justified on two grounds: (1) the bonobo factor's highest-loading items (anxious \[−.81\], timid \[−.76\], fearful \[−.75\], independent \[.71\], dominant \[.68\]) more closely resemble the Assertiveness facet of human Extraversion in the Costa & McCrae framework than a pure competitive-dominance construct, and (2) the label avoids implying the male-dominated competitive framework evoked by "Dominance" in a species where females hold higher social rank.

Behavioral correlates have been established for both species. In chimpanzees, Freeman et al. (2013) correlated personality ratings with two years of behavioral coding in 99 individuals: Dominance predicted display, intervention, and proximity behaviors; Agreeableness predicted affiliation; Extraversion predicted play and sexual behavior. In bonobos, Staes et al. (2016, 2017\) linked Assertiveness to receiving more allogrooming, closer proximity to conspecifics, and receiving less aggression — suggesting **affiliative dominance** rather than agonistic dominance. In wild chimpanzees at Gombe (Weiss et al., 2017; N=128), higher Dominance and lower Conscientiousness predicted rank; Dominance predicted siring success.

### **2\. Factors present in one species but absent in the other**

**Attentiveness (bonobos only)** is defined by intelligent (.68), not disorganized (−.71), not clumsy (−.68), not thoughtless (−.68), and not distractible (−.59). Its absence in chimpanzees is a robust statistical finding, not merely a null result: congruence coefficients across all three chimpanzee samples were uniformly below .80. In chimpanzees, these items distribute across Dominance (intelligent, decisive, perceptive) and Conscientiousness (organized, not clumsy). The items still vary in chimpanzees but do not coalesce into a distinct dimension. Weiss et al. (2015) interpreted this as human Conscientiousness splitting into two factors in bonobos: one capturing deliberation and low impulsive aggression (Conscientiousness) and one capturing order, focus, and self-discipline (Attentiveness). An analogous split has been reported in brown capuchin monkeys.

**Neuroticism (chimpanzees, variably)** emerges in zoo-housed chimpanzees (King & Figueredo, 1997; Weiss et al., 2009\) but not in laboratory chimpanzees and not in bonobos. In bonobos, the items that define chimpanzee Neuroticism (anxious, fearful, vulnerable, excitable) load as reversed Assertiveness; items like "depressed" and "autistic" load as reversed Extraversion. This is a structural difference: the traits exist and vary in bonobos, but they organize differently. No separate Neuroticism factor emerged in either captive or wild bonobo samples. The instability of Neuroticism even within chimpanzees — its disappearance in some laboratory populations — suggests it may be particularly sensitive to housing conditions.

**Extraversion** exists in both species but is compositionally distinct. In chimpanzees, Extraversion loads on active, playful, sociable, and their reverses (solitary, lazy). In bonobos, "active" and "playful" shift to Openness, and Extraversion is defined primarily by the absence of social withdrawal: not solitary (−.67), not depressed (−.67). This reorganization may reflect the different social functions of play across species: in bonobos, play serves tension-reduction and trust-building functions more closely aligned with exploration/openness than sociability per se.

### **3\. Experimentally established temperament and cognitive differences**

**Risk preference.** Chimpanzees are more risk-prone than bonobos across multiple studies. The earliest comparison (Heilbronner et al., 2008\) used only 5 individuals per species, but the direction has been replicated in Rosati & Hare (2011, 2013\) with larger samples (up to 23 chimps and 15 bonobos). Haux et al. (2023) raised concern that sample sizes of 4–24 may be underpowered to detect small-to-medium effects and that task design influences magnitude. The direction is generally robust; the magnitude remains uncertain.

**Patience.** Chimpanzees choose delayed larger rewards more often than bonobos (Rosati et al., 2007: F(1,55) \= 18.78, p \< .001; replicated in Rosati & Hare, 2013). Both species are remarkably patient compared to most animals tested.

**Neophobia.** A single large study (Herrmann et al., 2011\) found bonobos more cautious than chimpanzees toward novel objects and novel people. Some researchers distinguish between social neophilia (bonobos may be more attracted to strangers) and non-social neophobia (more cautious with objects), but this distinction awaits systematic testing.

**Social tolerance and co-feeding.** The seminal Hare et al. (2007) study found bonobos co-fed more and cooperated more than chimpanzees. However, this finding has been substantially challenged. Nolte et al. (2023) conducted a conceptual replication with both species at the same facility and found only a **trend** for greater bonobo co-feeding (p \= .074); species differences in play and socio-sexual behavior were non-significant. More strikingly, Jaeggi et al. (2010), studying captive groups at the Royal Zoological Society of Antwerp, found **chimpanzees shared more frequently, more tolerantly, and more actively than bonobos**, directly reversing the standard narrative. The evidence quality for a categorical species difference in social tolerance is now considerably weaker than widely assumed.

**Theory of mind and social cognition.** The Primate Cognition Test Battery (Herrmann et al., 2010\) found bonobos outperformed chimpanzees on tasks assessing understanding of social causality, while chimpanzees outperformed on physical causality and tool use. This is a single large study comparing sanctuary populations at Lola ya Bonobo and Tchimpounga. On false belief tasks (Krupenye et al., 2016, 2019), no species differences were found: chimpanzees, bonobos, and orangutans all passed anticipatory-looking measures, with 41 and 47 apes tested respectively.

**Physical cognition and tool use.** Chimpanzees outperformed bonobos on physical cognition tasks in the PCTB (single study) and are prolific tool users in the wild, while bonobos rarely use tools in natural settings. Notably, captive bonobos demonstrate capable tool use, suggesting the wild difference reflects ecological context rather than underlying capacity. A 2025 study found no species differences in captive tool-use performance, reinforcing this interpretation.

**Spatial memory.** Chimpanzees demonstrate superior spatial memory in foraging paradigms, with adult bonobos performing at the level of juvenile chimpanzees (Rosati & Hare, 2012; Rosati, 2019). This finding has been replicated and interpreted through the lens of heterochrony — bonobos retain juvenilized spatial cognition, possibly reflecting reduced foraging demands.

**Emotional reactivity.** A single but influential study (Wobber et al., 2010, PNAS) measured hormonal responses during dyadic food competition. Bonobo males showed anticipatory **cortisol** increases (stress framing), while chimpanzee males showed anticipatory **testosterone** increases (dominance framing). Developmental work (Wobber et al., 2010, Current Biology) found bonobos retain juvenile levels of play into adulthood, while chimpanzees show age-related declines in food-context play.

**Response to distress.** A critical finding from 2025 overturns a common assumption: Brooker et al. (2025, Evolution and Human Behavior) directly compared consolation behavior in 40 bonobos (Lola ya Bonobo) and 50 chimpanzees (Chimfunshi Wildlife Orphanage) and found **both species consoled at similar rates**, with within-species variation eclipsing between-species differences. Earlier work by Webb et al. (2017) documented stable individual differences in consolation tendency in chimpanzees over a decade, suggesting "empathetic personalities." On prosociality toward strangers, bonobos show a distinctive pattern: Tan & Hare (2013) found bonobos voluntarily shared food with unfamiliar individuals and helped strangers obtain food, a pattern not documented in chimpanzees. However, bonobos failed the standard prosocial choice paradigm (Tan et al., 2015), highlighting paradigm sensitivity.

**Contagious yawning** data from both species reveals a potential difference in social scope: chimpanzees show contagious yawning preferentially with in-group members, while bonobos respond equally to group-mates and strangers (Tan et al., 2017; Campbell & de Waal, 2011).

### **4\. Sex differences: a dramatic reversal in dominance**

The most striking sex-difference finding is a **reversal in Assertiveness/Dominance**: males score higher than females in chimpanzees, but **females score higher than males in bonobos** (Staes et al., 2017). Female bonobos also score higher on Extraversion. This reversal directly maps onto species differences in social organization: male chimpanzees dominate females and form the core of the social hierarchy, while female bonobos achieve higher rank through coalition formation and occupy more central social positions.

In chimpanzees, King et al. (2008; N=202) found males rated higher on Dominance and females higher on Agreeableness, with sex differences exceeding comparable human gender differences. Age patterns parallel those in humans: declines in Extraversion and Openness, increases in Agreeableness and Conscientiousness with age.

In wild bonobos (Garai et al., 2016; N=16), males scored higher on the behavioral factor Introversion and lower on questionnaire-rated Irritability. This small-sample wild finding is broadly consistent with the captive data showing males as less socially central.

No other clear sex reversals have been documented beyond Dominance/Assertiveness and Extraversion, though Conscientiousness showed sex-by-age effects in bonobos that differed from those in chimpanzees and humans.

### **5\. Genetic and neurobiological substrates**

**Heritability.** In bonobos (Staes et al., 2016; N=112 rated, N=43 behaviorally coded, pedigree-based animal models), all ten personality dimensions showed nonzero heritabilities, ranging from **h² \= 0.17** (Extraversion, rated) to **h² \= 0.58** (Sociability, behavioral coding) in intercept models. Best-fit models with covariates yielded lower estimates (0.06–0.19). In chimpanzees, the earliest study (Weiss et al., 2000; N=99) found only Dominance significantly heritable at **h² \= 0.63**, but later pedigree-based studies using larger samples and Bayesian animal models (Wilson et al., 2017, N=129; Latzman et al., 2015, N=238) found broader heritability across multiple dimensions. The median heritability across primate personality studies is approximately 0.25. No GWAS has been conducted in either species.

**AVPR1A (vasopressin receptor 1A)** represents the best-studied genetic locus. Chimpanzees are polymorphic for a \~360-bp deletion (DupB) in the 5′ promoter region. **Bonobos entirely lack the DupB deletion**, making the bonobos' promoter region more similar to that of humans. In chimpanzees, the DupB+ (long) allele is associated with higher Conscientiousness, lower Extraversion, and greater sociability across multiple independent studies (Hopkins et al., 2012; Anestis et al., 2014; Staes et al., 2015; Wilson et al., 2017\) — the best-replicated gene-personality finding in nonhuman primates. In bonobos, longer RS3 alleles at this locus are associated with higher Attentiveness and lower behavioral Openness (Staes et al., 2016). Broader coding-sequence analysis (Staes et al., 2021\) revealed bonobos have **57% more fixed substitutions** than chimpanzees in four neurochemical receptor genes (AVPR1A, OXTR, DRD2, HTR1A), while chimpanzees show **31% more polymorphic coding variation**.

**Serotonergic differences** are among the most dramatic neurochemical findings. Stimpson et al. (2016; N=6 per species, postmortem) found bonobo amygdalae contain **more than twice the density of serotonin transporter-immunoreactive axons**, particularly in the basal and central nuclei. Male bonobos show even higher serotonergic innervation than female bonobos in the central nucleus — a sexual dimorphism absent in chimpanzees. This finding parallels elevated serotonin levels in foxes and rats selectively bred for reduced aggression, but comes from a single small-sample study.

**Neuroanatomy.** Rilling et al. (2012; N=3 bonobos, 4 chimpanzees in vivo; 3+3 cadaver DTI) found bonobos have more gray matter in the right dorsal amygdala, right anterior insula, hypothalamus, and ventral anterior cingulate cortex — regions implicated in empathy and emotional processing. Chimpanzees have more gray matter in primary motor cortex, visual cortex, and hippocampus — consistent with greater tool use and spatial memory. Bonobos possess a **larger pathway connecting the amygdala to the ventral anterior cingulate cortex**, potentially supporting both top-down aggression control and bottom-up empathic responding. Microstructural studies (Issa et al., 2019; N=10 per species, postmortem histology) confirmed greater neuropil fraction in bonobo amygdala subnuclei and subgenual ACC. These findings are compelling but come from very small samples and postmortem tissue.

**Hormonal ontogeny.** Chimpanzee males show a clear developmental rise in testosterone; bonobo males show minimal pubertal testosterone increases (Wobber et al., 2013). Bonobos respond to feeding competition with cortisol; chimpanzees respond with testosterone (Wobber et al., 2010). These hormonal profiles align with species differences in male competitive strategies.

**Self-domestication hypothesis.** Hare et al. (2012) proposed bonobos underwent selection against reactive aggression analogous to domestication, producing correlated trait changes including greater social tolerance, increased play, reduced spatial memory, and altered emotional reactivity. Morphological evidence includes reduced cranial size (up to 20% reduction in male cranium), shorter canines, and retention of juvenile features. This framework has been substantially challenged by Mouginot et al. (2024), who found wild male bonobos engage in **2.8× more aggressive interactions** and **3× more physical aggressions** than chimpanzees at Gombe using gold-standard focal-follow methodology (12 bonobo males, 14 chimpanzee males). However, bonobo aggression was exclusively male-male and individualistic, while chimpanzee aggression more often targeted females and used coalitions (13.2% vs. 1%). This distinction has prompted a refinement: the relevant selection may have targeted reactive aggression specifically, while proactive/coalitionary aggression was maintained or elaborated differently in each species.

### **6\. Coalition behavior and individual differences in aggression**

**Chimpanzee patrol participation** is remarkably uniform rather than individually variable. Massaro et al. (2022; Gombe, 30 years of data, 1,945 patrol opportunities) found males participated in a mean of **74.5% ± 11.1%** of patrols, and personality scores from a modified HPQ **did not significantly predict participation**. Sighting frequency (gregariousness), age, and hunting participation were the best predictors. Separately, Gilby et al. (2013) showed that impact hunters and impact patrollers were **different individuals** at Gombe, arguing against a unitary aggressive/bold behavioral syndrome. At Ngogo, Watts & Mitani (2001) found some individual variation in patrol frequency with an inverted-U age relationship, and individual patrol frequency correlated with mating success and coalition formation.

**Bonobo aggression** has been revised upward. Wild data from Kokolopori and Gombe (Mouginot et al., 2024\) showed male bonobos were more frequently aggressive than male chimpanzees. Bryon et al. (2026; 22 zoo-housed groups) found no overall species difference in aggression rates when accounting for group size and sex ratio, but aggression patterns differed by sex: bonobos showed higher female-to-male aggression, chimpanzees showed the reverse. Female coalitions are central to bonobo power dynamics: Surbeck et al. (2025; 30 years of data from 6 wild communities) found females target males in **85% of their coalitions** and achieve higher rank when forming more frequent coalitions. A first documented lethal incident during a bonobo intergroup encounter was reported in 2026 from Kokolopori (an infant death following coalitionary aggression on a female), challenging the view that bonobos entirely lack lethal intergroup violence — though this differs qualitatively from chimpanzee coalitionary killing.

**Deception** research is almost entirely restricted to chimpanzees. The Byrne & Whiten tactical deception database identifies *Pan* as the highest-scoring genus, and experimental work (Hare et al., 2006\) demonstrates flexible use of competitor-knowledge in chimpanzees. No systematic comparative study of deception frequency exists between species, and no study has linked personality dimensions to deceptive behavior in either species. This is a major gap.

**Reconciliation** patterns diverge from expectation. Surbeck et al. (2017) found lower reconciliation frequencies among wild bonobos than described in captive populations, while chimpanzees showed robust reconciliation rates (mean conciliatory tendency \~41% across studies). Chimpanzees may invest more in relationship repair because male-male coalitions are more critical to their fitness.

### **7\. Methodological concerns**

**Instrument limitations.** The HPQ was developed from human Five-Factor Model items, creating a potential top-down anthropocentric bias. Items calibrated to chimpanzee behavioral repertoires may inadequately capture bonobo-specific personality variation, such as the use of socio-sexual behavior as a social tool or the dynamics of female coalition formation. Jana Uher and colleagues have offered the most sustained critique, arguing that questionnaires reflect "retrospective memory-based judgments containing stereotypical biases" and proposing bottom-up behavioral coding as superior. However, multiple validation studies show convergent validity between ratings and behavioral observations (Vazire et al., 2007; Freeman et al., 2013; Staes et al., 2016), and ratings have been validated against genetic polymorphisms, brain morphology, and life outcomes.

**Captive-wild generalizability** is a critical concern. In wild chimpanzees at Gombe (N=128), personality dimensions assessed via Swahili HPQ were "not as interpretable as those from captive samples." In wild bonobos (N=16), the factor structure was markedly different from captive bonobos, with no clear Dominance/Assertiveness factor emerging. However, the wild bonobo sample was far too small for reliable factor analysis. Captive environments lack fission-fusion dynamics, natural foraging challenges, predator pressure, and intergroup competition — all of which could alter personality expression. A 2025 study found no captive species differences in tool use despite marked wild differences, demonstrating that captivity can mask ecologically driven behavioral divergence.

**Sample sizes.** The total personality-assessed bonobo population numbers approximately **170–200 unique individuals** (154 captive via HPQ \+ 16 wild \+ smaller behavioral-only samples). The chimpanzee total exceeds **650 unique individuals** (538 captive in the largest database \+ 128 wild at Gombe \+ additional unique samples). The ratio is approximately 3–4:1. The captive bonobo HPQ sample of 154 represents \~80% of the US and European captive population, meaning the ceiling for captive bonobo personality research is effectively reached. Wild bonobo personality data is severely limited.

**Facility confounds** pervade experimental comparisons. Most studies test bonobos and chimpanzees at different sanctuaries in different countries (Lola ya Bonobo in DRC vs. Tchimpounga in Republic of Congo), making it difficult to separate species effects from environmental effects. The Nolte et al. (2023) study, which housed both species at the same facility, represents a methodological advance and notably weakened the tolerance finding.

**No dedicated systematic review or meta-analysis** comparing personality specifically across *Pan* species exists. Freeman & Gosling (2010) reviewed primate personality broadly, and Weiss et al. (2015) provided the most direct cross-species comparison, but a formal meta-analytic synthesis is lacking.

## **Section 2: Annotated source list**

**1\. King, J. E., & Figueredo, A. J. (1997).** The Five-Factor Model plus Dominance in chimpanzee personality. *Journal of Research in Personality, 31*, 257–271. Foundational study establishing the 6-factor personality structure in 100 zoo chimpanzees using 43 trait-descriptive adjectives. Demonstrated that chimpanzee personality extends the human Five-Factor Model with an additional Dominance factor. Interrater reliability η \= .75. Limited to US zoo populations; no behavioral validation in this study. **\[personality structure\]**

**2\. Weiss, A., Staes, N., Pereboom, J. J. M., Inoue-Murayama, M., Stevens, J. M. G., & Eens, M. (2015).** Bonobo personality traits are heritable and associated with vasopressin receptor gene 1a variation. *Psychological Science, 26*(9), 1430–1439. The primary reference for bonobo personality factor structure. Assessed 154 captive bonobos from 16 facilities using the 54-item HPQ. Identified six factors, compared them via Procrustes rotation to three chimpanzee samples, and documented Attentiveness as a bonobo-unique dimension. Excellent sample relative to population size; captive-only; behavioral validation reported separately. **\[personality structure\]**

**3\. Staes, N., Weiss, A., Helsen, P., Korody, M., Eens, M., & Stevens, J. M. G. (2016).** Bonobo personality traits are heritable and associated with vasopressin receptor gene 1a variation. *Scientific Reports, 6*, 38193\. Estimated heritability for all 10 bonobo personality dimensions (6 rated, 4 behavioral) using pedigree-based Bayesian animal models. All dimensions showed nonzero heritabilities. Also linked AVPR1A RS3 allele length to Attentiveness and Openness. Captive population; heritability estimates may not generalize to wild. **\[genetics/neurobiology, personality structure\]**

**4\. Herrmann, E., Hare, B., Call, J., & Tomasello, M. (2010).** Differences in the cognitive skills of bonobos and chimpanzees. *PLoS ONE, 5*(8), e12438. Primate Cognition Test Battery comparison: bonobos outperformed chimpanzees on social causality/theory of mind tasks; chimpanzees outperformed on physical causality/tool use. Tested at Lola ya Bonobo and Tchimpounga sanctuaries. Single study; facility differences not controlled. **\[temperament/cognition\]**

**5\. Rosati, A. G., & Hare, B. (2013).** Chimpanzees and bonobos exhibit emotional responses to decision outcomes. *PLoS ONE, 8*(5), e63058. Compared 23 chimpanzees and 15 bonobos on risk and temporal discounting tasks, confirming chimpanzees are more risk-seeking and more patient. Documented emotional responses (vocalizations, scratching) to decision outcomes. Moderate sample sizes; sanctuary populations. **\[temperament/cognition\]**

**6\. Wobber, V., Hare, B., Maboto, J., Lipson, S., Wrangham, R., & Ellison, P. T. (2010).** Differential changes in steroid hormones before competition in bonobos and chimpanzees. *PNAS, 107*(28), 12457–12462. Demonstrated species differences in hormonal appraisal of competition: bonobo males show cortisol (stress) anticipation; chimpanzee males show testosterone (dominance) anticipation. Single study; novel saliva-collection methodology; influential in self-domestication framework. **\[temperament/cognition, genetics/neurobiology\]**

**7\. Rilling, J. K., Scholz, J., Preuss, T. M., Glasser, M. F., Errangi, B. K., & Behrens, T. E. (2012).** Differences between chimpanzees and bonobos in neural systems supporting social cognition. *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, 7*(4), 369–379. First DTI and VBM comparison of Pan brains. Bonobos showed more gray matter in social-emotional regions; chimpanzees in motor/spatial regions. Larger amygdala-to-ACC pathway in bonobos. Very small samples (3–4 per species for in vivo; 3+3 for DTI); partially supported by later microstructural studies. **\[genetics/neurobiology\]**

**8\. Stimpson, C. D., Barger, N., Taglialatela, J. P., Gendron-Fitzpatrick, A., Hof, P. R., Hopkins, W. D., & Sherwood, C. C. (2016).** Differential serotonergic innervation of the amygdala in bonobos and chimpanzees. *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, 11*(3), 413–422. Found bonobos have \>2× the density of serotonin transporter-immunoreactive axons in the amygdala compared to chimpanzees. Small sample (N=6 per species, postmortem); control regions showed no species differences, supporting specificity. Parallels domestication-related serotonin changes. **\[genetics/neurobiology\]**

**9\. Mouginot, M., Wilson, M. L., Desai, N., & Surbeck, M. (2024).** Differences in expression of male aggression between wild bonobos and chimpanzees. *Current Biology, 34*, 1780–1785. Used gold-standard focal-follow methodology (841 focal follows, 7,309 observation hours, 14 community-years) to show wild male bonobos engage in 2.8× more aggressive interactions and 3× as many physical aggressions than chimpanzees. Qualitative differences preserved: bonobo aggression is male-male and individualistic (13.2% coalitionary in chimps vs. 1% in bonobos); chimpanzee aggression targets females and uses coalitions. Small sample (12 bonobo males at Kokolopori, DRC; 14 chimpanzee males at Gombe, Tanzania); paradigm-challenging. **\[aggression/coalitions\]**

**10\. Massaro, J. A., Gilby, I. C., Desai, N., Weiss, A., Feldblum, J. T., Pusey, A. E., & Wilson, M. L. (2022).** Are chimpanzee patrol participants altruistic? *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 377*, 20210151\. Thirty-year Gombe dataset (1,945 patrol opportunities, 23 males). Found personality scores did not predict patrol participation; most males participated at high rates (\~75%). Demonstrated that personality, as currently measured, does not explain variation in this critical domain of chimpanzee behavior. **\[aggression/coalitions, methodology\]**

**11\. Freeman, H. D., Brosnan, S. F., Hopper, L. M., Lambeth, S. P., Schapiro, S. J., & Gosling, S. D. (2013).** Developing a comprehensive and comparative questionnaire for measuring personality in chimpanzees using a simultaneous top-down/bottom-up design. *American Journal of Primatology, 75*, 1042–1053. Combined top-down (FFM) and bottom-up (chimpanzee-specific) item development in 99 laboratory chimpanzees. Found 5–6 factors with behavioral validation. Identified Reactivity/Undependability as absorbing Neuroticism items. Important for methodological debate about anthropocentric bias in item selection. **\[personality structure, methodology\]**

**12\. Hare, B., Wobber, V., & Wrangham, R. (2012).** The self-domestication hypothesis: Evolution of bonobo psychology is due to selection against aggression. *Animal Behaviour, 83*, 573–585. Influential review synthesizing evidence for bonobo self-domestication. Organized behavioral, cognitive, hormonal, and morphological parallels between bonobos and domesticated species. Now actively contested by Mouginot et al. (2024) aggression data and Sánchez-Villagra & van Schaik (2019) critique of the domestication syndrome concept. **\[temperament/cognition, genetics/neurobiology\]**

**13\. Brooker, J. S., Kordon, L., & Clay, Z. (2025).** A direct comparison of consolation behavior in bonobos and chimpanzees. *Evolution and Human Behavior.* First systematic direct comparison of consolation: 40 bonobos and 50 chimpanzees showed similar consolation rates, with within-species variation exceeding between-species differences. Challenges the assumption that bonobos are more empathic consolers. Both sanctuary populations; single study. **\[temperament/cognition\]**

**14\. Garai, C., Weiss, A., Arnaud, G., & Furuichi, T. (2016).** Personality in wild bonobos (*Pan paniscus*) at Wamba. *American Journal of Primatology, 78*(11), 1178–1189. The only study of personality in wild bonobos. Sixteen individuals assessed with 31 HPQ items and 316 hours of behavioral observations. Factor structure markedly different from captive; no clear Dominance factor. Very small sample limits interpretability but provides crucial wild baseline. **\[personality structure, methodology\]**

**15\. Hopkins, W. D., Donaldson, Z. R., & Young, L. J. (2012).** A polymorphic indel containing the RS3 microsatellite in the 5′ flanking region of the vasopressin V1a receptor gene is associated with chimpanzee (*Pan troglodytes*) personality. *Genes, Brain and Behavior, 11*, 552–558. First report linking AVPR1A DupB polymorphism to chimpanzee personality dimensions (Conscientiousness, Extraversion) in 174 individuals. This genetic locus is now the best-replicated gene-personality association in nonhuman primates. Captive population (Yerkes). **\[genetics/neurobiology\]**

**16\. Surbeck, M., Cheng, L., Kreyer, M., Gort, G., Mundry, R., Hohmann, G., & Fruth, B. (2025).** Drivers of female power in bonobos. *Communications Biology*. Compiled 30 years of data from 6 wild communities across 3 DRC field sites (Kokolopori, LuiKotale, Wamba). Of 1,786 male–female conflicts, females won 1,099 (\~61.5%); in 85% of observed female coalitions, the coalition targeted a male. First demonstration that female solidarity can invert male-biased power structures. **\[aggression/coalitions\]**

**17\. Bryon, E., Roth, T. S., Torfs, J. R. R., Eens, M., van Leeuwen, E. J. C., & Staes, N. (2026).** Chimpanzees are not more aggressive than bonobos, but target sexes differently. *Science Advances, 12*(11). Compared 22 zoo-housed groups of chimpanzees and bonobos; found no overall species difference in aggression rate but a distributional difference — chimpanzee aggression is male-driven and directed at everyone, bonobo aggression comes from everyone but is directed mostly at males. Substantial between-group variation within each species (some bonobo groups were both the most and least aggressive observed). The captive-population complication to the wild-population Mouginot et al. (2024) finding. **\[aggression/coalitions\]**

## **Section 4: Gaps and contradictions**

### **Active contradictions**

**Social tolerance.** The founding claim (Hare et al., 2007\) that bonobos are more tolerant co-feeders than chimpanzees has been weakened by a partial replication failure (Nolte et al., 2023, same-facility test, trend-level only) and directly reversed by one study (Jaeggi et al., 2010, Antwerp). Within-group variation appears to rival or exceed between-species variation. Whether the species difference is real but small, context-dependent, or an artifact of facility differences remains unresolved.

**Aggression levels.** The traditional view of bonobos as less aggressive is contradicted by Mouginot et al. (2024) for overall male aggression rates, though qualitative differences (bonobo aggression is individualistic, non-coalitionary, and non-lethal) remain. Bryon et al. (2026) found no captive species difference in overall rates. The tension between these findings and the self-domestication framework is the most actively debated issue in the field.

**Consolation and empathy.** Prior to Brooker et al. (2025), bonobos were widely assumed to console more. The direct comparison found no species difference, but the study used sanctuary/orphanage populations. Whether rearing history (many subjects were bushmeat-trade orphans) affects consolation patterns remains unclear.

**Neuroticism stability.** Neuroticism emerges in zoo-housed but not laboratory-housed chimpanzees, suggesting either environmental sensitivity of the trait's expression or measurement artifacts linked to housing conditions. This internal inconsistency within chimpanzees complicates its interpretation as a species-level trait.

### **Major gaps**

**Wild personality data for bonobos** is represented by a single study of 16 individuals at one site. This is insufficient for factor analysis, sex-difference analysis, or comparison to captive structures. The wild bonobo factor structure bore little resemblance to the captive structure, but the sample was too small to determine whether this reflects genuine environment effects or noise.

**Personality and fitness in bonobos.** In chimpanzees, personality dimensions have been linked to rank, reproductive success, and longevity. No equivalent fitness analyses exist for bonobos.

**Personality and deception.** No study has systematically measured individual variation in deceptive behavior or tested whether personality predicts deception in either species. Given that *Pan* is the genus with the most documented tactical deception, this is a conspicuous gap.

**Direct captive-wild comparison.** No study has assessed personality in captive and wild individuals of the same species using identical methods and the same research team. Such a study would clarify how much captivity alters personality expression and structure.

**Longitudinal personality change.** Test-retest data exist for bonobos over \~5.6 years (Weiss et al., 2015\) and for a 25-year captive chimpanzee study (2025), but no comparable longitudinal data exist for wild populations of either species.

**Bonobo-specific personality items.** The HPQ was built from human FFM items and first validated on chimpanzees. Bottom-up behavioral repertoire analysis specific to bonobos has not been used to develop personality items, meaning bonobo-specific personality variation (e.g., related to socio-sexual behavior, female coalition dynamics, xenophilic tendencies) may be systematically unmeasured.

**Neuroanatomical replication.** The influential Rilling et al. (2012) brain comparison used 3–4 individuals per species. While partially corroborated by Issa et al. (2019, N=10 per species) and Stimpson et al. (2016, N=6 per species), the total number of bonobo brains that have been imaged or histologically examined remains extremely small. Power to detect anything other than large effects is negligible.

**Functional genomics.** AVPR1A associations are well-replicated but the functional consequences of the DupB deletion for receptor expression and vasopressin signaling remain uncharacterized in vivo. No GWAS has been attempted in either species, and the 5-HTTLPR — despite the striking serotonergic innervation differences — has not been directly linked to personality in *Pan*.

### **Most informative unstudied comparisons**

The single study that would most advance the field is a **multi-site, same-method personality assessment of wild bonobos** across Wamba, Kokolopori, and LuiKotale, with sample sizes sufficient for factor analysis (minimum \~60 individuals). This would determine whether the captive bonobo factor structure holds in natural conditions, enable fitness analyses, and provide a legitimate comparison to wild chimpanzee data from Gombe. The second most informative study would be a **same-facility experimental comparison** of multiple cognitive and temperamental domains (risk, patience, social tolerance, tool use) with adequate sample sizes (≥30 per species), controlling for the facility confounds that weaken existing work.
