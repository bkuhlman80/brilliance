# **Self-regulation is at least three things, not one**

The latent structure underlying self-regulation, impulsivity, conscientiousness, and related constructs is **not unitary**. Across more than 30 empirical studies using factor analysis, genomic SEM, twin modeling, lesion evidence, and pharmacological dissociations, the evidence converges on **3–5 separable dimensions** at the phenotypic level, with the single most robust finding being that **self-report and behavioral task measures occupy essentially independent factor spaces**. Within self-report measures, impulsivity-related scales reliably decompose into dimensions aligned with major personality factors—Neuroticism/urgency, Conscientiousness/constraint, and Extraversion/sensation seeking. Genetically, executive function and self-reported impulsivity share only **rg ≈ .13** of their variance, while a broad externalizing/disinhibition factor captures shared genetic liability across substance use, antisocial behavior, and personality disinhibition. The apparent coherence of "self-regulation" as a single construct is substantially inflated by shared method variance within self-report instruments.

---

## **Studies supporting a single latent factor**

Several lines of evidence support extracting one overarching factor, but each comes with important qualifications about scope.

**Karlsson Linnér et al. (2021)** conducted the largest genomic SEM analysis to date (effective **N \= 1,492,085**), fitting factor models to genetic covariance among seven externalizing-adjacent phenotypes: ADHD, problematic alcohol use, cannabis use, age at first sex (reversed), number of sexual partners, risk tolerance, and smoking initiation. A **single EXT factor** provided adequate fit (CFI \= 0.957). All seven phenotypes loaded on EXT, and leave-one-out analyses showed near-perfect correlations (rg ≈ .984–.999) between full and reduced models. The factor showed genetic correlations with **91 external phenotypes** including negative correlations with conscientiousness and agreeableness. This study identified **579 genome-wide significant loci** enriched for brain-expressed genes. However, the phenotype set was restricted to externalizing behaviors and did not include cognitive control tasks, emotion regulation, or conscientiousness measured directly.

**Krueger, Hicks, Patrick, Carlson, Iacono & McGue (2002)** modeled the externalizing spectrum in **N \= 1,048** 17-year-old twins. A single externalizing factor subsumed substance dependence (alcohol, nicotine, illicit drugs), antisocial behavior, and disinhibitory personality traits from the MPQ Constraint dimension. This factor was **\~80% heritable**. Specific genetic and environmental influences accounted for distinctions among individual phenotypes within the spectrum, but the dominant source of covariance was the shared genetic factor.

**Takahashi et al. (2021)** used multivariate twin modeling in **\~2,000 Japanese adolescent twin pairs** to examine conscientiousness, effortful control, self-control, and grit. These four constructs were highly intercorrelated (r \= .74–.79) with heritabilities of **62–64%**. A common genetic factor explained **over 84%** of the genetic variance, strongly supporting unity within the conscientiousness-self-control domain. This is important: constructs that are conceptually close (conscientiousness, self-control, grit, effortful control) appear to be a single genetic entity.

**Huang, Luan, Wu et al. (2024, PNAS)** administered **48 measures** (10 self-report scales and 10 behavioral tasks) to **N \= 1,676** participants in a preregistered bifactor analysis. A general factor "I" emerged, loaded primarily by self-report measures (BIS-11, UPPS-P, Dickman, Eysenck I7), with **six specific factors** capturing residual variance from individual behavioral tasks. Factor I was temporally stable and predicted impulsivity-related behaviors better than any single scale. However, behavioral tasks loaded on their own specific factors, not on the general factor—so the "unity" is confined to the self-report method space. The authors developed the Adjustable Impulsivity Scale (AIMS) to measure factor I.

**The p-factor: Caspi et al. (2014)** analyzed 11 psychiatric disorders in the Dunedin birth cohort (**N ≈ 1,037**) across ages 18–38. A bifactor model with one general psychopathology factor (p) plus three specific factors (internalizing, externalizing, thought disorder) fit best. High p was associated with life impairment, familiality, compromised developmental history, and worse early-life brain function. Caspi et al. explicitly stated that high-p individuals experience "difficulties in regulation/control when dealing with others, the environment, and the self." Subsequent work by **Romer et al. (2021, N \= 1,330)** confirmed that higher p was associated with less successful self-regulation for both promotion and prevention goals, even after controlling for Big Five personality.

**The General Factor of Personality** was meta-analytically supported by **van der Linden, te Nijenhuis & Bakker (2010)** across **K \= 212 studies, N \= 141,100**, explaining \~45% of Big Five variance. However, **DeYoung (2006)** demonstrated that in multi-informant data (N \= 490, 4 raters per target), the two metatraits Stability and Plasticity were **uncorrelated** once evaluative bias was removed, and **de Vries (2011)** found that GFPs from different instruments were negatively correlated. The GFP is now widely regarded as a **method artifact** rather than a substantive factor.

## **Studies supporting two latent factors**

The two-factor level reveals a fundamental split between self-report personality traits and behavioral task performance—and, within personality, between stability-related and plasticity-related variance.

**DeYoung (2006)** confirmed two higher-order metatraits using CFA on multi-informant personality data (**N \= 490**, 4 raters per target). **Stability** (Conscientiousness \+ Agreeableness \+ Emotional Stability) and **Plasticity** (Extraversion \+ Openness/Intellect) emerged as uncorrelated latent variables when modeled from shared informant variance, eliminating rater bias. DeYoung linked Stability to serotonergic function and self-regulatory capacity, and Plasticity to dopaminergic exploration. This was elaborated in **Cybernetic Big Five Theory (DeYoung, 2015\)**, which positions the Stability metatrait as reflecting threat-sensitive control mechanisms that protect goal pursuit from disruption by impulses and negative emotion.

**DeYoung, Peterson, Séguin & Tremblay (2008)** tested the metatrait model against externalizing behavior in adolescent males from the Montreal Longitudinal Study. Using SEM, they found externalizing behavior was predicted by **low Stability**, high Plasticity, and low cognitive ability. After controlling for cognitive ability, Openness showed a positive association with externalizing—a suppression effect revealing the independent contributions of the two metatrait dimensions.

**Friedman, Hatoum, Gustavson et al. (2020)** produced the most critical twin-study evidence for a two-factor split. In two adult twin samples (**combined N ≈ 1,510**), they modeled three EF latent variables (Common EF, Updating-Specific, Shifting-Specific) alongside five UPPS-P dimensions. Phenotypic correlations between Common EF and impulsivity dimensions ranged from **r \= −.20 to .11**. Genetic correlations were similarly modest (**rA \= −.44 to .04**). Critically, Common EF and impulsivity dimensions **independently predicted externalizing psychopathology**, while multiple impulsivity dimensions (but not Common EF) predicted **internalizing psychopathology**. This is a genetic-level double dissociation.

**Gustavson, Morrison, Mallard et al. (2024)** extended this using genomic SEM with GWAS data from UK Biobank and 23andMe (EF **N \> 100,000**; impulsivity **N up to 133,517**). The genetic correlation between low Common EF and high Common Impulsivity was only **rg \= .13**. Controlling for impulsivity, low EF uniquely predicted genetic variance in internalizing (βg \= .15), externalizing (βg \= .13), thought disorders (βg \= .38), and compulsive disorders (βg \= .22). EF and impulsivity are **molecularly distinct dimensions of control**.

**Markon, Krueger & Watson (2005)** demonstrated a hierarchical personality structure integrating normal and abnormal personality across multiple inventories (NEO-PI-R, MPQ, DAPP, SNAP, MMPI PSY-5). At the **two-factor level**, the structure split into Internalizing/Negative Emotionality versus Externalizing/Positive Emotionality–Disinhibition. At the three-factor level, Disinhibition separated from Positive Emotionality, and at the five-factor level, the structure mapped onto the Big Five.

## **Studies supporting three or more latent factors**

The three-factor level is where most cross-battery phenotypic studies converge, with additional factors appearing as measurement scope broadens.

**Sharma, Markon & Clark (2014)** conducted meta-analytic factor analyses of impulsivity measures, analyzing self-report and behavioral tasks separately. Self-report measures yielded **three factors** aligned with the Big Three personality dimensions: (1) **Neuroticism/Negative Emotionality** (urgency-type scales), (2) **Disinhibition vs. Constraint/Conscientiousness** (with subfactors for disinhibition and willpower), and (3) **Extraversion/Positive Emotionality/Sensation Seeking**. Behavioral tasks yielded **four separate factors**: Inattention, Inhibition, Impulsive Decision-Making, and Shifting. Cross-method correlations were consistently **low to very low**, and both modalities independently predicted real-world impulsive behaviors.

**Creswell, Wright, Flory, Skrzynski & Manuck (2019)** replicated the Sharma three-factor model in a single community sample (**N \= 1,295**, ages 30–54) using 54 self-report scales from BIS-11, BIS/BAS, MPQ, NEO-PI-R, UPPS-P, SNAP, and STAI, plus four behavioral tasks (Stroop, IGT, delay discounting, WCST). EFA on self-report scales yielded three factors: **Disinhibition vs. Constraint/Conscientiousness**, **Neuroticism/Negative Emotionality**, and **Extraversion/Positive Emotionality**. Relations between self-report factors and behavioral tasks were **small or nonexistent**. Disinhibition/Constraint predicted all externalizing outcomes with medium-to-large effects.

**MacKillop, Weafer, Gray et al. (2016)** tested the latent structure of impulsivity across three measurement traditions in **N \= 1,252** young adults. CFA confirmed **three uncorrelated factors**: (1) **Impulsive Choice** (delay discounting task, Monetary Choice Questionnaire), (2) **Impulsive Action** (CPT, Go/No-Go, Stop Signal), and (3) **Impulsive Personality Traits** (BIS-11, UPPS-P). These three factors showed **near-zero inter-factor correlations**. Sensation seeking was excluded from the final model because it did not fit with impulsive personality traits. Different factors were differentially associated with substance use outcomes.

**Billen, Garofalo & Bogaerts (2022)** used bass-ackwards hierarchical EFA on the UPPS-P, DERS-16, Brief Self-Control Scale, and PID-5 scales in general population and forensic samples. Three components emerged: (1) **Emotion Regulation** (DERS scales, UPPS-P negative urgency), (2) **Risk Behavior** (sensation seeking, positive urgency), and (3) **Cognitive Regulation** (lack of premeditation, lack of perseverance, self-control, distractibility). Notably, the Tangney self-control scale loaded with cognitive regulation, not emotion regulation. Negative urgency bridged the emotion regulation and impulsivity domains.

**Miller & Gizer (2024)** tested dual-systems models using genomic SEM with GWAS data (sensation seeking effective **N \= 710,971**). Three correlated genetic factors emerged: (1) **Sensation Seeking** (UPPS-P Sensation Seeking, risk-taking, adventurousness), (2) **Urgency** (Negative/Positive Urgency, Harm Avoidance), and (3) **(Lack of) Self-Control** (Lack of Premeditation, BIS total, Novelty Seeking). Each factor showed separable but overlapping associations with neuroimaging phenotypes. This three-factor genetic structure maps onto dual-systems theory, with sensation seeking as bottom-up reward-based, urgency as bottom-up emotion-based, and self-control as top-down regulatory.

**Whiteside & Lynam (2001)** produced the foundational four-factor cross-battery EFA (**N \= 437**) that created the UPPS model. Across NEO-PI-R impulsivity-related facets, BIS-11, Eysenck I7, Dickman Impulsivity, EASI-III, and the Sensation Seeking Scale, four factors emerged: **(Lack of) Premeditation** (NEO Deliberation, BIS-11 non-planning), **Urgency** (NEO Impulsiveness, BIS-11 motor), **Sensation Seeking** (NEO Excitement Seeking, SSS scales), and **(Lack of) Perseverance** (NEO Self-Discipline, EASI persistence). Each factor mapped to a different Big Five facet: Premeditation and Perseverance to Conscientiousness, Urgency to Neuroticism, Sensation Seeking to Extraversion.

**Harden et al. (2017)** examined 15 self-report and task-based measures in **810 adolescent twins** from the Texas Twin Project, using ESEM plus behavioral genetic modeling. Four factors emerged: **Premeditation**, **Fearlessness** (risk-taking, thrill-seeking), **Cognitive Dyscontrol** (task-based EF), and **Reward Seeking** (delay discounting, reward sensitivity). Genetic influences on cognitive dyscontrol overlapped near-perfectly with genetic influences on IQ (**rA \= −.91**), while self-report dimensions had distinct genetic architectures.

**Gustavson et al. (2020)** used genomic SEM on UPPS-P, BIS-11, and delay discounting GWAS data (impulsivity **N up to 22,861**; internalizing traits **N up to \~400,000**). The best-fitting model had **five genetic factors**: Urgency (Negative \+ Positive), Lack of Premeditation \+ BIS, Lack of Perseverance, Sensation Seeking, with **delay discounting genetically distinct** from all five self-report factors. Urgency factors were most strongly genetically correlated with an internalizing psychopathology factor. BIS-11 subscales were genetically indistinguishable (rg ≈ 1.0).

## **The task-survey divide is the most replicated structural finding**

The most consistently replicated finding across this literature is that self-report and behavioral task measures of ostensibly the same constructs form **separate factor spaces** with minimal convergent validity. This has profound implications for interpreting any "unitary" factor derived from a single measurement modality.

**Toplak, West & Stanovich (2013)** systematically reviewed 20 studies comparing performance-based EF tasks with behavioral ratings (primarily the BRIEF). The median correlation was **r \= .19**, and only 19% of 182 correlations reached significance. They concluded these measurement modalities capture different levels of cognition: tasks capture processing efficiency, while ratings capture goal-pursuit success.

**Eisenberg, Bissett, Enkavi et al. (2019, Nature Communications)** administered **37 behavioral tasks** (129 DVs) and **22 self-report surveys** (64 DVs) to **N \= 522** participants. The resulting factor structure **bifurcated completely by measurement method**: 5 task factors and 12 survey factors emerged with essentially no cross-method loading. Surveys modestly predicted real-world outcomes (mean R² ≈ .10); tasks largely did not. The authors concluded that "self-regulation lacks coherence as a construct."

**Enkavi et al. (2019, PNAS)** documented the psychometric basis for this divide. Self-report DVs showed high test-retest reliability (median ICC ≈ .82), while behavioral task DVs showed substantially lower reliability (median ICC ≈ .35). This "reliability paradox" partly explains low cross-method correlations, but **Friedman & Gustavson (2022)** demonstrated the separability persists even at the latent variable and genetic levels, ruling out mere measurement noise as the explanation.

**Cyders & Coskunpinar (2011)** meta-analyzed **27 studies** comparing self-report and behavioral impulsivity measures, finding a non-significant overall correlation of **r \= .097**. **Dang, King & Inzlicht (2020)** extended this observation across domains (self-control, emotional intelligence, empathy, risk preference), finding average cross-method correlations of **r \= 0–.20** in every case.

**Duckworth, Tsukayama et al. (2022)** demonstrated an additional method-specific confound in **N \= 229,685** adolescents: self-report self-regulation measures showed **reference bias** (students in high-performing schools rated themselves lower), while a behavioral academic diligence task did not. At the between-school level, higher self-reported grit paradoxically predicted lower graduation rates—an effect absent from the behavioral measure.

## **Lesion, pharmacological, and developmental dissociations confirm separability**

Converging evidence from neuroscience and developmental psychology establishes that the separable factors are not merely psychometric artifacts but reflect genuinely dissociable neural and developmental systems.

**Dias, Robbins & Roberts (1996)** demonstrated the canonical double dissociation in marmoset monkeys: **orbitofrontal cortex lesions** impaired reversal learning (affective/motivational flexibility) while sparing extradimensional set shifting, whereas **lateral prefrontal cortex lesions** impaired set shifting while sparing reversal learning. This dissociates emotional/motivational control (OFC) from cognitive flexibility (lateral PFC). **Bissonette et al. (2008)** replicated this double dissociation in mice.

**Eslinger et al. (2011)** found the same dissociation in **N \= 157** patients with neurodegenerative disease: orbitofrontal atrophy specifically predicted socioemotional disinhibition (not EF deficits), while middle frontal gyrus atrophy specifically predicted EF deficits (not disinhibition). This human clinical double dissociation confirms two separable neural systems for cognitive control and emotional regulation.

**Skandali et al. (2018)** demonstrated a pharmacological triple dissociation in **N \= 66** healthy volunteers: acute SSRI (escitalopram) **impaired** cognitive flexibility (reversal learning, set shifting) but **improved** stop-signal inhibition, while leaving emotional processing **unaffected**. This dissociates at least three self-regulation subfunctions with a single neurotransmitter manipulation. **Pine, Shiner, Seymour & Dolan (2010)** showed that L-DOPA selectively increased temporal discounting without affecting reward magnitude sensitivity, dissociating delay-based impulsivity from reward valuation.

**Steinberg, Albert, Cauffman et al. (2008)** provided a developmental dissociation in **N \= 935** individuals aged 10–30: sensation seeking followed a curvilinear trajectory (increasing to \~15, then declining), while impulsivity showed monotonic linear decline from age 10\. Sensation seeking was linked to pubertal maturation; impulsivity was not. **Harden & Tucker-Drob (2011)** replicated these diverging trajectories in **N \= 7,640** youth from the National Longitudinal Study of Youth, confirming they are "distinct phenomena subserved by different brain systems."

**Muhammed et al. (2020)** showed in Parkinson's patients that those with impulse control disorders exhibited **heightened reward sensitivity** (pupillary response to reward cues) but no differences in cognitive impairment, depression, or anhedonia compared to PD patients without ICD, demonstrating reward sensitivity as dissociable from both cognitive control and emotional function.

## **The externalizing spectrum absorbs much of the self-regulation space at the psychopathology level**

**Krueger, Markon, Patrick, Benning & Kramer (2007)** developed the Externalizing Spectrum Inventory (**N \= 1,787**) with 23 facet scales covering aggression, impulsivity, irresponsibility, rebelliousness, sensation seeking, and substance use. Factor analysis yielded three higher-order factors: **General Disinhibition** (the core self-regulation factor), **Callous-Aggression**, and **Substance Abuse**. Normal-range personality traits (impulsivity, sensation seeking, low constraint) loaded directly on the general factor alongside clinical pathology, demonstrating continuity from personality to psychopathology.

The externalizing spectrum intersects with the Stability metatrait from the personality side. **DeYoung, Quilty & Peterson (2007)** showed that each Big Five domain decomposes into two aspects: Conscientiousness into **Industriousness** and **Orderliness**, Neuroticism into **Volatility** and **Withdrawal**, Extraversion into **Enthusiasm** and **Assertiveness**, Agreeableness into **Compassion** and **Politeness**, and Openness/Intellect into **Openness** and **Intellect**. Phillips et al. (2024) found that the Conscientiousness aspect **Deliberation** showed the largest relations with UPPS-P urgency (r \= −.44 to −.58), demonstrating that aspect-level analysis reveals tighter cross-construct connections than domain-level analysis.

## **How many factors? A synthesis organized by scope**

The number of factors required depends on which constructs and measurement modalities are included:

**Within self-report personality/impulsivity only**, the answer is **3 factors** aligned with Big Three personality dimensions. Sharma et al. (2014), Creswell et al. (2019), and multiple replication studies converge on Neuroticism/Urgency, Disinhibition/Conscientiousness, and Extraversion/Sensation Seeking. A bifactor general factor can be extracted (Huang et al., 2024\) but captures mainly shared self-report method variance.

**When crossing self-report with behavioral tasks**, at minimum **2 additional factors** are required (impulsive action/inhibition and impulsive choice/delay discounting), as shown by MacKillop et al. (2016) and Eisenberg et al. (2019). The three measurement traditions (personality trait, behavioral choice, motor inhibition) are essentially **uncorrelated**.

**When including emotion regulation**, a fourth self-report dimension emerges (Billen et al., 2022), though urgency partially bridges impulsivity and emotion regulation domains.

**Genetically**, the picture is hierarchical. At the broadest level, a single externalizing factor captures shared genetic variance across behavioral disinhibition phenotypes (Karlsson Linnér et al., 2021). But executive function is genetically separable from self-reported impulsivity (rg \= .13; Gustavson et al., 2024), delay discounting is genetically distinct from self-report impulsivity (Gustavson et al., 2020), and within impulsivity, sensation seeking is genetically separable from urgency and premeditation (Miller & Gizer, 2024). Conscientiousness-related self-report constructs form their own genetic cluster (Takahashi et al., 2021). The genetic evidence thus supports **at least 4 partially overlapping factors**: (a) externalizing/disinhibition, (b) cognitive control/EF, (c) sensation seeking, and (d) delay discounting, with further separation of urgency from premeditation possible.

**At the higher-order personality level**, the Stability metatrait (DeYoung, 2006\) absorbs Conscientiousness, Agreeableness, and Emotional Stability, and predicts externalizing behavior (DeYoung et al., 2008). But this metatrait does not capture task-based EF or delay discounting, which are dissociable by independent converging evidence.

## **Conclusion: What is genuinely one thing, and what is not**

The field-mapping evidence supports the following structural conclusions. **First**, there is no single "self-regulation" factor. The construct fragments along at least three fault lines: measurement method (self-report vs. task), content domain (cognitive control vs. emotional regulation vs. reward processing), and developmental/neural substrate (prefrontal cognitive systems vs. limbic/striatal motivational systems). **Second**, the strongest unity exists within the self-report personality space, where most impulsivity and self-control questionnaires reduce to dimensions of the Big Three (Neuroticism, Conscientiousness, Extraversion)—suggesting that self-report "impulsivity" is largely personality measured with different instruments. **Third**, delay discounting is reliably separate from everything else—weakly correlated with personality, uncorrelated with cognitive tasks, and genetically distinct from self-report impulsivity. **Fourth**, sensation seeking is the most consistently separable dimension, dissociable by factor analysis, genetics, developmental trajectory, and pharmacology. **Fifth**, the apparent unity of self-regulation in much of the literature is substantially an artifact of studying self-report instruments in isolation; when behavioral tasks enter the analysis, coherence collapses. The minimum defensible model for the full construct space, incorporating both measurement modalities and the major content domains, requires **5 or more factors**: cognitive control/EF, self-report disinhibition/low conscientiousness, emotional urgency/dysregulation, sensation seeking/reward sensitivity, and delay discounting—with an additional method factor distinguishing self-report from task-based measurement.
