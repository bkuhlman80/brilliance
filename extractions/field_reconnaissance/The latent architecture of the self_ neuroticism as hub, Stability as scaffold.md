# **The latent architecture of the self: neuroticism as hub, Stability as scaffold**

**Across dozens of cross-battery factor analyses, meta-analytic correlation matrices, twin studies, and network analyses, the self-evaluative construct space is dominated by a single latent dimension anchored by neuroticism.** Self-esteem, self-concept clarity, core self-evaluations, self-compassion, ego resilience, and dispositional mindfulness all converge on the Stability metatrait (low Neuroticism \+ high Agreeableness \+ high Conscientiousness), suggesting that 2–3 latent factors—not one per construct—account for most cross-construct covariance. Self-awareness constructs (mindfulness, reflection, rumination, alexithymia, emotional granularity) form a partially separable metacognitive cluster that does not collapse into a single factor. The genetic evidence reinforces this structure: neuroticism captures virtually all the genetic variance in self-esteem relevant to psychopathology, while self-efficacy and mindfulness retain partially independent genetic architecture. Below, the evidence is organized by study type, with specific correlations, factor loadings, and dissociations documented.

---

## **(a) Studies and meta-analyses reporting factor structures**

### **Core Self-Evaluations: one factor survives the acquiescence test**

Judge, Erez, Bono, and Thoresen (2002) established the foundational case across four studies and a meta-analysis: self-esteem, generalized self-efficacy, internal locus of control, and emotional stability (low neuroticism) load on a **single higher-order factor** with poor discriminant validity among components. Each component contributed little incremental variance beyond the Core Self-Evaluations (CSE) composite. The 12-item CSES developed by Judge et al. (2003) correlated with self-esteem at corrected r \= **.87**, with self-efficacy at **.82**, and with neuroticism at **−.76**.

The definitive structural test came from Gnambs and Schroeders (2024; **N \= 31,843** across 53 samples), a meta-analytic CFA that resolved a longstanding debate. Prior studies frequently found two-factor CSES solutions separating positively and negatively worded items. Once acquiescent responding was modeled via a random intercept, all 12 items loaded on a **single common factor** (ω \= .87) with partial metric invariance across English, German, and Spanish versions. The two-factor findings were measurement artifacts, not substantive dimensions. A 2025 bifactor-ESEM study of CSE components in German secondary students (**N \= 1,573**) confirmed that a general self-evaluation factor coexisted with meaningful specific factors for neuroticism, self-esteem, and self-efficacy—supporting the view that CSE is real but does not fully absorb its components.

### **Self-concept clarity occupies a separable but overlapping niche**

Campbell, Trapnell, Heine, Katz, Lavallee, and Lehman (1996) reported the foundational correlations: SCC with self-esteem r \= **.61**, SCC with neuroticism r \= **−.64**, with additional independent associations with conscientiousness, agreeableness, and chronic self-analysis. In simultaneous regression, SCC retained independent paths from multiple personality variables, suggesting it is not simply the inverse of neuroticism.

Weber, Hopwood, Nissen, and Bleidorn (2023; **N \= 460 \+ 412**, 8 longitudinal waves) provided the strongest test of separability. CFA confirmed SCC and self-esteem items loaded on **distinct latent factors**. Cross-lagged models showed SCC states predicted subsequent self-esteem states but not vice versa. Each construct predicted life outcomes (well-being, interpersonal functioning) **incrementally beyond the other**. The authors characterized them as "sibling constructs."

DeMarree and Bobrowski (2017; N \= 347\) subjected six different SCC operationalizations to ESEM, finding **three factors**: the SCCS scale, certainty/extremity, and latitudes of self-description. Critically, many documented SCC correlates were "eliminated or reduced" when self-esteem was controlled—but perseverative thinking, narrative coherence, and social functioning retained unique SCC variance. Nissen et al. (2024; **N \= 859**) confirmed SCC contributes incrementally beyond all Big Five traits for social functioning.

**A striking gap**: no published study has jointly administered the SCCS and the CSES in the same sample. Given that both are saturated with self-esteem and neuroticism variance (SCC–SE r ≈ .61; CSES–SE r ≈ .87), their joint factor structure is an open empirical question. Estimated SCC–CSES correlation based on shared self-esteem/neuroticism variance would likely fall in the **.55–.70** range.

### **The self-awareness cluster separates into 2–4 factors, not one**

DaSilveira, DeSouza, and Gomes (2015; **N \= 602**) performed the most comprehensive cross-battery factor analysis of self-awareness measures, administering the SCS-R, Rumination-Reflection Questionnaire, Self-Reflection and Insight Scale, Self-Absorption Scale, and Philadelphia Mindfulness Scale. Principal axis factoring at the subscale level yielded **three factors** (63.3% variance): a maladaptive factor (social anxiety, rumination, self-absorption, low insight, low acceptance), an adaptive self-reflection factor (private self-consciousness, reflection, self-reflection), and a public factor. Mindful awareness did not load on any of these three factors—it emerged as a **distinct fourth factor** in item-level analysis, absorbing all awareness items from across scales. The correlation between the Reflection and Awareness factors was **−.38**, indicating these are genuinely different processes.

Solem, Thunes, Hjemdal, Hagen, and Wells (2015; N \= 224\) jointly factor-analyzed the FFMQ and MCQ-30, finding **two latent factors**: a metacognition factor (all MCQ subscales plus FFMQ acting with awareness and nonjudging) and a mindfulness factor (FFMQ observing, nonreacting, describing). Mindfulness and metacognition did not collapse into a single construct.

Trapnell and Campbell (1999; N up to **1,720**) provided the canonical dissociation within self-focused attention. Rumination and reflection were virtually **uncorrelated** (r ≈ .11) despite both being forms of self-attention. Rumination loaded on neuroticism (r ≈ .50); reflection loaded on openness to experience (r ≈ .50). This double dissociation via personality correlates established that the Private Self-Consciousness scale confounds two motivationally distinct dispositions.

### **Self-compassion factor structure remains genuinely contested**

Neff, Tóth-Király, and Colosimo (2019; **N \= 11,685** across 20 samples) compared five CFA/ESEM models and found a general self-compassion factor in bifactor ESEM explaining **95% of reliable item variance**, supporting total score use. However, Brenner et al. (2017; N \= 1,115) found a two-general-factor bifactor model (self-compassion \+ self-coldness) fit best. Tóth-Király et al. (2018; **N \= 1,725** adolescents) found only a two-factor model showed acceptable fit. The analytic method choice (CFA vs. ESEM) critically determines conclusions. Marsh et al. (2023) using Bayesian SEM found the correlation between compassionate and uncompassionate general factors was ≈ **.60**, significantly less than unity—endorsing separate subscores.

The Big Five collectively explain **34%** of self-esteem variance, with emotional stability as the strongest predictor (Robins et al. 2001; N \= 326,641). Giluk (2009) and Banfi and Randall (2022) confirmed mindfulness shows strongest negative correlation with neuroticism and positive correlations with conscientiousness and agreeableness—the same Stability triad.

### **Critical pattern across meta-analytic matrices**

Every self-evaluative construct examined meta-analytically shows its strongest personality correlate is **neuroticism** (negative) or **emotional stability** (positive). The second-tier correlates are conscientiousness and agreeableness. This consistent pattern across independent literatures (CSE, SCC, self-compassion, mindfulness, alexithymia, ego resilience) is the strongest evidence that these constructs share a latent dimension aligned with Stability.

## **(c) Discriminant validity and dissociation evidence**

### **Self-concept clarity versus self-esteem: clear but negative self-views exist**

Weber et al. (2023) demonstrated that SCC and self-esteem have different developmental trajectories, different within-person dynamics (SCC predicts SE change but not vice versa), and different outcome profiles. DeMarree and Bobrowski (2017) showed SCC uniquely predicts narrative coherence of self-defining memories (β \= .26) controlling for self-esteem, while self-esteem does not predict coherence. However, SCC's association with depression drops from β \= −.56 to **−.15** after controlling for self-esteem—suggesting that for affective outcomes, much of SCC's predictive power is borrowed from shared self-esteem variance.

### **Rumination versus reflection: the cleanest dissociation in the space**

Trapnell and Campbell's (1999) double dissociation is remarkably clean: rumination–reflection r ≈ **.11**, rumination–neuroticism r ≈ .50, reflection–openness r ≈ .50. These two constructs share the form of self-focused attention but differ completely in motivational substrate and personality correlates. This finding has been replicated consistently across samples.

### **Humility versus low self-esteem: structurally distinct**

HEXACO Honesty-Humility is a structurally distinct sixth personality factor that emerges across languages (Ashton, Lee, & de Vries, 2014). Blötner (2025; **N \= 3,976**) confirmed H is "correlated with yet distinguishable from" both Big Five and HEXACO agreeableness. Critically, humility shows weak positive or null correlations with self-esteem (r ≈ .10–.20; Kruse et al. 2017; Aghababaei et al. 2016 found non-significant correlations in 2 of 3 samples). Intellectual humility likewise correlates positively, not negatively, with self-esteem (Leary et al. 2017; Bak & Kutnik 2021). **Humility is not low self-esteem**; it involves accurate self-assessment and reduced self-focus, orthogonal to the evaluative dimension.

### **Alexithymia versus low emotional granularity: different experiential profiles**

Ottenstein (2020; N \= 96\) used linguistic analysis of autobiographical narratives to demonstrate that alexithymia is associated with **negative bias and hyper-focus on the self**, while low emotion differentiation is associated with **poor emotion-situation correspondence**. Hoemann's (2022) dissertation from the Barrett lab showed network measures of emotional granularity predicted anxiety and depression controlling for TAS-20 alexithymia. The constructs share referent (difficulty with emotions) but differ in mechanism: alexithymia reflects a deficit in emotional awareness/labeling, while low granularity reflects a deficit in conceptual differentiation of emotional states.

### **Self-compassion versus self-esteem \+ neuroticism: partial redundancy**

The Pfattheicher et al. (2017) finding that negative SCS components correlate at latent r ≥ .85 with neuroticism depression/anxiety facets represents the strongest redundancy evidence—suggesting self-judgment, isolation, and over-identification are "old wine in new bottles." Neff et al. (2018) responded with bifactor ESEM showing the general self-compassion factor retains incremental validity for life satisfaction, emotion regulation, and well-being beyond neuroticism. The positive SCS components (self-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness) appear to carry unique variance; the negative components are largely neuroticism repackaged. Self-compassion's meta-analytic r \= .65 with self-esteem means approximately **42% shared variance**—substantial but not total redundancy. Neff (2003) showed self-compassion, unlike self-esteem, is uncorrelated with narcissism (r ≈ 0 with NPI), a clean discriminating feature.

### **Social desirability: more substance than artifact**

de Vries, Zettler, and Hilbig (2014) provided the strongest evidence that social desirability scales "measure personality traits instead" of pure response sets. Paulhus and John (1998) mapped the two SD factors onto personality metatraits: **self-deceptive enhancement** (egoistic bias) aligns with Plasticity (Extraversion, Openness), while **impression management** (moralistic bias) aligns with Stability (Agreeableness, Conscientiousness). Erdle and Rushton (2011; **N \= 628,640**) found controlling for social desirability reduced GFP loadings only marginally (.59 → .56), confirming the GFP is not an SD artifact. Social desirability functions primarily as **substantive self-presentation motivation** that maps onto the same higher-order personality structure as other self-evaluative traits.

## **(d) Genetic architecture evidence**

### **Neuroticism is the genetic core of self-evaluation**

Twin studies converge on self-esteem heritability of **29–52%** (Kendler et al. 1998: 29–32%; Roy, Neale & Kendler 1995: 52% with measurement error correction; Raevuori et al. 2007: up to 60% in mid-adolescence). This is consistently lower than neuroticism heritability (**40–60%**), suggesting self-esteem is a more environmentally malleable surface manifestation of deeper personality dispositions.

The critical finding is Kendler, Myers, and Prescott's (1999) trivariate twin model of self-esteem, neuroticism, and major depression in 2,163 women. When neuroticism was modeled as upstream, the genetic correlation between self-esteem and depression **disappeared entirely**. When self-esteem was upstream, a substantial genetic correlation remained between neuroticism and depression. This asymmetry demonstrates that **neuroticism subsumes virtually all genetic variance in self-esteem relevant to psychopathology**. Van den Berg et al. (2025; 1,288 Finnish twins) confirmed phenotypic self-esteem–neuroticism correlations of r \= **−.70 to −.72**, with additive genetic factors explaining 22–66% of cross-trait covariation.

### **Self-efficacy is surprisingly heritable; mindfulness and alexithymia moderately so**

Waaktaar and Torgersen (2013; 1,394 Norwegian adolescent twin pairs) found generalized self-efficacy was **75% heritable** using multi-rater psychometric common factors—one of the highest heritability estimates for any self-evaluative construct. This is substantially higher than self-esteem heritability, suggesting self-efficacy may have partially distinct genetic architecture. Locus of control heritability is more modest (\~**30%**; Mosing et al. 2012; N \= 7,902). Self-control heritability meta-analyzed at **60%** (Willems et al. 2019; 31 studies, \>30,000 twins).

Trait mindfulness is **32% heritable** (Waszczuk et al. 2015; 2,100+ TEDS twins), with roughly two-thirds of its genetic variance **independent of depression and anxiety sensitivity**. Alexithymia is **30–36% heritable** (Jørgensen et al. 2007; **8,785 Danish twin pairs**—the largest alexithymia twin study), with a notable shared environment contribution of 12–20%, unlike most personality traits.

### **Major gaps in the genetic literature**

No published GWAS examines self-esteem in healthy populations. No genomic SEM analysis has modeled the self-evaluative trait constellation (self-esteem, self-efficacy, locus of control, SCC) as a set. Self-concept clarity genetics are **completely unstudied**. Self-compassion has no twin study. These gaps mean the question of whether CSE components share genetic architecture or are genetically separable remains unanswered by molecular methods. Grotzinger et al.'s (2019) genomic SEM methodology exists and has been applied to psychiatric traits and neuroticism factor structure, but no one has applied it to self-evaluative constructs.

### **The Stability metatrait has neurobiological grounding**

Manuck et al. (2019; N \= 441\) demonstrated that the Stability metatrait was associated with **central serotonergic responsivity** measured via citalopram infusion challenge, supporting DeYoung's hypothesis that Stability reflects serotonin-mediated behavioral regulation. Since self-evaluative traits load heavily on Stability components, this provides a plausible neurobiological mechanism for their shared architecture.

## **(e) Centrality and network analysis findings**

### **Neuroticism dominates broad personality networks**

Letina et al. (2017/2019; **N \> 1,000,000** from the MyPersonality Project) constructed a Gaussian graphical model of 26 psychological attributes and found **Emotional Stability was the most central trait** across degree, closeness, and betweenness centrality indices. The network displayed small-world properties. Changes to emotional stability would propagate most widely through the system—consistent with its role as the genetic hub identified in twin studies.

### **Alexithymia is most central in interoceptive awareness networks**

Zhang et al. (2021; **N \= 1,360**) found that alexithymia exhibited the **highest centrality and expected influence** in a network connecting interoceptive sensibility, empathy, self-awareness, and autistic traits. A separate network analysis of interoception, alexithymia, and Big Five in 504 adults found **Difficulty Identifying Feelings and Neuroticism** had the highest strength centrality, serving as bridges between interoception and personality.

### **Self-compassion and mindfulness are central in well-being networks**

Chen et al. (2025; N \= 789\) found **mindfulness held the highest node strength centrality** in a self-compassion/empathy cross-lagged network. In healthcare professionals' resilience networks, self-compassion had the **highest centrality strength (1.11)** with strongest connections to optimism and self-care. An ADHD/self-compassion/Big Five network found **neuroticism was the highest bridge symptom** linking clinical and personality clusters, with self-judgment and over-identification (negative self-compassion) as key bridges.

### **Self-esteem bridges well-being and psychopathology in clinical networks**

In a 62-symptom network of 4,616 psychotherapy patients, low self-esteem and inferiority were among the **four most central symptoms** spanning anxiety, somatoform, and personality disorders. In adolescent networks (N \= 2,441), self-esteem bridged personal well-being and social competence clusters.

### **The Stability metatrait absorbs self-evaluative constructs at the highest level**

DeYoung, Peterson, and Higgins (2002) established Stability (low N \+ high A \+ high C) and Plasticity (E \+ O) as two metatraits. Hanley and Garland (2018; N \= 403\) showed dispositional mindfulness associates **principally with Stability**. Erdle, Irwing, Rushton, and Park (2010; **N \= 628,640**) found self-esteem was highly correlated with the General Factor of Personality, but controlling for self-esteem reduced GFP loadings only marginally (.59 → .56)—confirming the GFP is not reducible to self-esteem. The p-factor (general psychopathology) correlates r \= **.86–.88 with neuroticism** (Levin-Aspenson et al. 2023; N \= 1,833), and Mezquita et al. (2020) found the GFP and p-factor substantially related (β \= .42–.49). The p-factor maps onto the inverse of the Stability metatrait.

## **Answers to the seven empirical questions**

**Does self-concept clarity add incremental variance beyond self-esteem and neuroticism?** Yes, but modestly and selectively. SCC uniquely predicts perseverative thinking, narrative coherence, temporal stability of self-descriptions, and social functioning beyond self-esteem and the Big Five. However, SCC's associations with depression and anxiety are substantially attenuated (β drops from −.56 to −.15) when self-esteem is controlled. SCC captures a **structural/metacognitive property** (consistency, stability of self-knowledge) rather than an evaluative one—it is the clarity of the map, not the favorability of the territory.

**Is CSE unitary or does it decompose?** CSE is robustly unitary when acquiescent responding is modeled (Gnambs 2024). However, bifactor-ESEM models show that meaningful specific variance exists for each component beyond the general factor. CSE has never been tested alongside SCC, which would likely reveal whether the structural (clarity) and evaluative (positivity) dimensions of self-knowledge are one factor or two.

**Are self-awareness, emotional granularity, and mindfulness one thing?** No. The evidence supports **at least three separable constructs**: (1) adaptive self-reflection (correlated with openness), (2) maladaptive rumination (correlated with neuroticism), and (3) mindful awareness (a distinct factor that does not load with either reflection or rumination in DaSilveira's 2015 analysis). Emotional granularity is causally downstream of mindfulness (MBSR increases granularity) but predicts outcomes beyond it. Metacognition and mindfulness form two factors, not one (Solem 2015). The umbrella concept of "metacognitive resolution" has no empirical support as a unitary latent trait.

**Where does humility land?** Humility is structurally distinct from the self-evaluative dimension. HEXACO Honesty-Humility is a sixth factor that emerges cross-linguistically, correlates weakly and positively with self-esteem (not negatively), and is not reducible to agreeableness (though it shares variance with FFM agreeableness's Straightforwardness and Modesty facets). Humility's primary structural position is as **inverse antagonism**—it correlates −.95 with the Dark Triad common factor at the latent level—rather than as a point on the self-evaluation continuum.

**Is alexithymia the low pole of emotional granularity?** No. Despite conceptual overlap, alexithymia is characterized by **negative emotional bias and self-focused attention**, while low granularity is characterized by **poor emotion-situation correspondence** (Ottenstein 2020). Granularity measures predict anxiety and depression controlling for TAS-20 alexithymia (Hoemann 2022). Alexithymia–mindfulness correlations (r ≈ −.55) leave \~70% of variance unshared. Alexithymia also has unique shared environmental contributions to its heritability (12–20%), unlike mindfulness or personality traits.

**Does social desirability function as a trait or artifact?** Primarily as a trait. de Vries et al. (2014) demonstrated SD scales measure personality rather than pure response sets. The two SD components map onto the two personality metatraits: SDE (egoistic bias) reflects Plasticity; IM (moralistic bias) reflects Stability. Controlling for SD barely affects the General Factor of Personality (Erdle & Rushton 2011). Social desirability is **substantive self-presentation motivation** embedded in the same personality hierarchy as other self-evaluative traits.

**Is self-compassion distinct from self-esteem \+ mindfulness?** Partially. With meta-analytic r \= .65 with self-esteem and r \= .53 with mindfulness, self-compassion shares enormous variance with both. The negative SCS components (self-judgment, isolation, over-identification) appear largely redundant with neuroticism at the latent level (r ≥ .85; Pfattheicher 2017). The positive components (self-kindness, common humanity) carry more unique variance. A key discriminating feature: self-compassion is uncorrelated with narcissism while self-esteem is positively correlated with it (Neff 2003). Self-compassion is best understood as **self-esteem without the narcissistic vulnerability**, with a mindfulness-based regulatory mechanism—overlapping but not redundant.

## **How many latent factors are needed?**

The cross-construct evidence converges on **three to four latent dimensions** organizing this construct space:

1. **General self-evaluation / Stability** — the dominant factor, capturing the shared variance among self-esteem, CSE, self-concept clarity, self-compassion, ego resilience, dispositional mindfulness, and (inversely) neuroticism, alexithymia, and rumination. This factor aligns with the Stability metatrait and is genetically anchored by neuroticism. Self-esteem and neuroticism are its most central indicators.

2. **Metacognitive clarity / Self-knowledge structure** — a narrower factor capturing self-concept clarity's structural properties, self-reflection (not rumination), emotional granularity, and the Describe/Act-with-Awareness facets of mindfulness. This factor is about the resolution and organization of self-knowledge rather than its evaluative valence. It retains modest variance beyond Factor 1\.

3. **Honesty-Humility / Prosocial self-presentation** — structurally distinct from self-evaluation, capturing humility, impression management, and aspects of agreeableness. This factor is orthogonal to self-esteem and reflects interpersonal rather than intrapersonal self-regulation.

4. **Openness-linked self-exploration** — capturing adaptive self-reflection, intellectual humility's open-mindedness component, and the Observe/Nonreact facets of mindfulness. This aligns with the Plasticity metatrait.

The most **central** constructs—highest average loading and most cross-factor bridging—are **neuroticism/emotional stability** (which dominates Factor 1 and bridges to factors 2 and 4), **self-esteem** (which loads strongly on Factor 1 and bridges to clinical networks), and **difficulty identifying feelings** (the alexithymia subscale that bridges interoceptive, personality, and self-awareness domains). Ego resilience is the least structurally independent construct, mapping almost entirely onto the positive poles of all Big Five traits—essentially a compound trait rather than a distinct dimension.
