# **Personality factor structure across animal taxa: what the evidence actually shows**

**The single most important finding is negative: no formal meta-analysis of personality factor structure across animal taxa exists.** What passes for cross-taxon knowledge rests on narrative reviews (Gosling & John, 1999, covering just 19 studies across 12 species), a single comparative empirical study of 7 species (White et al., 2020), and a patchwork of species-specific factor analyses conducted with radically different methods. The apparent increase in personality factor count from invertebrates (\~3 factors) through fish (\~2–3) to mammals (\~4–5) and great apes (\~5–6) is confounded by an unmeasured artifact: studies of "simpler" taxa typically measure fewer behavioral variables, which mechanically constrains extractable factors. Until this confound is resolved, claims about how personality structure evolves across the cognitive complexity gradient should be treated as provisional hypotheses, not established findings.

That said, the evidence is not silent. Three structural patterns emerge with reasonable confidence: some personality dimensions (boldness/fearfulness, activity, sociability) appear across nearly all vertebrate taxa studied; a distinct **Dominance** dimension appears in every non-human primate species tested and in several social mammals, then disappears as a separate factor only in humans; and the interfactor correlation structure — arguably the most informative feature for understanding how dimensions relate — is almost completely unknown because **\>80% of studies use varimax rotation**, which zeros correlations by mathematical fiat. This last gap is not merely an omission. It is a systematic methodological choice that renders the field unable to answer one of its most important questions.

The apparent gradient — **3 factors in octopuses, 2–3 in fish, 3–5 in non-primate mammals, 5–6 in great apes, 5 in humans** — is tantalizing but confounded. Fish and invertebrate studies typically measure **3–7 behavioral variables**. With only 5 variables, extracting more than 3 factors is mathematically impossible regardless of the animal's actual personality complexity. Primate studies using the 54-item HPQ have the measurement bandwidth to detect 6 factors. Uher and Asendorpf (2008) demonstrated this problem directly: the same chimpanzee species yielded 3 factors with one adjective list but 6 factors with a more comprehensive list. The difference was not biological — it was the measurement instrument's scope.

**No study has formally decomposed variance in factor count into taxonomic versus methodological components.** This is the single most important unfilled cell in the literature. Until someone applies comparable measurement instruments (with equal numbers of variables) across invertebrates, fish, and mammals, the question of whether factor count genuinely increases with cognitive complexity remains open.

## **Which dimensions are conserved, and which are derived?**

Despite the methodological heterogeneity, some personality dimensions recur across distantly related taxa with enough consistency to suggest either deep homology or strong convergent evolution. Others appear restricted to specific lineages.

**Dimensions with broad phylogenetic distribution.** Three personality axes appear across nearly all vertebrate taxa studied: a **boldness–fearfulness** axis (called shyness–boldness in fish, Neuroticism or Anxiety in mammals, Fear-Avoidance in guppies); an **activity** axis (general locomotion, often the first or second PCA component in fish and invertebrates); and a **sociability** axis (response to conspecifics, found from guppies through elephants to great apes). These three dimensions map loosely onto the Réale et al. (2007) theoretical framework, which proposed boldness, exploration, activity, sociability, and aggressiveness as the core axes of animal temperament. The Garamszegi et al. (2013) meta-analysis of behavioral correlations across 81 studies found the average between-trait correlation was only **r ≈ 0.19**, meaning these dimensions, while recurrent, are only weakly correlated with each other in most species.

**Dimensions with restricted distribution.** Two dimensions appear clearly restricted. **Conscientiousness** — the tendency toward orderliness, persistence, and impulse control — has been identified as a separate factor only in chimpanzees, and possibly bonobos (where it is labeled Conscientiousness but with somewhat different item content). Orangutans show a merged "Intellect" factor combining Openness and Conscientiousness content. No non-primate species has yielded a clearly separable Conscientiousness factor, though Delgado and Sulloway (2017) argued in *Psychological Bulletin* that conscientiousness-like attributes exist more broadly if defined functionally rather than psychometrically. Their evidence, however, comes from a factor analysis of citation patterns rather than behavioral data. **Openness to Experience** is similarly restricted: it appears recognizably in great apes and some monkeys (capuchins, macaques) but its manifestation in non-primates is typically limited to exploration-curiosity, missing the core human facets of openness to ideas, aesthetics, and values.

**Evidence for factor splitting and merging.** The evidence for dimensions that differentiate (split) or merge across taxa is surprisingly thin but not absent. The clearest case is the **Openness–Conscientiousness merger** in orangutans: Weiss et al. (2006, N=152) found these two human dimensions collapsed into a single "Intellect" factor, suggesting they may have differentiated only in the African great ape lineage after the orangutan split (\~14 MYA). In cats, the "Feline Five" (Litchfield et al., 2017\) includes an **Impulsiveness** factor that has no clear single Big Five analogue — it may represent a merger of low Conscientiousness and high Neuroticism content, or it may be genuinely species-specific. The **boldness–exploration** relationship in fish provides another case: in sticklebacks, boldness and aggression form a single behavioral syndrome in predator-sympatric populations but are uncorrelated in predator-naïve populations (Dingemanse et al., 2007), demonstrating that factor structure can vary between populations of the same species depending on ecological context.

## **Dominance: a dimension that disappears only in humans**

The trajectory of Dominance across the primate order is the clearest structural story in the comparative personality literature and one of the few supported by adequate sample sizes across multiple species.

Dominance appears as a **separate, often first-extracted personality factor** in every non-human primate species studied with multi-dimensional instruments. In chimpanzees, the foundational study (King & Figueredo, 1997, N=100) identified a six-factor structure — the human Big Five plus Dominance — replicated across U.S. zoos and an African sanctuary (King et al., 2005), at Yerkes (Weiss et al., 2007, N=175), and in Japanese zoos (Weiss et al., 2009, N=146). In gorillas, Dominance emerges as the first component in wild mountain gorillas (Eckardt et al., 2015, N=116), though the gorilla version emphasizes protective leadership rather than pure aggression. In orangutans (Weiss et al., 2006, N=152), Dominance appears as the second factor (after Extraversion), defined more narrowly around despotic tendencies. In bonobos (Weiss et al., 2015, N=154), the equivalent factor is labeled Assertiveness and — notably — females score higher than males, consistent with female social dominance in bonobo society.

Critically, Dominance is **not limited to great apes**. It appears in rhesus macaques (Weiss et al., 2011, N=124), Barbary macaques (Konečná et al., 2012), Hanuman langurs (Konečná et al., 2008), brown capuchins (Morton et al., 2013, N=127), and common marmosets (Weiss et al., 2021, N=128). Its presence in both Old World and New World monkeys — lineages that diverged **\~35–40 million years ago** — strongly suggests it is an ancestral primate personality dimension, not a recent innovation.

Outside primates, Dominance-like factors appear in several socially complex mammals. Cats show a separate Dominance factor in the best-powered study (Litchfield et al., 2017, N=2,802). Horses show a Dominance component (Lloyd et al., 2007). The spotted hyena's "Assertiveness" factor correlated **r \= .85** with rhesus macaque Dominance in cross-species comparison (Gosling, 1998), though this rests on just 34 hyenas. Zoo elephants yielded a Dominance factor (Yasui et al., 2013, N=75), while semi-captive elephants showed an "Aggressiveness" factor with similar content (Seltmann et al., 2018, N≈250).

**In humans, Dominance does not emerge as a separate factor.** Dominance-related traits load on Extraversion, specifically the Assertiveness facet of Costa and McCrae's NEO-PI-R. Altschul and Moore (\~2023, *Personality Neuroscience*) reviewed this "Whither Dominance?" question and found that when the HPQ is used for human self-report, no clear Dominance factor separates. Their interpretation — shared by Eckardt et al. (2015) — is that the dissolution of Dominance as an independent dimension reflects the evolutionary shift toward egalitarian social organization in ancestral humans, possibly beginning with cooperative foraging in the Pleistocene. This is plausible but remains a hypothesis; the alternative — that the disappearance is an artifact of how human personality questionnaires are constructed — cannot be fully ruled out.

The item content of Dominance shifts across species in ways that may be evolutionarily meaningful. In chimpanzees, Dominance captures competitive prowess, social competence, and fearlessness. In gorillas, it emphasizes protective, helpful, and intelligent leadership — "wise leaders" rather than aggressive despots. In macaques, it loads more on aggressive tendencies consistent with their despotic social hierarchy. Whether these differences reflect genuine species-level variation or item interpretation bias by observers is unclear.

## **Interfactor correlations: the question the field cannot answer**

The interfactor correlation structure — how personality dimensions relate to each other — is arguably more informative about the architecture of personality than factor count alone. A system of 5 uncorrelated factors has fundamentally different implications than a system of 5 factors with moderate positive intercorrelations (suggesting a general factor) or a system with mixed positive and negative correlations (suggesting trade-offs). In humans, the Big Five show modest intercorrelations (typically |r| \= 0.1–0.3), and oblique rotation is now standard practice.

In the animal personality literature, **this question is almost completely unanswerable from published data**. The reason is simple and structural: the dominant analytic approach is PCA with varimax rotation, which forces all interfactor correlations to exactly zero by mathematical constraint. Weiss (2017) describes the standard HPQ protocol: researchers run both promax (oblique) and varimax (orthogonal) rotations, but if promax correlations fall below r \= 0.40, the study defaults to varimax and the correlation information is discarded. Simpson et al. confirmed this practice for infant macaque personality: "we examined each structure with both varimax and promax rotations; if the correlations in the promax rotation were relatively low (below r \= 0.40) then we used the varimax rotation."

This means that moderate interfactor correlations (r \= 0.20–0.39) — which are precisely the range found in human personality data — are systematically hidden. They are checked, found to be "low" by an arbitrary threshold, and then eliminated from the reported results by switching to varimax. The consequence is that the field literally cannot determine whether personality dimensions in animals are orthogonal, moderately correlated, or hierarchically organized.

The handful of exceptions are revealing. Litchfield et al. (2017) used oblimin rotation for the Feline Five and reported correlations (values in supplementary materials). Morton et al. (2013) reported promax correlations for brown capuchins in supplementary Table S2. Weiss et al. (2021) found sufficiently high interfactor correlations in marmosets to extract second-order factors (Pro-Sociality and Boldness), implying the first-order factors were meaningfully correlated. These scattered data points suggest that interfactor correlations exist and vary across species, but there are too few data points to identify any pattern.

**The most productive low-cost intervention the field could make would be to re-analyze existing varimax datasets with oblique rotation and publish the interfactor correlation matrices.** No new data collection is needed — the information already exists in the raw data. This re-analysis could determine whether personality dimensions in different species show the same correlation patterns, whether there is evidence for a General Factor of Personality (Adams et al. tested for and failed to find a GFP in chimpanzees, orangutans, and rhesus macaques), and whether interfactor correlations change systematically across the cognitive complexity gradient.

## **How much of the observed structure is method, not biology?**

Five methodological factors demonstrably shape the personality structures that emerge from factor analyses, and the field has not adequately controlled for any of them.

**Variable count constrains factor count.** This is the most consequential confound. Fish personality studies typically measure 3–7 behavioral variables, invertebrate studies 5–19, and primate studies 43–54 questionnaire items. The number of extractable factors cannot exceed the number of input variables, so the apparent increase in factor count from fish to primates partly reflects measurement bandwidth. The Réale et al. (2007) framework proposes 5 dimensions for all animals, but most individual fish studies can only test 2–3 of these because they measure too few variables. No study has applied a 50+ item instrument to fish or invertebrates to test whether more factors would emerge with adequate measurement bandwidth.

**Etic instruments impose human structure.** The HPQ, used in \~58% of primate trait-rating studies, was derived from Goldberg's human Big Five lexical taxonomy with 2 added ape-specific items. It reliably yields Big Five–like factors plus Dominance — but this may partly reflect the instrument's construction rather than the animals' actual personality organization. When emic (bottom-up) approaches are used, different structures emerge. In dogs, the etic approach (Gosling et al., 2003, N=78) yielded 4 human-like factors, while the emic DPQ (Jones, 2009, N=6,293) yielded Fearfulness, two types of Aggression, Activity/Excitability, and Responsiveness to Training — a structure that does not map onto the Big Five. Uher and Asendorpf (2008) found "trait constructs beyond current personality models" using their bottom-up Behavioral Repertoire × Situations approach with great apes. Koski (2011) explicitly criticized the HPQ's etic bias, noting that "the selection of traits assumed to be relevant was based on the human five-factor model, and thus chosen top-down."

**Questionnaire ratings and behavioral coding yield different structures.** Vazire et al. (2007) found mean ICC was .42 for behavioral codings versus .61 for trait ratings, suggesting ratings are more reliable but potentially more subject to observer bias. Uher and Asendorpf (2008) found that behavior ratings best predicted actual behavior, but adjective ratings (the HPQ approach) captured aspects "not captured through codings, including intelligence, apprehension, excitability and gentleness." The upshot: trait ratings yield broader, more human-like personality factors; behavioral codings yield narrower, more behavior-specific dimensions. The method partially determines the conclusions.

**PCA inflates factor structure.** The overwhelming majority of animal personality studies use PCA (principal component analysis), which is technically not factor analysis at all. PCA assumes all variance is meaningful and tends to inflate factor loadings compared to proper EFA methods (maximum likelihood, unweighted least squares). With small samples — common in zoo and wildlife studies — this distinction matters. Budaev (2010, *Ethology*) provided detailed guidelines for animal behavior researchers, noting that many simply use SPSS defaults without considering whether PCA or EFA is more appropriate. Proper EFA has been used in only a handful of studies: Weiss et al. (2015) for bonobos (unweighted least squares), Litchfield et al. (2017) for cats (principal axis factoring), and Aguilar et al. (2007) for mice.

**Ecological context reshapes factor structure within species.** The stickleback literature provides the most dramatic example: the boldness–aggression–activity behavioral syndrome exists in populations sympatric with predators but not in predator-naïve populations (Dingemanse et al., 2007, across 12 populations). Bell and Sih (2007) showed that predation exposure can *generate* the boldness–aggressiveness correlation. If ecological pressures can create or dissolve behavioral syndromes within a single species, then cross-taxon comparisons of factor structure must account for ecological context — and almost none do.

## **What we know, what we don't, and what we could learn without new data**

The evidence supports three conclusions with moderate confidence. First, **a small set of personality dimensions — boldness/fearfulness, activity, and sociability — recur across vertebrate taxa**, though whether this reflects deep homology or convergent selection cannot be determined from personality data alone. Second, **Dominance is an ancestral primate personality dimension** that appears across all tested primates (New World, Old World, and great apes) and dissolves into Extraversion only in humans — the strongest cross-taxon structural finding in the literature. Third, **Conscientiousness as a separable factor is restricted to chimpanzees and possibly bonobos**, making it the most phylogenetically derived of the well-established personality dimensions.

The evidence does **not** support confident claims about a cognitive-complexity gradient in factor count. The apparent increase from 2–3 factors in fish to 5–6 in great apes is confounded by measurement bandwidth (fewer variables measured in "simpler" taxa), instrument bias (etic instruments designed from human personality structure), and analytic choices (varimax rotation, PCA instead of EFA). Uher and Asendorpf's (2008) demonstration that item list scope — not species biology — drove factor count differences in chimpanzees is a serious challenge to the complexity-gradient narrative.

Three empirical questions could be substantially advanced by re-analyzing existing data, without collecting a single new data point:

* **Oblique re-rotation of all varimax HPQ datasets** would reveal interfactor correlations across \~15 primate species and allow the first systematic comparison of correlation structure across taxa. The raw data exist; the promax correlations have in many cases already been computed and then discarded.

* **Applying comparable multi-item questionnaires to non-primate species** would test whether the low factor counts in fish and invertebrates are biological or artifactual. If dogs yield 5 factors from 75 items, what would guppies yield from 75 behavioral measures?

* **Formal variance decomposition** of factor count across published studies — partitioning variance into taxonomic group, number of variables, measurement method (etic/emic, questionnaire/behavioral), rotation method, and sample size — would quantify how much of the cross-taxon variation in factor count is methodological. The data for this analysis already exist in published studies; it requires only systematic coding and multilevel modeling.

The field of comparative animal personality has established that personality exists across animal taxa, that it is repeatable (meta-analytic r ≈ 0.37; Bell et al., 2009), and that it has fitness consequences. But it has not yet answered its most structurally interesting question — whether the architecture of personality changes across the tree of life — because the methodological tools needed to answer it (standardized cross-taxon instruments, oblique rotation as default, adequate variable counts in all taxa) have not been consistently applied. The evidence is richest where the methods are most standardized (great apes using the HPQ), thinnest where they are most varied (fish and invertebrates using idiosyncratic behavioral batteries), and most distorted where the analytic choices (varimax rotation, PCA, etic instruments) systematically shape what can be found. Honesty requires acknowledging that the most cited narrative — personality gets more complex as brains get bigger — remains more assumption than established fact.

**Yes, individual differences in nematode behavior are an active research area, but the framing is different from what you're imagining, and your specific design (barrier-crossing as an individual-difference covariate in a repeated-measures or cross-task design) appears not to have been done.**

**How individuality is quantified.** The dominant approach is high-throughput automated tracking, not observer judgment. Harel et al. (2024, *Cell Reports*) continuously extracted \~1.2 billion body postures from \~2,200 individual *C. elegans* throughout their full development, using unsupervised inference of low-dimensional movement modes to identify both stereotyped developmental trajectories and individual-unique behavioral spaces. The landmark earlier study is Stern, Kirst, and Bargmann (2017, *Cell*), from the Bargmann lab at Rockefeller, which monitored single nematodes over their complete developmental trajectories at high spatiotemporal resolution and found that a fraction of individuals within isogenic populations raised in controlled environments have consistent, non-genetic behavioral biases that persist across development. These biases are in spontaneous foraging behaviors — roaming vs. dwelling, speed, postural dynamics — and they're stable over hours and days, even among genetically identical animals reared in identical environments.

So the measurement approach is overwhelmingly automated posture/locomotion tracking, not observer ratings. No one is watching a worm and making a judgment call about its "personality." The field uses PCA on postural time series to extract movement modes, then looks at which individuals deviate from the population stereotypy.

**The barrier-crossing paradigm you're describing exists, but it's used differently.** Ghosh et al. (2016, *Neuron*) confronted *C. elegans* with the decision whether to cross a hyperosmotic barrier presenting the threat of desiccation to reach a source of food odor (diacetyl), and identified a specific interneuron (RIM) that controls this decision. The barrier is typically copper sulfate or high-osmolarity fructose rather than NaCl specifically, but the logic is exactly what you described — titrate the aversive concentration to a range where some worms cross and some don't. Matty et al. (2022, *PLoS Genetics*) used this same sensory integration assay to show that food-deprived animals cross the copper barrier more readily than well-fed animals, driven by insulin signaling between intestine and neurons.

However, the key difference from what you're asking about is this: **these studies treat barrier-crossing as a population-level outcome variable modulated by experimental condition (fed vs. starved, wild-type vs. mutant), not as an individual-difference variable.** They report the fraction of worms that cross, compare conditions, and manipulate circuits. They do not track which individual worms cross, then re-test those same individuals on a second task to ask whether "crossers" differ from "non-crossers" on some other behavioral dimension.

**Why hasn't your design been done?** I think there are two practical reasons. First, the technical infrastructure for long-term individual tracking of *C. elegans* is optimized for single-worm-on-a-plate continuous monitoring (Stern et al. 2017\) or multi-worm population tracking (the Multi-Worm Tracker). The barrier-crossing assay is typically a batch endpoint assay — put 50–100 worms on a plate, count how many are on each side after 15 minutes. Tracking *which specific individual* crossed and then recovering that individual for a second assay is technically harder with worms than it sounds, because they're 1mm long and you can't mark them. Second, the C. elegans individuality literature frames inter-individual variation as arising from neuromodulatory systems (dopamine, serotonin, neuropeptide receptor NPR-1, TGF-β peptide DAF-7) operating on developmental timescales, not as stable trait-like individual differences in the personality psychology sense. The conceptual vocabulary is "behavioral states" and "individuality dimensions," not "personality traits."

That said, Lockery's lab has shown that *C. elegans* food choices satisfy the Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference — consistent internal preference orderings at the individual level — using microfluidic Y-chips that track single-worm choices. And Stephens et al. (2019, *J. R. Soc. Interface*) found that motility parameters across nematode species vary in a correlated, low-dimensional manner suggestive of a common mode of behavioral control, which is structurally analogous to a factor-analytic finding even though they don't frame it that way. So the pieces exist — individual tracking technology, barrier-crossing paradigms, evidence of stable individual biases, microfluidic single-worm choice tracking — but nobody has assembled them into the cross-task individual-difference covariate design you're describing.

**Bottom line:** The specific experiment you're imagining — use a mid-concentration barrier to classify individual worms as "crossers" vs. "non-crossers," then use that as a predictor in a second behavioral assay — is a clean design that is technically feasible with current tools but has not been reported. In terms of **discrete behavioral variables that have been measured across individual worms**, the literature gives you something like this, though never assembled into a single multi-trait study:

* Roaming vs. dwelling (proportion of time in each state)  
* Instantaneous speed  
* Reversal frequency  
* Omega turn frequency  
* Exploration range / path tortuosity  
* Chemotaxis index (attraction/avoidance to specific chemicals)  
* Barrier-crossing probability (the copper/osmolarity assay)  
* Patch exploitation latency (how long before accepting a food patch)  
* Postural modes (the first 3–4 eigenshapes)

That's maybe 8–10 distinguishable behavioral dimensions if you were to treat them as separate variables. But critically, these have never been measured in the same individuals simultaneously and subjected to factor extraction. The Yemini et al. (2013, *Nature Methods*) behavioral phenotyping database measures **702 behavioral parameters** per worm — but those are mostly fine-grained kinematic decompositions of locomotion (midbody amplitude, head bend angle, etc.), not functionally distinct behavioral categories in the personality-relevant sense, and the database is designed for mutant-vs-wild-type classification, not individual-difference structure within a genotype.

So to map this back onto the variable-count confound from the main report: nematode studies have anywhere from **2 behavioral variables** (roaming/dwelling in Stern et al.) to **702 kinematic parameters** (Yemini et al.), but the number that would be appropriate inputs to a personality-style factor analysis — functionally distinct, repeatedly measured behavioral categories — is probably in the range of **5–10**. Which, per the logic of the main report, mechanically constrains you to extracting at most 2–3 factors regardless of the organism's actual behavioral complexity.

The Réale et al. (2007) framework would predict that nematodes should show at least boldness, exploration, and activity as separable dimensions. The barrier-crossing paradigm is arguably a boldness measure. Roaming/dwelling maps onto exploration. Speed maps onto activity. Whether these are separable factors or a single axis in nematodes is an empirical question that, as far as I can tell, has simply never been asked in factor-analytic terms.

Right — this is exactly the interfactor correlation problem from the report, applied to the specific case that matters most.

The honest answer is: **we almost certainly cannot answer this from published data, and the reason is the varimax problem compounded by a measurement problem.**

Here's what would need to be true for us to know whether boldness, activity, and sociability intercorrelate similarly across taxa:

1. Multiple species would need to have all three dimensions extracted as separate factors in the same study.  
2. Those studies would need to have used oblique rotation.  
3. They would need to report the interfactor correlation matrix.

Condition 1 is rarely met. Most fish and invertebrate studies measure boldness and activity but not sociability (because many study designs isolate individual animals). Most mammal questionnaire studies capture all three but bundle them with other dimensions. The Réale et al. (2007) framework proposes all five dimensions theoretically, but very few empirical studies measure all five in the same sample with enough variables per dimension to extract them separately.

Condition 2 is met in maybe 3–4 studies total across the entire animal personality literature. And condition 3 — actually reporting the correlation values — is met in even fewer.

So what we're left with is a situation where the human finding you're pointing to (activity and sociability cluster together as Extraversion facets, and both are more distant from fearfulness/Neuroticism) is one of the most robust structural findings in all of personality psychology, replicated across dozens of cultures — and we have essentially **zero comparable data** from non-human animals to know whether that same clustering holds.

There are a few indirect hints, none strong enough to draw conclusions from. The behavioral syndrome literature in fish (Dingemanse, Bell, and others) finds that boldness and activity often correlate positively within populations — bold fish tend to be more active — which is at least directionally consistent with the human pattern where both load on Extraversion. But those correlations come from bivariate analyses (just correlating two behaviors), not from a factor model where you can ask about the full pattern. And sociability is almost never in the same dataset because most fish personality studies use isolated individuals in novel-environment or predator-exposure tests.

The Garamszegi et al. (2013) meta-analysis of behavioral correlations across 81 studies found the average pairwise correlation between personality-relevant behaviors was only r ≈ 0.19 — but that's across all behavior pairs, not specifically boldness-activity or activity-sociability. And it's a meta-analytic average that collapses across species, measurement approaches, and contexts, so it can't tell you whether the *pattern* of correlations (which pairs are higher vs. lower) resembles the human pattern.

The one place where you'd expect the best data is great apes, because the HPQ studies extract 5–6 factors with enough items per factor that oblique rotation could reveal the correlation structure. And some of those studies did run promax rotation — but as the report describes, the standard practice is to check whether promax correlations exceed r \= 0.40, and if they don't, default to varimax and discard the correlations. So the information may literally exist in supplementary tables or in unpublished rotation outputs sitting on Alexander Weiss's hard drive, but it's not systematically available in the published literature.

This is actually a stronger version of the gap than I flagged in the main report. It's not just that we don't know whether interfactor correlations *differ* across taxa — we don't even know whether the most basic structural feature of human personality (the clustering of activity and sociability into Extraversion, separate from Neuroticism) has any phylogenetic depth at all. That's a pretty striking hole given how central Extraversion is to the human personality literature.

If you wanted to push on this, the single highest-value analysis would be to take the existing HPQ datasets for chimpanzees, orangutans, bonobos, and macaques, re-run them all with oblique rotation, and compare the interfactor correlation matrices — specifically asking whether the Extraversion-like factors (Surgency, which captures both sociability and activity content) correlate with Dominance and Neuroticism in patterns that resemble or diverge from the human structure. The data exist. The analysis would take an afternoon. Nobody has published it.
