# **What Is "Social Cognition"? Are Dogs Socially Savvier Than Apes?**

**Reference note.** Synthesizes Bohn et al. 2026 (*Psychological Science*) and Bennett (*A Brief History of Intelligence*) on the structure of social cognition in great apes, with implications for the dogs-outperform-apes claim.

## **The short answer**

"Social cognition" is not one thing. It fragments along two independent axes:

* **Evolutionary** (Bennett): gaze-following, joint attention, intent-attribution, knowledge-attribution, and false-belief tracking are separate systems with different phylogenetic origins. Some are reflexes shared with turtles. One is a primate breakthrough riding on the granular prefrontal cortex.  
* **Psychometric** (Bohn et al.): when you run multiple "social" tasks longitudinally on the same great apes, performance on one doesn't predict performance on another. There is no coherent social-cognition factor at the individual-differences level.

The folk grouping "social cognition" survives in neither frame. Any claim about a species being "socially smarter" than another has to specify *which system*, on *which task*.

## **What Bohn et al. 2026 actually found**

48 great apes (12 bonobos, 24 chimps, 6 gorillas, 6 orangutans), six tasks, ten time points over 1.5 years, pooled with three more tasks from Bohn et al. 2023\.

Three tasks were tagged as "social cognition":

* **Gaze-following** — does the ape look up when the experimenter looks up?  
* **Attention-following** — object-choice based on where the experimenter is looking  
* **Communicative-cues** — ostensive cues plus pointing/tapping the cup with the food

Result: these three tasks did not correlate with each other, and did not correlate with any of the nonsocial tasks. Meanwhile, nonsocial tasks (executive function, quantity discrimination, inferential reasoning) formed a coherent cluster — 12 of 21 correlations significant, the rest positive.

The authors are explicit that this isn't a power problem. They have ten measurements per individual and a measurement model that separates true-score variance from noise. The communicative-cues task itself is reliably measuring *something stable*; it just isn't measuring the same thing the other "social" tasks are measuring.

The methodological point worth keeping: **consistency** (proportion of variance from a stable trait) and **reliability** (proportion of true-score variance vs. noise) are different. A task can have high consistency and low reliability, in which case the trait may be real but the measure is too noisy to say much. Probabilistic-reasoning failed both tests. Early-stage attention-following had high consistency but low reliability — the apes seem to have changed strategy partway through the study.

## **What Bennett's framework says**

Bennett places mentalizing as the fourth evolutionary breakthrough, \~10–30 mya, riding on the granular prefrontal cortex (gPFC). It's a "second-order generative model" — a model of one's own mind, which can then be extended to model others' minds.

Crucially, Bennett distinguishes mentalizing-proper from things that look social but aren't:

* **Reflexive gaze-following** — "even turtles do it"; brainstem-level  
* **Imperative cue-following** — basal ganglia, model-free reinforcement learning, "all vertebrates can do this"  
* **Joint attention** — deliberate triadic confirmation that two minds are attending to the same thing; Bennett claims nonhuman primates don't do this (contested)  
* **Mentalizing-proper** — intent attribution, knowledge attribution, false-belief tracking, tactical deception; gPFC-mediated

The first two are *not* mentalizing. The diagnostic tasks for mentalizing in Bennett's frame are goggles tasks, knower-guesser, unable-vs-unwilling, false-belief paradigms, and deception sequences (Belle/Rock).

## **The two frames converge on the same point**

Bohn's three "social" tasks map onto Bennett's pre-mentalizing systems:

| Bohn task | Bennett's system | Mentalizing? |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Gaze-following | Reflex (brainstem) | No |
| Attention-following (object-choice) | Imperative cue-following (basal ganglia, RL) | No |
| Communicative-cues | Cue-reading from ostensive signals | No |

So Bohn's null result on "social cognition" isn't surprising under Bennett's framework. The three tasks tap three separate lower systems with different evolutionary origins. They *shouldn't* correlate. The folk grouping put them together; the underlying neural architecture doesn't.

The Bohn battery contains no mentalizing-proper task. So the study cannot speak to whether mentalizing is a coherent individual-differences trait in apes — that question remains empirically open.

## **Are dogs socially savvier than apes?**

Brian Hare and colleagues have shown for two decades that dogs outperform chimpanzees on **object-choice tasks** — following human points, gaze cues, and other ostensive signals to find hidden food. This is real and replicated.

Bennett's reading, which is the right one: dogs are better at **cue-following from humans** than apes are. They are not better at **mentalizing**. When you run the knower-guesser paradigm — where the dog has to track *which human knows where the food is* — dogs fail. They can't represent that two humans hold different knowledge.

So the dogs-savvier-than-apes claim is paradigm-dependent in a specific way:

* **On cue-following from humans**: yes, dogs win. This is plausibly a domestication effect — dogs have been selected for \~15,000 years for attentiveness to human signals.  
* **On mentalizing-proper**: no, apes win. Apes pass goggles tasks, knower-guesser, intent-attribution, and increasingly false-belief tracking (Krupenye et al. 2016 and follow-ups). Dogs do not.

The "dogs are socially smarter than apes" headline survives only if you (a) define social cognition as cue-following from humans, and (b) ignore everything else. Once you carve social cognition into its actual subsystems, the comparison reverses on the systems that matter most for understanding the evolution of mind.

## **What's still empirically open**

Nobody has run a longitudinal psychometric battery on mentalizing-proper tasks (goggles, false-belief, knower-guesser, unable-vs-unwilling) in great apes. Bennett's framework predicts that if someone did:

* The mentalizing tasks would correlate with each other (one underlying system)  
* They wouldn't correlate with Bohn's three "social" tasks (different system)  
* They wouldn't correlate with the nonsocial cluster (different breakthrough)  
* Performance would track with rank, deception sophistication, and gPFC volume

This is a falsifiable prediction. The current literature underdetermines it.

## **What the evidence supports, in one sentence**

There is no unified "social cognition" — there are at least four separable systems (reflexive gaze-following, imperative cue-reading, joint attention, mentalizing-proper) with distinct evolutionary origins, distinct neural substrates, and distinct individual-differences profiles, and any cross-species comparison that doesn't specify which system is being measured produces results that look paradoxical only because the construct was wrong to begin with.
