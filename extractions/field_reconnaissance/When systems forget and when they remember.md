# **When systems forget and when they remember**

**The most consistent finding across all six domains is that forgetting is the default state of nearly every information-processing system.** Remembering is the exception — it requires dedicated infrastructure, active maintenance, and ongoing energy expenditure. The single variable that comes closest to a clean boundary condition separating the two regimes is **environmental autocorrelation** (how well the past predicts the future at the system's relevant timescale), but it is not sufficient alone. The actual structure is a two-dimensional interaction between the intrinsic dissipation rate of the storage substrate and the autocorrelation of the environment, with a third modulating variable — the cost of maintaining memory relative to re-acquiring it. Where these variables collide is where the most interesting pathologies and anomalies arise: systems trapped remembering what they should forget, or losing what they desperately need to retain.

This report surveys six domains — plant epigenetics, immune systems, neural systems, machine learning, organizational memory, and cultural memory — then synthesizes the findings to characterize the boundary between the forgetting and remembering regimes.

---

## **Plants erase almost everything, but self-reinforcing loops can override the reset**

In plant biology, **forgetting is emphatically the default**. Plants have evolved at least six overlapping mechanisms to erase epigenetic marks between generations: the DNA glycosylase DEMETER strips stress-induced methylation in the male germline; the histone demethylase ELF6 resets vernalization memory at FLC during embryogenesis; DDM1 and MOM1 suppress transgenerational memory; and a novel triple mechanism in sperm — silencing of Polycomb writers, activation of Polycomb erasers, and deposition of the resetting histone variant H3.10 — ensures that histone-based memory is wiped clean each generation. Wibowo et al. (2016) demonstrated this concretely: in wild-type *Arabidopsis*, stress-induced DNA methylation marks are actively erased by DEMETER in the male germline, but in *dme* mutants the marks persist and transmit paternally. The plant "knows" something but chooses to forget it.

The exceptions illuminate the rule. **Paramutation at the maize *b1* locus has never reverted in over 50 years** and thousands of plants — a self-reinforcing siRNA loop, where the silenced state produces the very small RNAs that maintain silencing, creates a thermodynamic trap that overwhelms normal resetting machinery. The *Linaria vulgaris* peloric epiallele has persisted stably for **over 250 years** through CG methylation at a promoter. In allotetraploid cotton, some epialleles appear stable across **\~1 million years of evolution**. What these cases share is not environmental stability but a storage format — DNA methylation at repetitive or TE-associated loci — that feeds back on itself.

The key predictors of forgetting vs. remembering in plants are: (a) the mark type (histone modifications reset; CG methylation persists), (b) the genomic context (euchromatic marks are fragile; heterochromatic and repeat-associated marks self-correct), (c) the reproductive mode (sexual reproduction resets; clonal propagation preserves), and (d) the number of generations of exposure (single-generation stress marks usually reset within 1–2 generations; multigenerational exposure can produce lasting marks). The critical anomaly is that when Williams & Gehring (2017) disrupted the ROS1/RdDM equilibrium, **heterochromatic methylation self-corrected within four generations while euchromatic methylation was permanently scarred** — demonstrating that the storage substrate's intrinsic repair dynamics, not just the environment, determine persistence.

## **Immune systems reveal that storage architecture dictates the default**

Across immune architectures, the clearest predictor of whether forgetting or remembering is the default is whether the system has **dedicated, long-lived memory cells**. This is not a trivial restatement — it identifies a specific substrate property that separates the regimes.

Vertebrate adaptive immunity is the most remembering-biased system in this survey. Amanna, Carlson & Slifka (2007) measured antibody half-lives across 8 antigens in 45 subjects: **measles antibodies have a half-life of \~3,000 years** (effectively infinite), while tetanus antibodies decay with a half-life of \~11 years. The key variable is antigen valence at induction — multivalent viral antigens produce far more durable responses than monomeric protein antigens. This memory is maintained by long-lived plasma cells in specialized bone marrow niches, independently of memory B cells. Bhoj et al. (2016) showed that patients receiving CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy, which ablates all B cells, nonetheless maintained vaccine-specific antibody titers for months — proving the independence of the plasma cell memory reservoir.

Every other immune architecture defaults to forgetting. **CRISPR-Cas systems lose spacers 374 times more frequently than nucleotide mutations occur** (Fehrenbach et al., 2024), with blockwise deletions averaging 2.7 spacers per event. Arrays are kept short — typically under 50 spacers in bacteria — due to crRNA dilution (more spacers mean weaker per-spacer defense), autoimmunity risk (\~0.4% of spacers are self-targeting), and restriction of beneficial horizontal gene transfer. Invertebrate immune priming typically lasts **one generation at most** (Tetreau et al., 2019, surveying 57 studies across 21+ species). Trained immunity in mammals, mediated by epigenetic reprogramming of monocytes and hematopoietic stem cells, persists for months to perhaps a year before natural cell turnover dilutes the marks.

The most revealing anomaly is **original antigenic sin** — where the vertebrate system's strong remembering bias becomes actively harmful. Pre-existing memory B cells, with their kinetic advantage over naive cells, dominate the response to variant pathogens and produce cross-reactive but poorly neutralizing antibodies. This is directly responsible for reduced vaccine efficacy against evolved influenza strains and antibody-dependent enhancement in secondary dengue infections. **The system cannot selectively forget its first encounter with a pathogen class**, and this rigidity is a direct structural consequence of dedicated long-lived memory cells. A parallel anomaly exists in trained immunity: metabolic danger signals (oxidized LDL, hyperglycemia) trigger the same epigenetic reprogramming evolved for infection defense, producing **chronic maladaptive inflammation that drives atherosclerosis** — trained immunity that the system has no mechanism to erase.

## **Neural forgetting is chronic, molecular, and actively regulated**

The neuroscience literature has undergone a paradigm shift over the past 15 years: **forgetting is not passive decay but an active, molecularly regulated process** that is the brain's default operating state. Davis & Zhong (2017) stated this explicitly: "Intrinsic forgetting may be the default state of the brain, constantly promoting memory erasure and competing with processes that promote memory stability like consolidation."

At least four independent molecular mechanisms drive active forgetting. In *Drosophila*, **Rac1-dependent cytoskeletal remodeling** continuously erases associative memories through the Scribble/Cofilin pathway (Shuai et al., 2010); this mechanism is conserved in mice (Liu et al., 2016). The **same dopamine neurons** that write memories also erase them — dDA1 receptors drive acquisition via cAMP, while DAMB receptors drive forgetting via calcium signaling (Berry et al., 2012). Critically, these dopamine neurons fire continuously in awake flies, constituting a **chronic forgetting signal** that is only suppressed during sleep. In mammals, **Synaptotagmin-3 drives AMPA receptor endocytosis** at synapses; Syt3 knockout mice learn normally but exhibit a striking inability to forget (Awasthi et al., 2019). And adult neurogenesis in the hippocampus actively disrupts old memory traces as new neurons integrate into existing circuits (Akers et al., 2014\) — a mechanism so powerful that it explains infantile amnesia: infant rodents, with their high neurogenesis rates, cannot retain hippocampal memories, but precocial species like guinea pigs (where neurogenesis is largely prenatal) do not show infantile amnesia.

Cross-species comparisons reveal that **ecological context calibrates forgetting rates with precision**. Clark's nutcrackers cache 22,000–33,000 seeds in 5,000–6,000 locations and retrieve 70–90% over 285+ days — but their visual memory capacity is actually *inferior* to pigeons' (Cook et al., 2018). Memory enhancement is domain-specific, not global: **forgetting is suppressed selectively where ecological demand is highest**. Bats retain spatial memories through hibernation despite massive synapse loss, while ground squirrels lose conditioned memories but preserve kin recognition — a hierarchy of memory protection calibrated to survival value (Ruczynski & Siemers, 2011; Millesi et al., 2001; Mateo & Johnston, 2000).

Richards & Frankland (2017) proposed the theoretical framework that best unifies these findings: the goal of memory is not veridical transmission of information but **optimization of intelligent decision-making**. Forgetting prevents overfitting, reduces proactive interference, and conserves metabolic resources. This is formally analogous to regularization in machine learning — a connection that turns out to be deep.

## **Artificial neural networks formalize what biology discovers empirically**

Standard artificial neural networks default to catastrophic forgetting for a precisely characterizable reason: **shared distributed representations mean that gradient updates for any new task can overwrite parameters critical for old tasks**. Lin et al. (2023, ICML) provided the first closed-form expression for expected forgetting in continual learning, revealing that forgetting approaches zero as the number of parameters approaches infinity — overparameterization is a brute-force solution that works by providing enough "room" for all tasks to coexist without interference.

The machine learning literature has formalized the key variables with a rigor that biological domains lack. **Environmental stationarity** determines the optimal strategy: in stationary distributions, all past data remains relevant and should be remembered; under concept drift, old data represents an outdated distribution and forgetting is optimal. Biehl et al. (2021) used statistical mechanics to show that the **optimal learning rate** (which directly controls forgetting rate) scales with the drift rate of the environment — faster drift demands faster forgetting. This is the ML equivalent of environmental autocorrelation.

The most productive anomaly is **beneficial forgetting**. The lottery ticket hypothesis (Frankle & Carlin, 2019\) shows that dense networks contain sparse subnetworks ("winning tickets") comprising less than 10–20% of parameters that match or exceed the full network's performance — meaning 80–90% of learned weights are not just unnecessary but actively harmful to generalization. Pruning them away is a form of selective forgetting that improves performance. The information bottleneck perspective (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby) reveals that most of training time is spent not learning but *compressing* — forgetting irrelevant input details to extract robust representations. Even dropout, one of the most successful regularization techniques, works by **randomly forgetting activations during training**, preventing co-adaptation and improving generalization.

The formal result from complementary learning systems theory (McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly, 1995\) deserves emphasis: **the brain avoids catastrophic interference by maintaining two systems with deliberately different forgetting rates** — a fast-learning, fast-forgetting hippocampal system and a slow-learning, slow-forgetting cortical system. Information transfers from fast to slow through replay, interleaved with ongoing experience. This architecture, replicated computationally in generative replay methods, suggests that the optimal solution to the forgetting-remembering trade-off is not a single rate but **a dual-rate architecture** that remembers and forgets at different timescales simultaneously.

## **Organizations face a paradox: thermodynamic forgetting vs. behavioral remembering**

Organizational memory presents a paradox not found in the other domains. **Forgetting is the thermodynamic default** — knowledge depreciates without active maintenance. Benkard (2000) estimated that at Lockheed's L-1011 production line, only **\~61% of accumulated production experience survived per year**. Moon et al. found that each percentage-point increase in weekly worker turnover increased cellphone failure rates by 0.74–0.79%. NASA literally lost the capability to go to the moon because institutional memory was not actively maintained.

Yet **remembering is simultaneously the behavioral default** — once routines, capabilities, and cultures form, they resist change through self-reinforcing mechanisms. Leonard-Barton (1992) showed that core capabilities become core rigidities; Levitt & March (1988) identified competency traps where favorable performance with an inferior procedure prevents adoption of superior alternatives; Henderson & Clark (1990) demonstrated that established firms failed at architectural innovation precisely because their organizational memory about "how things work" prevented recognizing structural shifts.

This paradox resolves once you distinguish between the *storage substrates*. Knowledge embedded in individuals (tacit knowledge, relational capital) dissipates rapidly with turnover — forgetting is the default. Knowledge embedded in routines, tools, and organizational structures (explicit procedures, architectural decisions, cultural values) persists stubbornly — remembering is the default. The same organization simultaneously suffers from too much forgetting in one substrate and too much remembering in another. **The most dangerous configuration is when an organization forgets its factual knowledge (what worked and why) while retaining its behavioral patterns (how we do things here)** — it loses the rationale but keeps the habit.

Christensen's innovator's dilemma is the organizational equivalent of original antigenic sin: incumbents' memory is most dangerous when it is most accurate about current markets, because accurate memory of what current customers want prevents response to disruptive innovations that initially serve different markets. **The system remembers too well what is true right now, and this prevents it from adapting to what will be true next.**

## **Cultural memory is the domain where technology most dramatically shifts the default**

For most of human history, **forgetting was the overwhelming default for collective memory**. Jan Assmann's framework quantifies this: communicative memory (informal, interpersonal, based on lived experience) degrades within **80–100 years** — about three to four generations. Anything that persists beyond this horizon must be deliberately converted into cultural memory through institutionalization: texts, rituals, monuments, ceremonies maintained by specialists.

The major anomaly in this domain is the extraordinary persistence of Aboriginal Australian oral traditions, which preserve accurate memories of post-glacial sea-level rise from **7,000–10,000+ years ago** across 300–400 generations (Nunn, Hamacher, Reid, multiple papers 2015–2023). This dramatically exceeds the expected durability of oral memory and reveals the critical variables: specialized story-keepers with formal accountability for accuracy, landscape anchoring through songlines, survival utility of the encoded information, multi-modal encoding (song, dance, place), and cultural isolation preventing contamination. **When institutional infrastructure is sufficient, even the most dissipative storage medium (human memory) can achieve persistence rivaling written records.**

The digital age represents an unprecedented inversion. Mayer-Schönberger (2009) argued that digital technology has made **remembering the default and forgetting the effortful act** for the first time in human history. The EU's "Right to Be Forgotten" (GDPR Article 17\) is essentially a legal mechanism to reimpose forgetting on a substrate that no longer naturally supports it. This inversion is already producing novel pathologies: frozen-in-time reputations, inability to form new identities, and the weaponization of archived information against individuals decades after the fact.

The most instructive cases involve deliberate collective forgetting. Spain's *Pacto del Olvido* after Franco's death enabled a bloodless democratic transition but created a growing moral debt — an estimated 114,000+ people remain in unmarked mass graves. Rwanda simultaneously mandates intensive genocide commemoration while legally prohibiting public discussion of ethnicity, creating a dual regime of enforced remembering and enforced forgetting. Both cases demonstrate that **adaptive forgetting has a temporal horizon** beyond which it becomes maladaptive, and that the boundary depends on political context rather than any universal principle.

## **The synthesis: two variables, one interaction, and no perfectly clean boundary**

Having surveyed all six domains, I can now address the central question directly: **is there a clean boundary condition separating the forgetting regime from the remembering regime?**

The answer is: almost, but not quite. The actual structure is a **two-dimensional interaction** between two variables, with a third variable modulating the boundary.

**Variable 1: Environmental autocorrelation at the system's relevant timescale.** This is the strongest single predictor. When the past reliably predicts the future — stable pathogen landscapes, stationary data distributions, continuous high-rate production, unchanging ecological niches — remembering is adaptive. When the environment changes faster than the system's update cycle — rapidly evolving pathogens, concept drift, discontinuous technological disruption, volatile climates — forgetting is adaptive. This variable correctly predicts the default strategy in perhaps 70–80% of cases across all six domains.

**Variable 2: The intrinsic dissipation rate of the storage substrate.** Different physical media have fundamentally different persistence properties. Histone modifications are inherently transient; CG DNA methylation with self-reinforcing siRNA loops is inherently stable. Synaptic weights are continuously eroded by active molecular mechanisms; cortically consolidated traces are more durable. Tacit knowledge in employees dissipates with turnover; knowledge embedded in organizational routines and tools persists. Oral memory degrades within generations; written and digital records persist indefinitely. **The storage substrate determines the structural default independent of whether that default is adaptive.**

The boundary condition that separates the two regimes is the **interaction between these two variables**:

* When the storage substrate naturally dissipates AND the environment is volatile → **forgetting is both the default and adaptive** (e.g., invertebrate immune priming, histone-based plant memory, employee tacit knowledge in rapidly changing industries, oral traditions in mobile cultures)  
* When the storage substrate naturally persists AND the environment is stable → **remembering is both the default and adaptive** (e.g., vertebrate immune memory against stable pathogens, CG methylation at TEs, organizational routines in continuous manufacturing, canonical cultural texts in stable societies)  
* When the storage substrate persists BUT the environment has changed → **remembering is the default but maladaptive** (e.g., original antigenic sin, paramutation persisting into changed environments, core rigidities and competency traps, maladaptive trained immunity driving atherosclerosis, Balkan collective memories fueling modern conflict, CRISPR spacers targeting integrated prophages)  
* When the storage substrate dissipates BUT the environment is stable → **forgetting is the default but maladaptive** (e.g., NASA's loss of Apollo knowledge, nuclear industry knowledge loss from retirements, infantile amnesia preventing retention of useful early experiences)

**The most pathological cases — and the most analytically revealing — occupy the off-diagonal cells** where substrate persistence and environmental autocorrelation are mismatched. Original antigenic sin, core rigidities, and the Kosovo myth all represent systems trapped in the "persistent substrate, changed environment" cell. NASA's knowledge loss and the nuclear industry's retirement crisis represent the "dissipative substrate, stable environment" cell. These are not edge cases; they are where real-world harm concentrates.

**Variable 3 (modulating): The cost of maintenance relative to re-acquisition.** Even when the environment is stable and remembering would be adaptive, systems default to forgetting if the cost of maintaining memory exceeds the cost of re-learning. CRISPR arrays face crRNA dilution costs; plants face fitness costs of maintaining primed states in disease-free years (van Hulten et al., 2006); organizations face knowledge management costs that may exceed the cost of rediscovering lost procedures. This variable explains why some systems forget even when they "shouldn't" — the energetic or opportunity cost of maintaining memory exceeds its expected value.

### **Why no single variable suffices**

Several candidates fail as sole boundary conditions:

**Environmental autocorrelation alone** cannot explain paramutation (irreversible remembering regardless of environment), Aboriginal oral traditions (persisting through massive environmental change), or digital memory (remembering everything regardless of relevance). These cases are governed by substrate properties, not environmental statistics.

**Storage substrate alone** cannot explain why the same substrate (DNA methylation) defaults to forgetting at euchromatic loci but remembering at heterochromatic loci in plants, or why the same immune architecture (vertebrate adaptive) produces both lifelong protective immunity and harmful original antigenic sin. These cases require the environmental variable.

**Cost alone** cannot explain why organizations retain dysfunctional routines at great expense (competency traps) or why societies maintain traumatic memories that cause demonstrable harm (Balkans, intergenerational trauma). Path dependence and identity stakes override cost-benefit calculations.

**The individual vs. collective distinction** initially seems promising — individual agents (neurons, immune cells) might default to forgetting while collectives (organizations, cultures) might default to remembering. But this fails empirically: individual vertebrate immune cells remember for decades, while entire organizations lose critical knowledge within years. The substrate matters more than the level of organization.

### **The deepest pattern: remembering is the thermodynamically expensive state**

Across every domain, **maintaining memory requires continuous energy expenditure against entropic forces that favor erasure**. Long-lived plasma cells secrete \~10³ antibodies per second from specialized bone marrow niches requiring constant survival signals. Plants must actively maintain DNA methylation through MET1-dependent copying at every cell division. Neural memory requires ongoing protection against Rac1-dependent cytoskeletal remodeling, AMPA receptor endocytosis, synaptic homeostasis during sleep, and neurogenesis-driven circuit remodeling. Organizational knowledge requires continuous practice (Benkard's depreciation) and knowledge management systems. Cultural memory requires institutions — libraries, archives, schools, ritual specialists — funded and maintained across generations.

**Forgetting is what happens when you stop paying the maintenance cost.** Remembering is what happens when you build infrastructure — molecular, cellular, institutional, technological — that actively counteracts dissipation. The question "when is forgetting the default?" is therefore equivalent to asking "when has the system not invested in memory infrastructure?" — and the answer to that depends on whether the expected value of the memory exceeds its maintenance cost, which depends jointly on environmental autocorrelation and the physical properties of the available storage substrates.

### **Five anomalies that most sharply test the framework**

* **Paramutation in maize** (remembering despite no obvious environmental pressure): The self-reinforcing siRNA loop creates a bistable switch with such a deep energy well that normal resetting mechanisms cannot overcome it. This is a case where substrate dynamics alone determine the outcome, regardless of environmental statistics. It is the biological equivalent of a digital bit — once flipped, it stays flipped.

* **Bats vs. ground squirrels in hibernation** (different forgetting outcomes despite similar physiology): Both lose 50–65% of synapses during torpor, yet bats retain spatial memories and ground squirrels do not. The most likely explanation is that bat longevity and complex navigational demands placed stronger selection pressure on neuroprotective mechanisms during torpor — an evolutionary response to the mismatch between substrate dissipation (synapse loss) and environmental demand (stable spatial environment).

* **Aboriginal Australian oral traditions** (10,000-year persistence in the most dissipative medium): Institutional infrastructure — specialized keepers, accountability systems, landscape anchoring — can override the intrinsic dissipation rate of the substrate. This demonstrates that the "effective dissipation rate" of a storage medium depends not just on its physical properties but on the institutional scaffolding around it.

* **NASA's loss of moon-landing capability** (forgetting in a stable, high-value domain): The environment hadn't changed — the laws of physics were the same. But the human substrate naturally dissipates (retirement, death), and the institutional substrate (knowledge management systems) was insufficient. This is a pure case of substrate-driven forgetting in a domain where the environmental signal was clearly "remember."

* **Syt3 knockout mice that cannot forget** (and are worse off for it): The inability to forget produces not superior cognition but behavioral inflexibility, supporting the view that forgetting is not a failure of memory but a feature of intelligent systems. Similarly, individuals with Highly Superior Autobiographical Memory (HSAM) report their condition as burdensome. **Perfectly retentive systems are not optimal systems.**

## **Conclusion: the actual structure of the boundary**

There is no single clean variable that separates forgetting-default systems from remembering-default systems. The boundary is a **surface in a low-dimensional space** defined primarily by two variables — environmental autocorrelation and storage substrate dissipation rate — with maintenance cost as a modulator. The interaction between these variables matters more than either alone, and the most important real-world phenomena (maladaptive memory persistence, catastrophic knowledge loss, beneficial forgetting) occur precisely where the variables are mismatched.

The deepest insight from this cross-domain survey is that **optimal information-processing systems do not choose between forgetting and remembering — they implement both simultaneously at different timescales and for different types of information**. The brain maintains fast-forgetting hippocampal circuits alongside slow-forgetting cortical traces. The immune system maintains long-lived plasma cells for stable pathogens while rapidly turning over CRISPR spacers for evolving threats. Organizations embed critical knowledge in routines and tools (persistent substrate) while allowing individual expertise to turn over naturally (dissipative substrate). Cultures maintain canons of actively circulated core texts while archiving the rest in institutions that make later retrieval possible but not automatic.

**The truly adaptive systems are not those that have solved the forgetting-remembering trade-off, but those that have built architectures allowing them to operate in both regimes simultaneously** — what McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly (1995) formalized as complementary learning systems, and what Aleida Assmann described as the dynamic interplay between canon and archive. The boundary between the two regimes is not a wall but a membrane, and the systems that thrive are those whose membranes are most selectively permeable.
