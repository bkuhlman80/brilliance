# Allostasis without brains: where the evidence lands

\*\*A slime mold and a plant’s stomata come closest to meeting all three criteria for functional allostasis — predictive, flexible, and set-point-adjusting metabolic regulation — while bacteria, fungi, and most plant priming phenomena fall short in revealing ways.\*\* Across six phenomenon categories and eleven primary papers, the evidence shows that non-neural organisms occupy a spectrum from genetically hardwired anticipation (bacteria) through enhanced reactive capacity (most plant priming) to what appears to be genuine experience-dependent predictive set-point adjustment (Physarum periodic anticipation, Arabidopsis guard cell memory). The critical finding is that the three allostasis criteria dissociate: predictiveness is common, flexibility is rarer, and true set-point adjustment at baseline is rarest of all. No researcher has yet published a paper explicitly arguing that “allostasis” as a term should be extended to non-neural organisms, creating a terminological gap between Sterling’s brain-centered definition and accumulating evidence of functionally equivalent processes described under different names.

\-----

\#\# Defining the bar: what the three criteria actually demand

Sterling and Schulkin (2019, \*Trends in Neurosciences\*) define allostasis as “brain-centered, predictive regulation” requiring a hypothalamus-centered clock, dopaminergic reward prediction, and hierarchical neural integration.  This definition explicitly excludes non-neural life. Friston’s Free Energy Principle (2012, \*Entropy\*) offers the opposing pole: all self-organizing systems with Markov blankets minimize variational free energy, implying predictive regulation is universal. Calvo and Friston (2017, \*Journal of the Royal Society Interface\*) went furthest, proposing a “Bayesian plant hypothesis” where plants perform active inference through vascular and plasmodesmatal signaling. 

The three criteria used here — \*\*predictive\*\*, \*\*flexible\*\*, \*\*set-point-adjusting\*\* — are deliberately demanding. Circadian anticipation of sunrise is predictive but not flexible (it is genetically hardwired). Faster response to a second drought is flexible but not predictive (the plant still waits for stress to begin). Only when an organism runs a different metabolic budget at baseline, updated by its own experience, before the predicted need arrives, do all three converge.

\#\# Category A: plant stress priming mostly enhances reactive capacity

\#\#\# Paper 1 — Ding, Fromm & Avramova (2012). “Multiple exposures to drought ‘train’ transcriptional responses in Arabidopsis.” \*Nature Communications\* 3: 740\.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*Arabidopsis thaliana\* (Plantae). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* Repeated 2-hour dehydration cycles produce “trainable” genes (RD29B, RAB18) that show progressively higher transcript levels with each stress cycle. Between stresses, transcript levels return to baseline, but two epigenetic marks persist: \*\*elevated H3K4me3 and stalled Ser5P RNA Polymerase II\*\* at trainable loci.  These marks last \~5 days,  enabling 3-fold faster transcriptional reactivation. 

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*No.\*\* The plant pre-positions transcriptional machinery but does not produce protective gene products at baseline. This is keeping a loaded fire extinguisher, not adjusting room temperature. Flexible — \*\*Yes.\*\* Memory marks are experience-dependent and decay over 5–7 days.  Set-point-adjusting — \*\*No.\*\* ABA levels, transcript levels, and photosynthetic parameters all return to pre-stress values between episodes.  The defended metabolic state is unchanged.

\*\*Mechanism:\*\* Stalled Pol II \+ retained H3K4me3 at gene loci; independent of ABA.  \*\*Metabolic cost:\*\* Not directly measured; chromatin maintenance is energetically cheap. \*\*Maladaptive overshoot:\*\* Not observed. \*\*Temporal scale:\*\* \~5 days somatic.

\*\*Assessment: HOMEOSTATIC+\*\* — enhanced reactive capacity, not predictive set-point regulation.

\#\#\# Paper 2 — Virlouvet & Fromm (2015). “Physiological and transcriptional memory in guard cells during repetitive dehydration stress.” \*New Phytologist\* 205: 596–607.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*Arabidopsis thaliana\* (Plantae). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* Previously drought-stressed plants maintain \*\*partially closed stomata during watered recovery periods\*\* — a genuine change in a physiological operating point between stress events.   Guard cells specifically upregulate ABA biosynthesis genes (NCED3, AAO3) at rest, maintaining elevated ABA that keeps stomata conservatively closed before any new drought arrives. 

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*Yes.\*\* The plant proactively conserves water before the next dehydration event occurs,  running a more conservative water economy at baseline. Flexible — \*\*Yes.\*\* Only previously stressed plants show the response; it decays over hours to days. Set-point-adjusting — \*\*Yes.\*\* The defended value of stomatal conductance is shifted to a lower, more conservative set point between stresses. This is not faster response upon re-exposure; it is a different operating point.

\*\*Mechanism:\*\* Elevated NCED3/AAO3 expression in guard cells → higher ABA → partially closed stomata.  Signaled via SnRK2.2/SnRK2.3 (distinct from basal stomatal control via SnRK2.6).  \*\*Metabolic cost:\*\* \*\*Yes.\*\* Partially closed stomata reduce CO₂ uptake and photosynthesis — a direct growth penalty. The plant trades photosynthetic capacity for water security. \*\*Maladaptive overshoot:\*\* Not directly tested, but the growth cost implies maladaptation if drought does not recur. \*\*Temporal scale:\*\* Hours to \~5 days.

\*\*Assessment: ALLOSTATIC\*\* — meets all three criteria. The strongest candidate in the plant abiotic stress literature. However, Takahashi and Kinoshita (2015, \*New Phytologist\*) explicitly describe this as “stomatal hysteresis,”  raising the question of whether this is purposive prediction or mechanical persistence. The distinction may be less meaningful than it appears: Sterling’s own allostatic regulation uses recent history as a heuristic for near-future conditions, which is precisely what the stomatal system does.

\#\#\# Paper 3 — Zuther et al. (2019). “Molecular signatures associated with increased freezing tolerance due to low temperature memory in Arabidopsis.” \*Plant, Cell & Environment\* 42: 854–873.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*Arabidopsis thaliana\*, accessions Col-0 and N14 (Plantae). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* Cold priming (3 days at 4°C) followed by a 7-day lag phase at 20°C enhances subsequent freezing tolerance.  Critically, the response is \*\*accession-specific\*\*: in N14, cold-induced cryoprotectants (raffinose, galactinol) remain elevated during the warm lag phase, representing a shifted metabolic baseline. In Col-0, metabolites return to near-baseline but are more strongly re-accumulated upon triggering. 

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*Partial\*\* (accession-dependent). N14 maintains elevated cryoprotectants proactively; Col-0 relies on enhanced reactive capacity. Flexible — \*\*Yes.\*\* Set-point-adjusting — \*\*Partial.\*\* N14 shows a genuinely altered metabolic baseline; Col-0 does not. The follow-up by Bittner et al. (2021, \*Journal of Experimental Botany\*) found that brief cold actually \*\*dampens\*\* gene regulation upon re-exposure — a protective mechanism against cost-intensive metabolic shifts from inappropriate priming.   This is evidence of active regulation against allostatic overshoot.

\*\*Mechanism:\*\* Compatible solute maintenance (N14) or chromatin-level memory (Col-0). \*\*Metabolic cost:\*\* Implied by sugar diversion from growth to cryoprotection. \*\*Maladaptive overshoot:\*\* The dampening phenomenon (Bittner et al. 2021\) shows plants actively regulate against it.  \*\*Temporal scale:\*\* Hours (seedlings) to \~7 days (mature plants). 

\*\*Assessment: PROTO-ALLOSTATIC\*\* (N14 accession); \*\*HOMEOSTATIC+\*\* (Col-0).

\#\#\# Maladaptive priming: evidence for allostatic overload analogues

Crisp et al. (2016, \*Science Advances\*) argue that “resetting and forgetfulness are likely the overriding strategies used by plants to maximize growth under favorable conditions”  — suggesting stress memory is the exception, not the rule.  A 2025 petunia study found that \*\*27 generations of chronic drought produced transgenerational fitness decline, not improvement\*\*: progressive deterioration through accumulated epigenetic stress memory.  This is the closest plant analogue to chronic allostatic overload — maintained stress memory becoming pathological when the organism cannot reset.

\#\# Category B: vernalization quantitatively calibrates a developmental set point

\#\#\# Paper 4 — Angel et al. (2011). “A Polycomb-based switch underlying quantitative epigenetic memory.” \*Nature\* 476: 105–108; supplemented by Shindo et al. (2006). \*Genes & Development\* 20: 3079–3083.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*Arabidopsis thaliana\* (Plantae). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* The FLC flowering repressor is silenced by Polycomb-deposited H3K27me3 marks during cold exposure.   Angel et al. showed the mechanism is \*\*digital at the single-cell level\*\* (each FLC locus is fully ON or fully OFF) but \*\*quantitative at the population level\*\* — the fraction of cells switching to the silenced state scales with cold duration.   Shindo et al. demonstrated \*\*hysteresis\*\*: partially vernalized plants resist returning to their previous FLC expression level, and the stability of this resistance is proportional to cold duration.

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*Yes.\*\* Cold exposure during winter sets the FLC expression level that will govern flowering competence months later in spring. The memory is formed prospectively. Flexible — \*\*Yes.\*\* Both cold duration and temperature profile are quantitatively integrated, calibrating FLC silencing to the individual plant’s actual winter experience. Duncan et al. (2015, \*eLife\*) showed accession-specific temperature optima — Lov-1 (Northern Sweden) vernalizes optimally at 8°C, not the canonical 5°C.   Set-point-adjusting — \*\*Yes.\*\* FLC expression is the defended value determining flowering competence, and it is persistently adjusted by cold history.

\*\*Mechanism:\*\* PRC2 deposits H3K27me3 at a nucleation region during cold; probability of switching scales with cold duration; H3K27me3 spreads across the locus in warm via positive feedback.   \*\*Metabolic cost:\*\* PRC2 maintenance through cell divisions. Lov-1’s unstable silencing means this accession requires longer cold for stable vernalization  — a cost in developmental time. \*\*Maladaptive overshoot:\*\* Under climate warming, high-latitude accession vernalization timing could become mismatched. \*\*Temporal scale:\*\* Integration over 4–12 weeks; memory persists months; reset at meiosis.

\*\*Assessment: PROTO-ALLOSTATIC.\*\* Vernalization is predictive, flexible, and set-point-adjusting — but it functions as a \*\*one-way developmental ratchet\*\* rather than a continuously adjustable regulator.   Once cells switch, they do not switch back in normal development.  The strongest allostasis parallel is in partial vernalization, where the system shows graded hysteresis. It is better described as a quantitatively calibrated developmental memory than as ongoing allostatic regulation.

\#\# Category C: root-shoot allocation shows inertia, not prediction

\#\#\# Paper 5 — Gedroc, McConnaughay & Coleman (1996). “Plasticity in root/shoot partitioning: optimal, ontogenetic, or both?” \*Functional Ecology\* 10: 44–50.

\*\*Organism:\*\* Annual plant species (Plantae). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* Plants transferred from low-nutrient to high-nutrient conditions \*\*maintained elevated root allocation without adjustment\*\*, even as growth increased.  This “allocation inertia” means the defended root:shoot ratio persisted beyond the conditions that created it.

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*No.\*\* The persistent allocation appears to reflect ontogenetic constraint, not anticipation of future nutrient scarcity. Flexible — \*\*Partial.\*\* Allocation responds to nutrients during early development but becomes fixed. Set-point-adjusting — \*\*Yes, with caveats.\*\* The ratio persists from prior experience. But Poorter and Nagel (2000, \*Functional Plant Biology\*) showed plants can also rapidly restore allocation to control levels,  suggesting the “set point” is continuously recomputed from current conditions in some contexts. 

Robinson (2023, \*Plant, Cell & Environment\*) challenges the entire framework, arguing root:shoot ratios are \*\*emergent properties of local resource capture\*\*, not regulated variables at all.  Under his Local Allocation Model, apparent “allocation inertia” reflects structural persistence of existing root biomass, not active memory. 

\*\*Mechanism:\*\* Ontogenetic developmental constraints; no identified epigenetic memory mechanism.   \*\*Metabolic cost:\*\* Yes — over-investment in roots after nutrient restoration. \*\*Temporal scale:\*\* Weeks.

\*\*Assessment: HOMEOSTATIC+\*\* — structural inertia rather than predictive memory. The most allostasis-relevant finding (persistent elevated root allocation) lacks a molecular memory mechanism and appears driven by developmental commitment rather than information storage.

\#\# Category D: mycorrhizal networks redistribute reactively

\#\#\# Paper 6 — Whiteside et al. (2019). “Mycorrhizal Fungi Respond to Resource Inequality by Moving Phosphorus from Rich to Poor Patches across Networks.” \*Current Biology\* 29: 2043–2050.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*Rhizophagus irregularis\* (Fungi) with \*Daucus carota\* roots. \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* Using quantum-dot-tagged phosphorus, the authors tracked AM fungi physically moving phosphorus from rich to poor patches, capitalizing on value differences to maximize carbon returns from host plants. Higher resource inequality stimulated greater trade. 

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*No.\*\* The fungus responds to current source-sink gradients. Flexible — \*\*Partial.\*\* Trading strategy adjusts to current inequality, but no evidence of learning from past distributions. Set-point-adjusting — \*\*No.\*\* Baseline allocation does not change based on history.

\*\*Assessment: HOMEOSTATIC+\*\* — sophisticated real-time market-based resource redistribution, but entirely reactive. Song et al. (2015, \*Scientific Reports\*) showed defense signaling through ectomycorrhizal networks can prime connected plants before they are attacked,  but this is signal relay, not network-level memory. \*\*No evidence of anticipatory regulation exists at the mycorrhizal network level.\*\* This is a genuine empty cell in the evidence matrix.

\#\# Category E: Physarum delivers the strongest case across all kingdoms

\#\#\# Paper 7 — Saigusa, Tero, Nakagaki & Kuramoto (2008). “Amoebae Anticipate Periodic Events.” \*Physical Review Letters\* 100: 018101\.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*Physarum polycephalum\* (Amoebozoa). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* Plasmodia exposed to three consecutive unfavorable pulses (cold/dry air) at constant intervals spontaneously slowed locomotion at the time the next pulse \*\*would have occurred\*\*, even in continuously favorable conditions. The organism anticipated periodic environmental change based on \*\*three training episodes\*\*. The memory decayed if unstimulated but could be \*\*recalled by a single reminder pulse\*\*. Multiple periodicities (30 min and 60 min) could be learned simultaneously.

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*Yes.\*\* The organism adjusts locomotion speed before the predicted adverse event, in a favorable environment where no current cue signals the change. Flexible — \*\*Yes.\*\* The prediction is learned from experience (3 pulses), not hardwired. Different periodicities can be acquired. Memory is recallable after extinction. Set-point-adjusting — \*\*Yes.\*\* The baseline locomotion speed — which determines foraging rate and metabolic expenditure — is temporarily reduced at predicted stress times.

\*\*Mechanism:\*\* Coupled oscillatory biochemical reactions (calcium oscillations, actomyosin contraction cycles, cAMP/NADH oscillators) become entrained to external periodic stimuli, creating period memory in phase relationships.  \*\*Metabolic cost:\*\* \*\*Yes.\*\* Anticipatory speed reduction means the organism foregoes foraging/expansion time during “false alarm” slowdowns. \*\*Maladaptive overshoot:\*\* \*\*Yes.\*\* When the stimulus does not recur, unnecessary slowdowns impose a foraging cost — a false-alarm penalty analogous to allostatic overshoot. \*\*Temporal scale:\*\* Minutes to hours.

\*\*Assessment: ALLOSTATIC.\*\* This is the strongest case for functional allostasis in any non-neural organism. It satisfies all three criteria unambiguously. The organism predicts, the prediction is learned from experience, and the defended locomotor state changes accordingly. Critically, this differs from Physarum’s famous maze-solving (Nakagaki et al. 2000), which is reactive optimization. Saigusa et al. demonstrate \*\*temporal anticipation based on learned periodicity\*\* — a qualitatively different phenomenon.

\#\#\# Paper 8 — Kramar & Alim (2021). “Encoding memory in tube diameter hierarchy of living flow network.” \*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences\* 118: e2007815118.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*Physarum polycephalum\* (Amoebozoa). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* Nutrient stimuli cause local release of a softening agent transported by cytoplasmic flow, dilating tubes toward previous food sources. This creates a \*\*permanent morphological memory\*\* — thick tubes pointing toward prior nutrient locations — that biases future migration decisions. Each new encounter updates the hierarchy.

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*Partial.\*\* The tube hierarchy doesn’t explicitly predict future events but biases allocation toward historically rewarding locations. Flexible — \*\*Yes.\*\* Updated by each new encounter. Set-point-adjusting — \*\*Yes.\*\* Baseline tube diameters (determining flow capacity and resource allocation rates) are permanently altered by past experience.

\*\*Mechanism:\*\* Flow-driven transport of softening agent → local tube dilation → persistent morphological imprint.  \*\*Temporal scale:\*\* 10–45 minutes for encoding; persists hours.

\*\*Assessment: PROTO-ALLOSTATIC\*\* — clear set-point adjustment and flexibility, but the predictive component is indirect (memory-biased allocation rather than temporal anticipation). Combined with Saigusa et al., Physarum possesses at least \*\*two independent memory systems\*\*: temporal anticipation (oscillator-based) and spatial memory (morphological encoding).

\#\# Category F: bacteria predict but cannot learn within a lifetime

\#\#\# Paper 9 — Tagkopoulos, Liu & Tavazoie (2008). “Predictive Behavior Within Microbial Genetic Networks.” \*Science\* 320: 1313–1317.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*Escherichia coli\* K-12 (Bacteria). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* Temperature increase (25→37°C) triggers anticipatory upregulation of anaerobic respiration genes while oxygen levels remain high.  This is maladaptive from a homeostatic perspective but predictive: in the natural ecology, temperature increase signals imminent transition to the oxygen-poor gut.   Transcriptional responses to temperature showed striking correlation with responses to oxygen downshift  (\*\*Pearson r \= \+0.39, p \< 10⁻⁷⁹\*\*). 

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*Yes.\*\* Metabolic state adjusts before the environmental change occurs. Flexible — \*\*No within a lifetime.\*\* When E. coli was evolved under reversed conditions (high temperature \+ high oxygen), the anticipatory coupling was decoupled  — but this required \*\*\~100 generations of genetic evolution\*\*, not within-lifetime learning. Set-point-adjusting — \*\*No in the allostatic sense.\*\* The regulatory program is genetically hardwired.

\*\*Mechanism:\*\* Transcriptional regulatory network wiring — temperature-responsive regulators connect to anaerobic metabolism genes, reflecting evolved environmental correlations.  \*\*Metabolic cost:\*\* \*\*Yes.\*\* Premature anaerobic gene expression while oxygen is still abundant represents a measurable fitness penalty — the “price of prediction.”  \*\*Temporal scale:\*\* Gene expression changes within \~40 minutes; evolutionary modification requires \~100 generations.

\*\*Assessment: PROTO-ALLOSTATIC\*\* — predictive but genetically hardwired. This is an evolved reflex analogous to pupillary constriction, not a learned anticipation like Pavlovian conditioning.

\#\#\# Paper 10 — Mitchell et al. (2009). “Adaptive prediction of environmental changes by microorganisms.” \*Nature\* 460: 220–224.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*E. coli\* and \*Saccharomyces cerevisiae\* (Bacteria/Fungi). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* Lactose exposure induces partial expression (\~37%) of maltose operon genes before maltose is encountered, reflecting the natural gut sequence (lactose precedes maltose). The anticipatory coupling is asymmetric — lactose induces maltose genes but not vice versa.  Pre-exposure to lactose confers a measurable fitness advantage on subsequent maltose encounter. 

\*\*Allostasis check:\*\* Predictive — \*\*Yes.\*\* Flexible — \*\*No.\*\* The program is genetically fixed; it was lost through hundreds of generations of evolution in laboratory strains exposed only to lactose. Set-point-adjusting — \*\*No.\*\* The \~37% induction level is a genetically encoded regulatory parameter.

\*\*Mechanism:\*\* Regulatory connections between the lactose and maltose operons hardwired by natural selection.  Mitchell and Pilpel (2011, \*PNAS\*) modeled the optimal “two-phase conditioning strategy” (partial anticipatory \+ full direct induction), matching the observed intermediate induction level.   \*\*Metabolic cost:\*\* \*\*Yes, explicitly measured.\*\* Maltose gene expression during lactose growth imposes a growth rate penalty.  \*\*Temporal scale:\*\* Gene expression within \~3 hours; evolutionary loss over hundreds of generations.

\*\*Assessment: PROTO-ALLOSTATIC.\*\*

\#\#\# Paper 11 — Mahilkar et al. (2022). “Experimental Evolution of Anticipatory Regulation in \*Escherichia coli\*.” \*Frontiers in Microbiology\* 12: 796228\.

\*\*Organism:\*\* \*E. coli\* K-12 MG1655 (Bacteria). \*\*Phenomenon:\*\* E. coli evolved under alternating rhamnose/paraquat exposure for \~850 generations developed \*\*de novo anticipatory regulation\*\*: rhamnose now pre-activated oxidative stress defense (soxS) before paraquat exposure. This capacity was absent in the ancestor and control lines. Three replicate lines achieved this via \*\*different mutations in global regulators\*\* (cyaA, rpoS, 16S rRNA).

This paper provides the strongest evidence that anticipatory regulation can be created de novo — but it required \*\*\~850 generations of genetic evolution through mutation and selection\*\*, not within-lifetime adjustment. No individual bacterial cell learned anything.

\*\*Assessment: PROTO-ALLOSTATIC\*\* — confirms that bacterial anticipation is an evolutionary adaptation, not experiential learning.

\#\# Who is doing this work and what they believe

\*\*Plant stress memory.\*\* Isabel Bäurle (University of Potsdam) leads research on chromatin-based stress memory,  having discovered FORGETTER1 — a nucleosome remodeling gene essential for heat stress memory.  She does not use allostasis terminology but describes mechanisms functionally analogous to predictive regulation. Monika Hilker (Freie Universität Berlin) co-authored the landmark review “Priming and memory of stress responses in organisms lacking a nervous system” (2016, \*Biological Reviews\*) with Bäurle and 15 other authors, explicitly framing stress memory as information storage without neural substrates.  Michael Fromm and colleagues (University of Nebraska / Freie Universität Berlin) produced the guard cell memory work most relevant to allostasis.

\*\*Bacterial anticipatory regulation.\*\* Saeed Tavazoie (Columbia University) and Ilias Tagkopoulos (UC Davis) pioneered the empirical demonstration of predictive behavior in microbial genetic networks. Freddolino and Tavazoie (2012, \*Annual Review of Cell and Developmental Biology\*) explicitly argued that homeostasis is inadequate for understanding cellular behavior and proposed a “predictive-dynamic framework.”  This is the closest any microbiologist has come to arguing for extending allostasis-like concepts to non-neural organisms, though they do not use the word “allostasis.”

\*\*Physarum learning and memory.\*\* Audrey Dussutour (CNRS, Toulouse) leads research on slime mold habituation and memory transfer,  including the discovery that memory can persist through dormancy and be transferred via cell fusion. Toshiyuki Nakagaki (Hokkaido University) co-authored the periodic anticipation study.  Karen Alim (Technical University of Munich) studies information encoding in Physarum’s flow networks.  

\*\*Theoretical bridges.\*\* Paco Calvo (University of Murcia) collaborates with Karl Friston (UCL) on applying the Free Energy Principle to plants. Their 2017 paper proposing a “Bayesian plant hypothesis” is the most explicit attempt to apply predictive regulation frameworks to non-neural organisms.   Michael Levin (Tufts University) argues through his TAME (Technological Approach to Mind Everywhere) framework that cognitive capacities, including predictive regulation, scale continuously from single cells to organisms through bioelectric networks. 

\*\*No one has published an explicit argument against extending allostasis to non-neural organisms\*\* — likely because Sterling’s definition is so explicitly neural that researchers working on non-neural predictive regulation simply use different terminology. Different communities have developed parallel vocabularies: bacteriologists say “anticipatory regulation,” plant scientists say “stress priming,” and theoretical biologists say “active inference.” The phenomenon each describes is functionally similar, but none claims the allostasis label.

\#\# What the evidence means and what remains uncertain

Two findings stand above the rest. \*\*Physarum’s periodic anticipation\*\* (Saigusa et al. 2008\) is the cleanest demonstration of allostasis-like regulation in any brainless organism: learned temporal prediction, updated by experience, altering a defended physiological state,  with measurable false-alarm costs. The mechanism — entrainment of coupled biochemical oscillators — is entirely non-neural. \*\*Arabidopsis guard cell memory\*\* (Virlouvet & Fromm 2015\) provides the strongest plant case: experience-dependent adjustment of a defended physiological parameter (stomatal conductance) at baseline, between stress events,  at a metabolic cost. Together, these two cases demonstrate that the functional architecture of allostasis — predict, adjust, pay the cost — can be instantiated without neurons.

The bacterial evidence is revealing in a different way. Tagkopoulos et al. and Mitchell et al. show that \*\*prediction without flexibility is the norm\*\* in prokaryotes. Bacteria exploit reliable environmental statistical structure through evolved genetic wiring, not through within-lifetime learning.  Mahilkar et al.‘s experimental evolution of de novo anticipatory regulation confirms this: creating new anticipatory associations requires hundreds of generations of mutation and selection.  The “Pavlovian conditioning” analogy invoked by these authors is misleading — Pavlovian conditioning occurs within an individual’s lifetime, while bacterial “conditioning” occurs phylogenetically.  This makes bacterial anticipation functionally analogous to a genetically programmed reflex, not to allostatic regulation.

Vernalization occupies an interesting middle ground. It is predictive, quantitatively calibrated by individual experience, and set-point-adjusting — formally meeting all three criteria. But its one-way ratchet character and developmental-switch function distinguish it from ongoing metabolic regulation.   The deepest insight may be that \*\*vernalization represents what allostasis looks like when implemented by Polycomb chromatin machinery rather than by neural circuits\*\*: a quantitative, history-dependent, hysteretic adjustment of a defended developmental variable,  with the same core logic implemented by radically different substrate. 

Several categories produced genuinely null results worth reporting. \*\*Mycorrhizal networks show no evidence of anticipatory regulation\*\* — their sophisticated resource redistribution is entirely reactive. \*\*Root-shoot allocation\*\* shows structural inertia but no molecular memory mechanism and no evidence of prediction. The strongest argument against over-interpreting these phenomena comes from Crisp et al. (2016), who argue that \*\*active forgetting — resetting stress memory to maximize growth — is the default plant strategy\*\*, with memory being the costly exception maintained only when environmental autocorrelation makes prediction worthwhile. 

The theoretical landscape reveals a clear gap. Sterling’s allostasis and Friston’s Free Energy Principle occupy opposite ends of a continuum: one restricts predictive regulation to brains, the other universalizes it to all self-organizing systems. The empirical evidence falls between these poles. Functional allostasis exists in brainless organisms, but it is \*\*rarer, shorter-lived, and more constrained\*\* than neural allostasis. The theoretical move needed — and not yet made — is to articulate allostasis as a \*\*substrate-independent computational principle\*\* that can be implemented by neural circuits, Polycomb machinery, oscillatory biochemistry, or gene regulatory networks, with each substrate imposing different constraints on the scope, duration, and flexibility of prediction. Physarum’s coupled oscillators and Arabidopsis’s guard cell ABA circuit are proof-of-concept implementations. Whether this generalization is worth the terminological risk of stretching “allostasis” beyond Sterling’s definition, or whether it is better captured by Friston’s existing vocabulary of active inference, remains an open question that the empirical evidence alone cannot resolve.
