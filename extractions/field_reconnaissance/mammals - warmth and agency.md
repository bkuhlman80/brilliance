# Joint Measurement of Stress-Reactivity/Temperament and Affiliative/Attachment Domains in Non-Human Mammals: A Reconnaissance

## Scope and Framing

The question asked is whether, across mammalian individuals, the trait dimension capturing autonomic/HPA reactivity, behavioral inhibition, and coping style is correlated with, orthogonal to, or otherwise structurally related to the trait dimension capturing affiliative/attachment behavior toward conspecifics. The literature is large but **fragmented along species lines**, and very few studies have actually estimated the cross-domain individual-difference covariance at trait level on the same animals. Most reports either (a) measure both domains in the same individuals but pool by group or treatment (so the cross-domain trait correlation is not reported), or (b) use one domain as the manipulation/predictor and the other as outcome (so what we have is a between-group difference, not a within-population covariance). This reconnaissance maps that evidence and flags the gaps as findings.

Evidentiary tiers used throughout: **[ES] empirically settled**, **[PI] plausibly inferred**, **[GC] genuinely contested**, **[TU] thin or untested**.

## 1. Microtine Rodents (Voles)

The prairie vole literature is the deepest mammalian body of joint-measurement work on this question, but its dominant designs are *between-species* comparisons (prairie vs. meadow/montane) and *manipulation-of-receptor* studies, not population-level trait-trait correlations.

### Pitkow, Sharer, Ren, Insel, Terwilliger, & Young (2001). *Journal of Neuroscience*, 21, 7392–7396.
Design: AAV-mediated V1aR overexpression in the ventral pallidum of male prairie voles (n ≈ 20 per group). Domain 1: elevated plus maze anxiety. Domain 2: partner preference / affiliative behavior. **Observed relationship**: V1aR upregulation simultaneously increased *both* affiliation/pair-bond facilitation *and* anxiety-like behavior, the single clearest demonstration that one molecular variable yokes the two domains in the same direction. **Caveat**: experimental manipulation, not natural individual-difference covariation. [ES] for the manipulation effect; [PI] for the inference that natural V1aR variation does the same.

### Smith, Tabbaa, Lei, Eastham, Butler, Linton, Altshuler, Liu, & Wang (2016). *Psychoneuroendocrinology*, 63, 50–58 (PMC5807102).
Design: Adult pair-bonded male and female prairie voles, n ≈ 60, randomized to Control, immobilization stress alone, or immobilization with bonded partner present. Domain 1: elevated plus maze, OTR/V1aR binding. Domain 2: pair-bond status (manipulated, not measured individually). **Observed**: Partner presence prevented stress-induced reduction in open-arm time; immobilization decreased NAcc OTR binding and partner presence dampened this. **Caveat**: This is a social-buffering design — group-level demonstration that the two systems are engaged in the same paradigm, not a trait correlation. [ES].

### Lambert, Sangoi, Nadler, Gourineni, Stetzik, Watson, Connelly, & Phelps (2018). “Individual variation in social behaviours of male lab-reared prairie voles is non-heritable and weakly associated with V1aR density.” *Hormones and Behavior*, 95, 35–45 (PMC5780391).
Design: Laboratory-reared male prairie voles (n ≈ 95) phenotyped on partner preference, aggression toward intruders, paternal care, *and* anxiety-like behavior (EPM, open field), with autoradiographic V1aR binding in ventral pallidum (VP) and retrosplenial cortex (RSC). **Observed cross-domain relationship**: substantial individual variation existed within each domain, but **partner preference, paternal care, aggression, and anxiety-like behavior were not significantly correlated with each other**. V1aR density in VP and RSC explained some variance in paternal care and aggression, but **not** in partner preference and not in anxiety. **Caveat**: Lab-only environment, single cohort, single anxiety paradigm, heritability estimates underpowered. This is the single best-designed joint-measurement study in the species, and it reports **largely orthogonal trait structure**. [ES] for the null result within this lab population; [GC] for whether the null generalizes.

### Ophir, Wolff, & Phelps (2008). *PNAS*, 105, 1249–1254 (PMC2234124).
Design: Semi-natural enclosures, male prairie voles (n ≈ 78), space use by radio telemetry, paternity by microsatellites, V1aR autoradiography. **Observed**: Sexual fidelity and space use did **not** covary with V1aR in ventral pallidum or lateral septum (the canonical "pair-bond" sites) but **did** covary with V1aR in posterior cingulate/retrosplenial cortex and laterodorsal thalamus — areas associated with spatial memory. No HPA measures were collected. **Joint-measurement gap**: this seminal paper is *not* a true joint-measurement of our two domains; it correlates mating tactic with V1aR but not with stress reactivity. [ES] for the V1aR-tactic association; [TU] for the cross-domain question proper.

### Ophir, Campbell, Hanna, & Phelps (2008). *Hormones and Behavior*, 54, 694–702.
Design: Field-tested males, avpr1a microsatellite genotype, V1aR autoradiography, paternity. **Observed**: Allele length predicted V1aR expression in pair-bond-relevant nuclei but did **not** predict sexual or social fidelity in the field. Reinforces a recurring theme: receptor density predicts neural phenotype more reliably than it predicts behavior; population-level behavioral consequences are weaker than the manipulation literature suggests. [ES].

### Insel & Shapiro (1992). *PNAS*, 89, 5981–5985.
Domain 2 only. Foundational comparative paper: monogamous prairie and pine voles have higher OTR density in nucleus accumbens and prelimbic cortex than promiscuous montane and meadow voles. **Caveat for the joint question**: species-level (not individual) and affiliation-only (no Domain 1 measure). [ES].

### Bales, Plotsky, Young, Lim, Grotte, Ferrer, & Carter (2007) and related Bales/Carter neonatal manipulation studies. *Neuroscience*, 144, 38–45.
Design: Neonatal oxytocin administration to prairie voles, followed by adult assessment of partner preference *and* anxiety/HPA reactivity. **Observed**: Sex-dependent and dose-dependent effects on both partner preference and HPA reactivity (corticosterone), in the same animals. **Caveat**: This is developmental programming, not a snapshot trait correlation, but it is one of the few designs in which both domains are explicitly co-measured in the same individuals; reported effects are dissociable across sexes — suggesting partial independence even when one peptide system is the lever. [PI] for trait-level interpretation.

**Meadow vole (M. pennsylvanicus)** comparative work (Beery, Routman, & Zucker; Cushing & Wynne-Edwards; Parker & Lee) documents seasonal photoperiod-driven shifts in OTR/V1aR density that track shifts in social tolerance, but **joint individual-difference correlations of anxiety/HPA with affiliation within meadow voles are essentially absent**. [TU] — explicit gap.

**Vole subsection synthesis**: The prairie vole literature establishes that experimentally manipulating V1aR or OTR moves both domains, and that bonded partners buffer stress responses; but at the natural-variation, trait-correlation level (Lambert 2018; Ophir 2008), the two domains appear **largely uncorrelated**, with shared neural substrates that nonetheless do not produce strong cross-domain trait covariation. This is a striking discrepancy between the manipulation literature (often interpreted as proving "shared system") and the individual-differences literature (showing dissociability).

## 2. Koolhaas Coping-Style Tradition (Rats, Mice, Wild Rodents)

### Koolhaas, Korte, de Boer, van der Vegt, van Reenen, Hopster, de Jong, Ruis, & Blokhuis (1999). *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 23, 925–935.
Foundational programmatic statement. Proactive coping = high offensive aggression, low HPA reactivity, high sympathetic tone, behavioral routinization. Reactive coping = low aggression, high HPA and parasympathetic reactivity, behavioral flexibility. Both styles are operationalized largely on *agonistic* behavior toward conspecifics and on *physiological reactivity to stress*; the framework does not natively include affiliative attachment. [ES] for the coping-style construct's stability.

### Koolhaas, de Boer, Coppens, & Buwalda (2010). *Frontiers in Neuroendocrinology*, 31, 307–321.
Review with a key analytic point: the qualitative dimension (proactive–reactive) and the quantitative dimension ("emotionality" / behavioral inhibition) are treated as **orthogonal**, with the quantitative dimension capturing intensity of HPA/autonomic engagement and the qualitative dimension capturing coping strategy. **Implication for the joint question**: within this framework, what affiliation-researchers call "attachment-related anxiety" would load onto the quantitative axis, not the qualitative axis — implying separable systems. [PI].

### Coppens, de Boer, & Koolhaas (2010). *Philosophical Transactions B*, 365, 4021–4028 (PMC2992750).
Review emphasizing that behavioral flexibility is the underlying variable distinguishing the coping styles. Proactive animals show rigid, routine-like behavior; reactive animals are more environmentally responsive. **Affiliative behavior is not a core indicator** in the rodent coping-style framework, although aggression *is*. This is a *theoretical* separation of the coping-style axis from an attachment-style axis. [PI].

### Houslay, Earley, White, Lammers, Grimmer, Travers, Heinsohn, Kasper, & Wilson (2023). "Coping styles vary with species' sociality and life history: A systematic review and meta-regression analysis." *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 152.
Meta-regression across many species. **Key finding for the joint question**: the predicted proactive–reactive correlations (boldness/aggression with low HPA reactivity) are far weaker than the classic Koolhaas description claims, and are moderated by species' sociality and life history. **Methodological caveat highlighted in the meta-analysis**: many primary studies use shared paradigms that engage both behavioral and physiological systems simultaneously (state confound), and use varimax or other orthogonalizing rotations that algorithmically prevent cross-domain factors from emerging. [ES] for weak meta-analytic correlations; [GC] for theoretical reconciliation.

### Sgoifo, Koolhaas, Musso, & De Boer (1999); Carere & Maestripieri (2013) volume.
Establish that proactive males in resident-intruder paradigms show higher sympathetic and lower vagal tone; reactive males show pronounced bradycardia. **Affiliative behavior toward bonded conspecifics is not assayed** in this tradition — a notable gap given that prairie voles, dogs, and primates would call this Domain 2's central paradigm. [TU].

**Subsection synthesis**: The Koolhaas tradition makes a theoretical commitment that coping style and "emotionality" are orthogonal dimensions, mapping our Domain 1 onto the *qualitative* axis (proactive vs reactive) and leaving affiliation/attachment essentially unmeasured. This is one programmatic position: **separable systems by design**.

## 3. Belyaev Silver Fox Experiment

### Trut, Plyusnina, & Oskina (2004). *Russian Journal of Genetics*, 40, 644–655; Trut, Oskina, & Kharlamova (2009). *BioEssays*, 31, 349–360.
Design: ~50-generation selection of silver foxes (Vulpes vulpes) for tameness — operationalized as approach to and tolerance of human contact, equivalent to a Domain 2-toward-humans measure. **Observed**: Selection produced concurrent reductions in basal cortisol and ACTH (3-5x lower by generation 45), smaller adrenal glands, delayed onset of the fear response, and dog-like affiliative behavior. **Methodological note**: This is a between-line difference, not a within-line correlation; cross-domain covariance within tame or within control populations is rarely reported in the available literature. Selection was on behavior toward humans only; the HPA changes are *correlated by-products*. [ES] for the selection-line difference; [GC] for whether tameness-stress coupling is unique to selection or also visible as within-population trait covariation.

### Hare, Plyusnina, Ignacio, Schepina, Stepika, Wrangham, & Trut (2005). *Current Biology*, 15, 226–230.
Domesticated foxes outperformed control foxes on human-cue social-cognition tasks. Provides convergent evidence that selection for one Domain 2 behavior (approach to humans) drags along both stress-physiology and social-cognitive phenotypes. [ES] for the behavioral comparison.

### Gogoleva, Volodin, Volodina, Kharlamova, & Trut (2010). *Behaviour*, 147, 1713–1736.
Acoustic and behavioral evidence that tame foxes show reduced stress vocalizations to human contact. Consistent with HPA-attachment co-selection. [ES].

**Subsection synthesis**: The fox lines establish that **selection on one Domain 2 variable (approach to humans) produces correlated change in Domain 1 (HPA tone) at the population level**. Whether this reflects a genuine shared underlying axis or pleiotropic neural-crest/HPA developmental linkage (Wilkins, Wrangham, & Fitch, 2014) is contested — the "domestication syndrome" framing favors a single underlying axis, while critics (Lord, Larson, Coppinger, & Karlsson, 2020) argue the syndrome is overstated. [GC].

## 4. Rhesus Macaques: Behavioral Inhibition, 5-HTT, and Attachment

### Higley, Suomi, & Linnoila (1991); Higley, King, Hasert, Champoux, Suomi, & Linnoila (1996). *Neuropsychopharmacology*, 14, 67–76.
Design: Free-ranging and captive rhesus, longitudinal CSF 5-HIAA. **Observed**: Low CSF 5-HIAA is trait-like, predicts impulsive/aggressive behavior, *and* predicts poor social competence and disrupted peer/maternal social behavior. Both Domain 1 (HPA, behavioral inhibition) and Domain 2 (social competence, attachment quality) load on serotonergic function — but with *opposite* directional signs in different studies, complicating a clean cross-domain claim. [ES] for the trait stability of 5-HIAA; [GC] for the cross-domain coupling direction.

### Champoux, Bennett, Shannon, Higley, Lesch, & Suomi (2002). *Molecular Psychiatry*, 7, 1058–1063.
Neonatal rhesus phenotyped on Brazelton-like temperament battery (Domain 1 — orienting, irritability, state control) and 5-HTTLPR genotype. Peer-reared *s*-allele carriers showed disrupted neonatal regulation. Adult follow-ups link to insecure peer-attachment patterns. [ES] for genotype × rearing × temperament; cross-domain trait correlation [PI].

### Barr, Newman, Shannon, Parker, Dvoskin, Becker, Schwandt, Champoux, Lesch, Goldman, Suomi, & Higley (2004). *Biological Psychiatry*, 55, 733–738.
Design: Infant rhesus (n ≈ 100), HPA response to separation stressor + 5-HTTLPR + rearing condition. **Observed**: rearing × genotype interaction on cortisol response. Peer-reared *s*-carriers show exaggerated HPA reactivity and disorganized attachment-like behaviors. This is a true joint-measurement design — both domains in the same individuals. [ES].

### Suomi (1997). *British Medical Bulletin*, 53, 170–184; Suomi (2006). *Annals of the NY Academy of Sciences*, 1094, 52–62.
Longitudinal program: ~20% of rhesus infants show a stable "anxious/reactive" temperament from infancy — high startle, high cortisol, behavioral inhibition. These same animals form less stable peer attachments and, as adults, show maladaptive maternal behavior. Suomi explicitly argues for a **temperament–attachment coupling** that is intergenerationally transmitted. [PI for trait covariation; ES for the temperament profile's stability].

### McCormack, Newman, Higley, Maestripieri, & Sanchez (2009). *Hormones and Behavior*, 55, 538–547.
Mother-infant abusive dyads in rhesus: infants show both elevated HPA stress reactivity and altered attachment-style proximity-seeking; the *s*-allele moderates both. Joint measurement in same individuals. [ES].

### Capitanio, Mendoza, Mason, & Maninger (2005). *Developmental Psychobiology*, 46, 318–330.
Compared HPA regulation across rearing conditions in young rhesus; included social separation paradigms. Found that rearing environment shapes HPA regulation in ways that parallel attachment-quality differences. [ES] for the rearing effect; [PI] for the trait-correlation reading.

**Subsection synthesis**: In rhesus, the temperament–attachment coupling is **the dominant empirical pattern**, but it is largely produced by *shared developmental antecedents* (early adversity, 5-HTTLPR × rearing) rather than demonstrated as a within-cohort trait correlation independent of those antecedents. The Suomi program is the strongest mammalian case that the two domains are tightly coupled — but as the Koolhaas tradition would point out, this could be developmental co-shaping rather than a single trait.

## 5. Sheep and Other Ungulates

### Boissy & Dumont (2002); Boissy, Bouissou, Fisher, Hinch, Jensen, Le Neindre, Mormède, Prunet, Roussel, Veissier, & Aubert (2007). *Physiology & Behavior*, 92, 375–397.
Programmatic statement: sheep temperament is multidimensional and contextual. Reactivity to social isolation (a Domain 2 stressor by construction) co-engages both behavioral activation (vocalization, locomotion) and HPA elevation (cortisol). [ES] for state-level co-engagement.

### Hild, Andersen, & Zanella (2010); Destrez, Deiss, Leterrier, Boivin, & Boissy (2013); Viérin & Bouissou (2003).
Joint measures of behavioral reactivity (isolation box, open field, human approach) and cortisol/heart rate in same animals. Reactive ewes show higher cortisol responses *and* stronger proximity-seeking to flock-mates when reunited. [ES] for state co-engagement; [PI] for trait covariation.

### Sébe, Aubin, Boue, & Poindron (2008); Nowak, Porter, Lévy, Orgeur, & Schaal (2000). *Reviews of Reproduction*, 5, 153–163.
Ewe-lamb attachment is olfactory and rapidly forming; high-cortisol-reactive ewes show altered maternal selectivity. [ES].

### Dwyer (2008). *Animal*, 2, 1162–1175; Doyle, Broster, Barnes, & Browne (2016). *Behavioural Processes*, 131, 53–58.
Temperament classifications (calm vs nervous) predict not only handling-stress reactivity but social-network position in the flock. Nervous ewes occupy more central positions and show stronger proximity-seeking. **Suggests a coupling, not orthogonality**. [PI].

### Doyle et al. (2022). *Applied Animal Behaviour Science* (PMID 35872160).
Multi-trait analysis of sheep temperament tests; finds that **the isolation-box test confounds activity and fearfulness**, and that what is often called "temperament" in sheep is actually a mixture of social-motivation and fear traits. **A direct methodological warning** that single-paradigm measurement creates the appearance of cross-domain correlation. [ES] for the construct-overlap concern.

**Subsection synthesis**: Sheep work documents strong state-level co-engagement of HPA and affiliative systems in isolation paradigms but is methodologically vulnerable to the construct-overlap problem — what looks like Domain 1 ↔ Domain 2 covariation is partly an artifact of the isolation-box paradigm engaging both systems by design. Trait-trait separation has not been clearly demonstrated.

## 6. Dogs (and Wolves)

### Topál, Miklósi, Csányi, & Dóka (1998). *Journal of Comparative Psychology*, 112, 219–229.
First adaptation of Ainsworth's Strange Situation to dog-owner dyads (n = 51). Established that dogs show attachment-classifiable behavior (proximity-seeking, separation distress, reunion behavior, secure-base use). No HPA measure. [ES].

### Topál, Gácsi, Miklósi, Virányi, Kubinyi, & Csányi (2005). *Animal Behaviour*, 70, 1367–1375.
Compared hand-reared wolves to dogs in the Strange Situation; wolves did not differentiate familiar caregiver from stranger at 16 weeks. Domain 2 only. [ES].

### Gácsi, Maros, Sernkvist, Faragó, & Miklósi (2013). *PLoS ONE*, 8, e58475.
Behavioral and heart-rate responses to stressful social stimuli with vs. without owner. Owner presence reduced HR responses — explicit social-buffering demonstration with autonomic Domain 1 measure. [ES] for state-level effect.

### Schöberl, Beetz, Solomon, Wedl, Gee, & Kotrschal (2016). *Hormones and Behavior*, 84, 102–112 (PMC reference in search results).
Dog-owner dyads tested in dog-adapted Strange Situation with salivary cortisol sampling. **Observed**: Securely-attached dogs showed lower baseline-to-SSP cortisol secretion. Insecure dogs showed less differentiated cortisol responses. This is the **best dog joint-measurement study** — Domain 2 attachment classification and Domain 1 cortisol reactivity in the same animals. Effect sizes were modest; sample n ≈ 132. [ES].

### Solomon, Beetz, Schöberl, Gee, & Kotrschal (2019). *Attachment & Human Development*, 21, 389–417 (PMC6532729).
Formal adaptation of Ainsworth ABCD classification to dogs; documented disorganized attachment patterns in dog–human relationships, with associated cortisol patterns. [ES] for the classification scheme's applicability.

### Hekman, Karas, & Dreschel (2014). *Applied Animal Behaviour Science*, 161, 94–104. Tuber, Hennessy, Sanders, & Miller (1996). *Journal of Comparative Psychology*, 110, 103–108.
Tuber et al. is an early demonstration that companion-dog cortisol is buffered by familiar human presence. Hekman documents within-individual cortisol variability in working/service dogs. [ES].

### Lenkei, Faragó, Bakos, & Pongrácz (2021); Riemer, Müller, Range, Huber, & Virányi (2014). *PLoS ONE*, 9, e92942.
Document that behavioral inhibition and bold-shy temperament in dogs predict separation-related behavior, but the cross-domain correlation, where reported, is moderate (r ≈ 0.2–0.4) and paradigm-dependent. [PI].

**Subsection synthesis**: Dogs are one of the few mammalian systems with reasonably mature joint-measurement designs. The dominant pattern is that **secure attachment is associated with lower cortisol reactivity in the Strange Situation**, *but* the Strange Situation is itself a stressor paradigm — so this is partially a state-level confound. Whether secure-attachment dogs would also be calmer in non-attachment stressors (a true trait test) is undersettled. [GC for trait interpretation].

## 7. Titi Monkeys and Marmosets

### Mendoza & Mason (1986a, 1986b). *Physiology & Behavior*, 38, 795–801; *American Journal of Primatology*, 11, 245–255.
Foundational: adult titi monkeys (Plecturocebus cupreus) show behavioral distress, vocal, locomotor, and cortisol responses to separation from pair-mate, but **not** to separation from parents — establishing the pair-bond/HPA coupling. n's typically 6–12 per condition. [ES].

### Hennessy, Mendoza, Mason, & Moberg (1995); Mendoza, Capitanio, & Mason (2000). In *Coping and Health* (Levine & Ursin volume).
Partner presence buffers stress responses to challenge; demonstrates within-bonded-pair Domain 2 → Domain 1 modulation. [ES].

### Bales, Mason, Catana, Cherry, & Mendoza (2007). *Brain Research*, 1184, 245–253.
Male titi monkeys (n = 13) pre- and post-pairing, FDG-PET. Pair-bond formation altered global cerebral glucose metabolism and changed cortisol regulation. [ES].

### Hinde, Muth, Maninger, Ragen, Larke, Jarcho, Mendoza, Mason, Rilling, Li, Patisaul, Bales, & Capitanio (2016). *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*, 10, 221.
Direct joint-measurement: pair-bonded titi monkeys, separation/reunion challenge, neural (PET), cortisol, and behavioral measures. Showed that the cortisol separation-response varies substantially across individuals and tracks with pair-bond behavioral indices. [ES] within-paradigm.

### Mustoe, Birnie, Korgan, Santo, & French (2014); French, Cavanaugh, Mustoe, Carp, & Womack (2018) — marmoset (Callithrix) and tamarin literature.
Marmosets show partner-preference, biparental care, and oxytocin/V1aR involvement; intranasal OT modulates both anxiety-like behavior and partner preference, but trait correlations across individuals are rarely reported. [PI].

**Subsection synthesis**: The titi monkey is the primate-pair-bond analog of the prairie vole. Joint measurement is consistent: separation activates HPA, partner presence buffers it. **Trait-level individual differences in pair-bond quality predicting baseline stress reactivity have not been clearly established**; most reported variation is at the state/treatment level. [TU] for trait correlation.

## 8. Squirrel Monkeys and Guinea Pigs

### Coe, Mendoza, Smotherman, & Levine (1978). *Behavioral Biology*, 22, 256–263; Coe, Glass, Wiener, & Levine (1983). *Psychoneuroendocrinology*, 8, 401–409.
Mother-infant squirrel monkey separations produce robust cortisol elevations and distress vocalizations. Repeated separations habituate the behavioral but not the endocrine response. [ES].

### Hennessy (1997). *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 21, 11–29.
Major review synthesizing HPA responses to brief social separation across species. **Key analytical point**: HPA activation does not invariably accompany behavioral attachment markers; the two can be dissociated (e.g., surrogate-reared infants show behavioral but reduced cortisol response). This is one of the strongest *dissociation* claims in the literature — **arguing for separable systems even within a single paradigm**. [ES].

### Levine, Wiener, & Coe (1993). *Psychoneuroendocrinology*, 18, 297–306.
Infant squirrel monkeys' cortisol response to maternal separation is buffered by presence of other colony members — social buffering generalizes beyond the dyadic bond. [ES].

### Lyons, Wang, Lindley, Levine, Kalin, & Schatzberg (1999). *Biological Psychiatry*, 45, 1611–1615.
Quantified individual variation in cortisol response to separation in squirrel monkeys (n ≈ 30). Animals with greater cortisol increases showed greater compensatory ACTH suppression. [ES] within-paradigm; no Domain 2 trait measure included.

### Hennessy & Moorman (1989). In *Maternal Effects in Mammals* — guinea pig (Cavia porcellus) work showing cortisol response to maternal separation that is moderated by mother's presence/absence and by individual temperament differences. [ES].

### Hennessy, Kaiser, & Sachser (2009). *Frontiers in Neuroendocrinology*, 30, 470–482.
Programmatic review proposing **passive coping (cortisol-driven, "sickness-behavior" mediated by proinflammatory cytokines) as a stage of separation response**. Strong theoretical claim that the affiliative and stress systems are coupled through specific neural and immunological mechanisms. [PI].

**Subsection synthesis**: Squirrel monkey and guinea pig work is dominated by state-level coupling (separation → HPA), but Hennessy's analyses are notable for explicitly arguing that **behavioral and endocrine separation responses are dissociable**, supporting a separable-systems position. [GC].

## 9. Rat Maternal Care and Adult Outcomes

### Liu, Diorio, Tannenbaum, Caldji, Francis, Freedman, Sharma, Pearson, Plotsky, & Meaney (1997). *Science*, 277, 1659–1662.
Naturally occurring variation in maternal licking-grooming (LG) in rats. Offspring of high-LG mothers show reduced HPA response to stress as adults (Domain 1). r > 0.6 between maternal LG frequency and offspring HPA measures. [ES].

### Caldji, Tannenbaum, Sharma, Francis, Plotsky, & Meaney (1998). *PNAS*, 95, 5335–5340.
Offspring of high-LG mothers show reduced behavioral fearfulness in adulthood. [ES].

### Francis, Champagne, & Meaney (2000). *Journal of Neuroendocrinology*, 12, 1145–1148; Champagne, Diorio, Sharma, & Meaney (2001). *PNAS*, 98, 12736–12741.
Variations in maternal behavior associated with differences in adult OTR levels in medial preoptic area (in females). Suggests maternal-care-driven setting of both stress reactivity and oxytocinergic substrates of affiliation. [ES].

### Francis, Young, Meaney, & Insel (2002). *Journal of Neuroendocrinology*, 14, 349–353.
Naturally occurring differences in maternal care associated with OTR (females) and V1aR (males) expression in adult offspring — providing a developmental bridge between Domain 1 antecedent and Domain 2 substrate. [ES].

### Starr-Phillips & Beery (2014). *Developmental Psychobiology*, 56, 1166–1177.
Direct joint measurement: rat offspring of high-LG mothers spent significantly more time in social contact with unfamiliar conspecifics *and* showed reduced anxiety; **anxiety and social interaction were correlated within individuals (r reduced anxiety ↔ higher social interaction)**. No OTR binding differences detected by ¹²⁵I-OVTA. This is one of the clearest single-cohort within-rat correlations between Domain 1 and Domain 2 in the rat literature. [ES].

### Champagne & Meaney (2006). *Biological Psychiatry*, 59, 1227–1235.
Gestational stress reduces postpartum LG and offspring OTR levels, producing transgenerational coupling of Domain 1 and Domain 2 phenotypes. [ES].

**Subsection synthesis**: The Meaney program is the strongest **trait-correlation evidence** for Domain 1 ↔ Domain 2 coupling in rodents, but the coupling is engineered through *shared maternal-care antecedents* rather than demonstrated as primary. Within a single experimental cohort, low-LG-reared adults show *both* high HPA reactivity and low affiliative engagement. Whether the coupling would survive controlling for the maternal-care antecedent (i.e., is there residual cross-domain correlation?) is **not typically reported** — a methodological gap. [GC].

## 10. Mouse Genetic Models

### McFarlane, Kusek, Yang, Phoenix, Bolivar, & Crawley (2008); Pobbe, Defensor, Pearson, Bolivar, Blanchard, & Blanchard (2011). *Behavioural Brain Research*, 216, 446–451.
BTBR T+tf/J inbred mouse — autism-model. **Low sociability** in three-chamber and reciprocal interaction tests. Anxiety phenotype is **inconsistent**: Pobbe et al. found anxiogenic profile in elevated plus maze; Silverman, Yang, Lord, & Crawley (2010) and Frye & Llaneza (2010) found elevated corticosterone; other groups found normal-to-low anxiety. [GC].

### Silverman, Yang, Turner, Katz, Bell, Koenig, & Crawley (2010). *Neuropsychopharmacology* studies and Frye & Llaneza (2010). *Physiology & Behavior*, 100, 264–267.
BTBR show elevated baseline and stress-induced corticosterone vs. C57BL/6J. **Direct joint observation**: a low-affiliation strain has high HPA reactivity. [ES] for the strain-level pattern. **Methodological caveat**: strain-level (not individual) variation and confounded by many other genetic differences — at most a single data point on cross-domain coupling.

### Pobbe, Pearson, Defensor, Bolivar, Young, Lee, Blanchard, & Blanchard (2012). *Hormones and Behavior*, 61, 436–444 — BTBR show altered OTR distribution patterns relative to B6.
[ES] for the neuroanatomy.

### Ayriyants, Ryabushkina, Sapronova et al. (2023). *Experimental Brain Research*, 241, 687–698.
BTBR vs. B6 with early-life-stress manipulation. Strain × stress interactions on both anxiety and social behavior. [ES] for the interaction.

### Lee, Caldwell, Macbeth, Tolu, & Young (2008). *Endocrinology*, 149, 3256–3263.
Conditional OTR knockout (forebrain). Males show impaired social recognition. **No deficits in anxiety-like behavior or sucrose intake**. Direct dissociation: removing OTR impairs Domain 2 but spares Domain 1 measures. [ES] — **this is a strong dissociation finding**.

### Choleris, Gustafsson, Korach, Muglia, Pfaff, & Ogawa (2003). *PNAS*, 100, 6192–6197.
OT-KO and OTR-KO mice show deficits in social recognition *and* in social anxiety. Suggests partial coupling. [ES].

### Mantella, Vollmer, Li, & Amico (2003). *Endocrinology*, 144, 2291–2296.
OT-deficient female mice show enhanced anxiety-related behavior. [ES] — supports coupling at the level of the OT gene.

### Jin, Liu, Lu, Higashida, & colleagues — CD38 knockout mouse work (multiple papers, 2007–2015).
CD38-KO mice have reduced central OT release, impaired social behavior, and altered anxiety/HPA. Combined Domain 1 + Domain 2 phenotype. [ES] for the gene-knockout phenotype; [PI] for the natural-variation reading.

### Higashida lab CD157/BST1 knockout work — *Brain Sciences*, 5, 3 (2015) and related: social avoidance + anxiety, both rescued by lipidated OT. [ES] for KO phenotype.

**Subsection synthesis**: Mouse genetic models, especially OT/OTR/CD38/CD157 knockouts, *generally* show that disrupting central oxytocin signaling affects both domains together — supporting a shared-substrate position. But conditional OTR knockouts (Lee et al. 2008) provide clean **dissociation evidence**: social-recognition deficit without anxiety phenotype. The genetic literature thus is genuinely contested between "yoked" and "dissociable" readings. [GC].

## Cross-Cutting Synthesis

### (a) Dominant joint-correlation pattern

There is **no single dominant pattern across the literature**. Three patterns coexist:

1. **State-level co-engagement** (in most paradigms — separation, isolation, Strange Situation, immobilization-with-partner). Whenever a stressor with social content is used, Domain 1 and Domain 2 systems engage together. This is the most robustly demonstrated finding. [ES].

2. **Developmental coupling** (Meaney rat LG; Suomi rhesus rearing × 5-HTTLPR; Belyaev fox lines). When early environment or selection shapes one domain, the other tends to shift together. **This is best read as shared developmental antecedents, not as a primary trait coupling**. [ES for the coupling; PI for its interpretation].

3. **Within-population trait orthogonality** (Lambert et al. 2018 prairie voles; Ophir et al. 2008 prairie voles; Lee et al. 2008 conditional OTR KO; Koolhaas theoretical framework). When researchers measure both domains at trait level *within* a homogeneous cohort and avoid shared paradigm artifacts, cross-domain correlations are weak to absent. [ES for these specific studies; GC for generalization].

### (b) Where correlations appear vs. don't

Correlations appear when (i) the paradigm engages both systems simultaneously (Strange Situation, isolation, separation-reunion), (ii) the manipulation acts on a shared substrate (OT, V1aR, 5-HTT, glucocorticoids, neural crest), or (iii) shared developmental antecedents have shaped both. They disappear or attenuate when researchers measure baseline trait variation in homogeneous lab cohorts using non-overlapping paradigms.

### (c) Paradigm dependencies

- **Acute social stressor paradigms** (separation, Strange Situation, immobilization, isolation box) almost guarantee Domain 1 ↔ Domain 2 covariation at the *state* level and should not be treated as evidence of trait coupling without explicit residualization.
- **Pharmacological challenge** with OT, OTR antagonists, AVP, V1aR antagonists generally affects both domains, supporting shared-substrate readings (Pitkow 2001).
- **Non-social stressors** (predator odor, restraint, novel object in isolation) and **non-stress affiliation measures** (free-living partner-preference, paternal care under undisturbed conditions) yield much weaker cross-domain correlations.
- **Naturalistic vs standardized**: Ophir's field studies of prairie voles report weaker neural-behavior associations than the laboratory partner-preference test, illustrating context dependence (Ophir et al. 2008).

### (d) State-vs-trait artifact concerns

The single largest methodological issue across this literature is that **the canonical "joint measurement" paradigms (Strange Situation, separation-reunion, isolation box) elicit both Domain 1 and Domain 2 simultaneously as states**. Reporting that cortisol elevation during the Strange Situation correlates with attachment classification *within the Strange Situation* is essentially a within-paradigm state observation, not a trait-level finding. The dog literature (Schöberl 2016) and sheep literature (Doyle 2022) are most vulnerable; the prairie vole partner-preference + separate-day EPM design (Lambert 2018) is more robust to this concern — and reports null trait correlations.

Other methodological concerns documented in the literature:
- **Construct overlap**: boldness scales sometimes include social approach items (Doyle 2022 for sheep), creating circular cross-domain correlations.
- **Varimax/orthogonal rotation** in temperament factor analyses (multiple species) algorithmically prevents cross-domain factors and **can mask genuine correlation** (Houslay 2023 meta-regression notes this).
- **Small samples**: vole and titi joint-measurement studies often run n < 30, with limited power to detect modest cross-domain correlations.
- **Same-rater/same-paradigm method variance** is common (a single observer codes both anxiety and affiliation in the same session).
- **Pseudoreplication** through multiple measures per individual treated as independent appears in several published behavioral-syndrome studies (flagged in Houslay et al. 2023).

### (e) Competing theoretical positions

**Position A — Yoked single system**: Carter (1998, 2014), Insel (2010), Young (2009), Porges polyvagal theorizing, and the domestication-syndrome tradition (Wilkins, Wrangham, & Fitch 2014) propose that affiliation and stress regulation are expressions of a shared social-engagement system grounded in OT/AVP/HPA/autonomic substrates. Evidence: cross-species comparisons (Insel & Shapiro 1992), receptor manipulations (Pitkow 2001), fox-line selection effects (Trut 2009), OT/OTR-KO double phenotypes (Choleris 2003).

**Position B — Separable systems**: The Koolhaas coping-style program (Koolhaas et al. 1999, 2010; Coppens et al. 2010) and the Hennessy dissociation analyses (Hennessy 1997, 2009) propose that stress-coping and affiliation are orthogonal trait dimensions that share neural infrastructure but vary independently across individuals. Evidence: trait-correlation null results (Lambert 2018; Ophir 2008), conditional OTR knockout dissociations (Lee 2008), behavioral-vs-cortisol dissociation in repeated separations (Hennessy 1986), and the Houslay 2023 meta-regression's weak cross-trait correlations.

**Position C — Developmental coupling, not primary coupling**: Meaney, Champagne, Suomi, and others argue the two domains are coupled because shared early-life experiences (maternal care, rearing) program both — but at the individual level, residual variation in the two domains may be orthogonal. This is the most theoretically modest position and is consistent with the largest number of empirical observations, but is rarely tested directly.

## Joint-Measurement Gaps (Explicit Findings)

These are species × developmental-stage × paradigm cells where **joint trait-level measurement of Domains 1 and 2 has not been done or is so thin as to constitute a gap**:

1. **Meadow vole within-population trait correlation** of HPA reactivity with social tolerance — essentially absent.
2. **Wolves vs. dogs joint measurement of HPA and attachment** — Topál et al. 2005 has the attachment piece, but cortisol comparisons are lacking.
3. **Adult prairie voles tested for cross-domain trait correlation while controlling for partner-bond status** — the cleanest design has not been published.
4. **Marmoset and tamarin trait-level correlations** between cortisol reactivity and partner-preference quality — pharmacology-heavy but light on individual-difference correlation.
5. **Bonobos and chimpanzees** — joint attachment + HPA reactivity individual-difference work is essentially absent, despite robust separate literatures.
6. **Marine mammals** (dolphins, sea lions) — no joint-measurement literature located in this reconnaissance.
7. **Marsupials and bats** — extensive social biology but no joint-measurement studies of the type called for.
8. **Belyaev tame and aggressive lines, within-line individual variation in HPA × tameness** — between-line is well documented, within-line is not.
9. **Rat juvenile play and adult HPA reactivity in same animals** — Panksepp-tradition adjacent but rarely fully cross-domain.
10. **Cattle and horses** — temperament/stress reactivity is studied (Boissy lineage) and social bond/attachment is studied separately (cow-calf, horse-rider), but trait-level joint measurement is sparse.

---

## Bottom Line

Across the mammalian literature surveyed, **state-level co-engagement** of Domains 1 and 2 is empirically settled. **Developmental co-shaping** of the two domains by shared early-environment or selection regimes is empirically settled. **Trait-level individual-difference correlation between the two domains, within homogeneous cohorts, in non-overlapping paradigms, is genuinely contested**: the small set of best-designed studies (Lambert 2018; Ophir 2008; Lee 2008 conditional OTR-KO; Hennessy 1997 dissociation review) lean toward orthogonality, while the developmental-program literatures (Meaney; Suomi; Belyaev) lean toward coupling-via-shared-antecedents. The methodological field is dominated by paradigms (Strange Situation, isolation, separation-reunion) that engage both systems simultaneously and inherently inflate apparent coupling. **The single most consequential unmet design is a within-population, multi-paradigm trait-correlation study with partner-bond status, baseline HPA, reactivity to a non-social stressor, and reactivity to a social stressor all measured on the same individuals** — and that design has been approached most closely in prairie voles, where it returns largely null trait correlations.
