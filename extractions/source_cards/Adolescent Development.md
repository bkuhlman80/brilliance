**BIO-STI-ADL-01 `[EMPIRICAL]`**

**Claim:** Cognitive abilities that operate in unhurried, non-emotional contexts ("cold cognition") — logical reasoning, factual understanding, working memory, response inhibition, planning, probability estimation — reach adult-level performance by approximately age 15–16. Psychosocial capacities that operate under social or emotional arousal ("hot cognition") — impulse control, resistance to peer influence, sensation seeking, real-time perspective-taking, fear extinction — show protracted development into the early to mid-twenties. Evidence: justice-involved youth show adult-like legal comprehension by 16; Tower of London performance matches adults by 15–17; gambling task probability estimation is adult-like even in younger adolescents. But self-reported impulse control keeps improving past 18; susceptibility to peer influence peaks in early adolescence and declines slowly; sensation seeking peaks \~14–19 and doesn't reach adult levels until the twenties; emotional go/no-go errors persist in adolescents when faces carry affective valence; fear extinction learning shows a temporary adolescent-specific impairment that resolves only in adulthood. Steinberg and Icenogle argue this dissociation should inform legal age boundaries: 16 is appropriate for contexts involving unhurried reflection (voting, medical consent), while 18–21 is more appropriate for contexts involving emotional arousal or peer pressure (criminal responsibility, alcohol access). In HPAM terms, this maps to Go (capacity of a general system, online by mid-adolescence) vs. Turn deployment under arousal (routing under social/emotional load, still calibrating into the twenties).

**BIO-CCG-BAB-01 `[FRAMING MOVE] [EVOLUTIONARY]`**

**Claim:** Casey, Cohen, and Galvan argue that the adolescent brain is "beautifully orchestrated" and that adolescent-specific changes — heightened reward sensitivity, altered fear extinction, increased peer sensitivity — represent adaptive traits evolved to help individuals gain independence, not deficits caused by prefrontal immaturity. Heightened reward sensitivity acts as a "pull mechanism" motivating adolescents to leave the safety of the home and explore new environments to secure resources independently. Adolescents show enhanced reversal learning — the ability to quickly change behavior when reward contingencies switch — which is adaptive for navigating novel, unpredictable environments. Temporary suppression of contextual fear (fear of a whole environment) during early adolescence may prevent abandoning exploration after a single negative encounter, while extinction-resistant cued fear (remembering a specific threat) preserves vigilance to actual predators. Development is hierarchical: subcortical circuit functionality is a "necessary precursor" for signaling the prefrontal cortex, which is in turn a necessary precursor for complex cortico-cortical interactions in adulthood. Blocking or disrupting the adolescent pattern — e.g., segregating adolescents who engage in risk-taking into isolated groups — can be harmful, disrupting the normal neuroanatomical shifts in reward sensitivity needed for healthy social transition.

**BIO-CRD-SAE-01 `[FRAMING MOVE]`**

**Claim:** Crone and Dahl argue that the standard "frontal cortical immaturity" model of adolescent behavior should be replaced by a model of social-affective engagement and goal flexibility. Changes in social and affective processing beginning at puberty confer adaptive advantages — specifically, greater flexibility in adjusting intrinsic motivations and goal priorities to navigate new social contexts. The adolescent brain is not deficient in control; it is functionally prioritizing social goals. The "online" usage of social cognitive skills — using another person's perspective to guide real-time decisions — continues to improve through late adolescence precisely because the architecture is being tuned for social complexity, not merely narrowed for intellectual efficiency. This reframe shifts the developmental story from "what's wrong with the adolescent brain" to "what is the adolescent brain specifically optimized to do during this period."

**BIO-BFE-HUE-01 `[EMPIRICAL]`**

**Claim:** Using data from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health (Add Health), Brumbach, Figueredo, and Ellis found that environmental harshness (exposure to violence — seeing someone shot or stabbed, being shot/stabbed) and environmental unpredictability (unreliable home life — parents not meeting basic needs, staying in a homeless shelter, removal by Social Services) during adolescence (ages 11–20) predicted a coordinated life history strategy in young adulthood (ages 18–26). Outcome variables formed an integrated higher-order factor linking health, sexual restrictedness (risky behaviors, contraception use, pregnancy history), social deviance (delinquency, drug/alcohol use, impulsivity, Machiavellianism), and resource-accruing potential (educational attainment, work history, financial security, reliance on government aid). The findings support a calibration model, not a damage model: natural selection favored mechanisms of phenotypic plasticity allowing individuals to facultatively adjust behavior to match local conditions. Adolescent exposure to violence and unpredictability signals high extrinsic mortality risk, making it adaptively beneficial to pursue a "fast" life history strategy — maturing quickly, reproducing early. The authors note that early childbearing in high-risk neighborhoods is often strategic planning rather than absence of planning. Life history strategy was persistent over the lifespan, and traits became more coherent and coordinated over developmental time.

**BIO-SKG-SAV-01 `[EMPIRICAL] [MECHANISM]`**

**Claim:** The adolescent HPA axis is qualitatively different from both child and adult versions. In rodent models, the acute hormonal stress response lasts approximately twice as long in adolescents as in adults, and adolescent stress responses fail to habituate to chronic stressors (adults habituate; adolescents show repeatedly heightened responses). Adolescence serves as a "second developmental stage" where the HPA axis can be recalibrated based on the current environment: longitudinal data on youth who suffered early childhood institutionalization (with blunted cortisol responses) showed cortisol increases over the course of puberty, reaching levels of never-institutionalized peers by late puberty — but only if they moved to stable, supportive environments. Brain regions with high glucocorticoid receptor density (prefrontal and limbic regions) are particularly vulnerable to prolonged stress hormone exposure. The association cortex remains in a more plastic state during adolescence compared to earlier-maturing regions, undergoing a gradual reduction in the excitatory-to-inhibitory (E/I) neurotransmission ratio — a biological hallmark of sensitive period closure. Stress can accelerate corticolimbic circuit maturation specifically ("stress acceleration"), which Sisk and Gee frame as potentially adaptive in the short term for meeting demands of a harsh environment. Supportive caregiving buffers against stress-associated changes in neural connectivity and fronto-amygdala structural development, and positive parenting can protect against neural effects of adolescent poverty into adulthood.

**BIO-BLM-SCP-01 `[EMPIRICAL] [MECHANISM]`**

**Claim:** The social brain network — medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), temporoparietal junction (TPJ), posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), and anterior temporal cortex (ATC) — undergoes protracted structural and functional development from late childhood through the early twenties. Gray matter volume and cortical thickness decrease in most social brain regions (dmPFC, TPJ, pSTS), reflecting synaptic pruning and white matter encroachment; the ATC is an exception, showing increased gray matter volume until adolescence. White matter volume and integrity increase throughout, reflecting myelination. Functionally, dmPFC activation during mentalizing tasks decreases between adolescence and adulthood while posterior regions (TPJ, pSTS) and ATC activation increase — and adolescents recruit dmPFC more broadly than adults even when social information is task-irrelevant. Critically, these changes are driven by dissociable factors: dmPFC maturation correlates with chronological age regardless of pubertal hormone levels, while ATC recruitment correlates with pubertal hormone levels (testosterone, estradiol) regardless of chronological age. This means different components of the social brain close their plasticity windows on different schedules within the same individual. Blakemore and Mills distinguish between capacity for social cognition (basic Theory of Mind, attained in mid-childhood) and deployment of social cognition in real time (using another's perspective to guide decisions, improving through late adolescence). The Director Task demonstrates this gap: 14–16-year-olds can follow perspective-taking rules but deploy perspective-taking less accurately than adults under communicative pressure.
