# CB5T / Big Five Metatraits — Per-Paper Structured Extractions

Faithful per-paper extractions on higher-order Big Five structure (Digman's Alpha/Beta, DeYoung's Stability/Plasticity, Cybernetic Big Five Theory, and alternative higher-order solutions). No cross-paper synthesis. Each section reports what the paper itself claims, finds, and argues, in the paper's own terminology.

Tier 1 = canonical CB5T / metatraits papers. Tier 2 = papers that test, extend, or critique the higher-order solution. Tier 3 = papers proposing alternative higher-order solutions (GFP, Big Two, alternative meta-architectures).

## Digman (1997) — Higher-order factors of the Big Five

**Citation:** Digman, J. M. (1997). Higher-order factors of the Big Five. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, *73*(6), 1246–1256. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.73.6.1246

**Tier:** 1

**Core claim:** The Big Five are not the highest level of the trait hierarchy. Re-analysis of factor correlations from 14 published Big Five studies recovers two — and only two — higher-order factors (provisionally labeled α and β) that emerge consistently across child, adolescent, and adult samples, across rater methods (self, peer, teacher), and across instruments. These higher-order factors map onto classical theoretical dichotomies (socialization vs. personal growth; communion vs. agency).

**Factor structure recovered:** Two higher-order factors. Factor α was indicated primarily by Agreeableness and Emotional Stability and generally also by Conscientiousness; factor β was indicated by Extraversion and Intellect/Openness in all studies. EFA yielded two factors with the first two eigenvalues of the inter-factor correlation matrices exceeding unity and parallel analysis supporting two factors in 12/14 datasets. CFA tested two nested models — Model 1 fixed factor coefficients ≥ .20 in accord with mean exploratory loadings, Model 2 freely estimated previously near-zero coefficients. Across all 14 studies, CFI for Model 1 ranged from .957 to 1.000; for the less-constrained Model 2, .981 to 1.000. Digman interprets α as a socialization factor (impulse restraint, conformity, agreeable conduct) and β as a personal growth / agency / surgency factor.

**Sample:** 14 published correlation matrices. 5 datasets based on children/early adolescents (teacher ratings); 9 on adults (4 self-rating adjective composites, 2 sets of NEO-PI-R correlations, 2 sets of peer ratings, 1 PCI study, 1 Yik & Bond Hong Kong sample). Sample sizes vary across the 14 source studies (not pooled).

**Instrument:** Multi-instrument: NEO-PI-R, PCI, Goldberg adjective markers, teacher ratings, peer ratings, self-rating composites.

**Statistical model:** Re-analysis of correlation matrices. EFA via common-factor model (squared multiple correlations as initial communalities, two iterations) with varimax rotation. Confirmatory factor analyses run in EQS testing two specified models against each of the 14 correlation matrices.

**Methodological wrinkles:** Re-analysis of *factor* correlations, not raw item or facet data — the input is already one level of aggregation removed. Most input correlation matrices came from the published literature with unweighted-composite factor scoring. Digman acknowledges that orthogonal first-order factor solutions are partly an artifact of varimax rotation; he argues correlated solutions are more theoretically defensible. Two child datasets (Costa & McCrae 2; John et al. 1) gave somewhat anomalous initial results, attributed to grouping of rating scales and small N (~70). The Hong Kong Yik & Bond data are flagged as possibly reflecting an indigenous Chinese structure; Digman includes them with a footnote.

**Direct quotes:**
- "Two higher-order factors were clearly evident in all studies." (p. 1246)
- "Many personality theorists appear to have considered one or both of these 2 metatraits, provisionally labeled α and β." (p. 1246)

**Page references:** Abstract and core claim, p. 1246; method (factor correlations, models), pp. 1247–1248; mean factor loadings (Tables 2–3) and CFI ranges, p. 1248; α/β interpretive mapping (socialization, personal growth, communion/agency), pp. 1249–1252.

## DeYoung, Peterson & Higgins (2002) — Higher-order factors of the Big Five predict conformity

**Citation:** DeYoung, C. G., Peterson, J. B., & Higgins, D. M. (2002). Higher-order factors of the Big Five predict conformity: Are there neuroses of health? *Personality and Individual Differences*, *33*(4), 533–552. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0191-8869(01)00171-4

**Tier:** 1

**Core claim:** Digman's (1997) higher-order Big Two replicates in two independent samples and is more naturally interpreted in *trait* rather than *outcome* terms. The two factors are renamed Stability (Emotional Stability + Agreeableness + Conscientiousness) and Plasticity (Extraversion + Openness), positioned as biologically predicated metatraits indexing serotonergic and dopaminergic function, respectively. Stability positively predicts conformity; Plasticity negatively predicts conformity — implying "neuroses of health" in highly conforming individuals (stable but rigid).

**Factor structure recovered:** Two-factor higher-order solution. Confirmatory replication of Digman's α/β across two samples; same five-domain → two-metatrait pattern. Loadings: Emotional Stability (low N), Agreeableness, Conscientiousness on Stability; Extraversion and Openness on Plasticity. SEM with conformity as outcome: Stability → Conformity β = .98 (university), .69 (community), p < .01 in both; Plasticity → Conformity β = –.48 (university), –.42 (community), p < .05 in both. Authors interpret the pattern as evidence that the metatraits are substantive traits, not artefacts of socially desirable responding.

**Sample:** Two independent samples — university students (N = 245) and community adults (N = 222), University of Toronto / surrounding community.

**Instrument:** Big Five Aspects (early version of what would become the BFAS), Conformity scale, social desirability indicators.

**Statistical model:** EFA replicating Digman; structural equation models with the two metatraits predicting conformity, controlling for socially desirable responding.

**Methodological wrinkles:** First labeling of "Stability" and "Plasticity" — these labels supersede "Alpha" and "Beta" in subsequent CB5T literature. Authors specifically argue the labels Stability/Plasticity are preferable because Digman's "socialization" and "personal growth" suggest *outcomes* rather than basic *tendencies*; the new labels emphasize that the metatraits are trait dimensions inheritable early in life. Conformity is operationalized via socially desirable responding indicators — there is unavoidable definitional circularity, which authors address by including both substantive personality scales and SDR indices in the SEM. Two-sample replication strengthens the structural inference but both samples are Canadian.

**Direct quotes:**
- "We will refer to the higher-order factors, or metatraits, as the Big Two." (p. 535)
- "The most thorough conformists will tend to be stable but also rigid, less able to adjust to novelty or change." (abstract, p. 533)

**Page references:** Abstract / core claim, p. 533; metatrait labels Stability/Plasticity introduced, p. 535; biologically predicated model (serotonin/dopamine), pp. 536–540; SEM results, pp. 545–548.

## DeYoung (2006) — Higher-order factors of the Big Five in a multi-informant sample

**Citation:** DeYoung, C. G. (2006). Higher-order factors of the Big Five in a multi-informant sample. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, *91*(6), 1138–1151. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.91.6.1138

**Tier:** 1

**Core claim:** When Big Five intercorrelations are estimated at the *latent* level using multi-trait multi-method (MTMM) data from four informants per target, they remain non-zero and recover Stability and Plasticity as higher-order factors — directly contradicting Biesanz & West (2004) and other claims that higher-order Big Five factors are pure rater-bias / evaluative-bias artifacts. The higher-order factors survive method-effect controls.

**Factor structure recovered:** Seven MTMM CFA models tested. The correlated traits / correlated uniquenesses (CTCU) model fit best:
- BFI: χ² = 136.01, df = 120, CFI = .99, RMSEA = .017 (90% CI .000–.029)
- Mini-Markers: χ² = 149.93, df = 120, CFI = .99, RMSEA = .023

Chi-square difference tests rejected orthogonality of the latent Big Five for both instruments (BFI χ²_diff(10) = 90.37; Mini-Markers χ²_diff(10) = 65.78; both p < .00001). Higher-order CFA recovered Stability and Plasticity at the latent level. Latent metatrait correlations (.45–.53 in DeYoung et al. 2002 single-rater data) reduced under multi-informant modeling. Latent average absolute Big-Five correlation: BFI .15, Mini-Markers .11. Phrase-rating BFI shows higher interrater agreement (mean different-informant same-trait r = .47, SD = .08) than single-adjective Mini-Markers (mean r = .36, SD = .08); the difference in interrater agreement is associated with weaker latent correlations and a less coherent higher-order structure for the Mini-Markers.

**Sample:** Eugene-Springfield Community Sample. N = 490 targets (rating themselves on BFI; 487 on Mini-Markers). Each target rated by self plus 3 peer informants (significant other 21.7%, spouse 28.0%, friend 11.4%, co-worker 27.9%, relative 1.2%, other 6.3%). Total informants ~1,470. Targets ages 18–80, M = 51.23, SD = 12.62; 97% Caucasian American.

**Instrument:** BFI (44 items, phrase-rating); Saucier Mini-Markers (40 single-adjective items). Both administered to all four informants per target.

**Statistical model:** Multi-trait multi-method confirmatory factor analyses (Amos 5.0, ML estimation). Seven competing models compared (CTNM, CTCU, OTCU, CTCM, CTOM, OTCM, OTOM). Higher-order CFA tested whether Stability/Plasticity recover at the latent trait level after partialling method effects. Comparison of BFI and Mini-Markers tests whether higher-order coherence is contingent on instrument format (phrase vs. single-adjective).

**Methodological wrinkles:** The CTCU model leaves the structure of method effects unspecified beyond allowing correlated uniquenesses within each rater — a less restrictive method-effect model than CTCM. Authors argue this is appropriate when method effects are not clearly unidimensional. Targets self-selected peer informants, which is the realistic but introduces target-by-informant non-independence. The Mini-Markers instrument was created by Saucier (1994) by selecting subsets of TDA adjectives chosen for clarity, so the lower interrater agreement is not a generic property of single-adjective inventories. Single sociocultural sample (community-recruited Caucasian Americans).

**Direct quotes:**
- "Latent metatraits derived from ratings of multiple informants might be less strongly linked to socially desirable or undesirable qualities — across all trait dimensions, in which case latent metatraits derived from ratings of multiple informants should be more reliably linked to the observable patterns of behavior than to individual informants' judgments about desirability." (p. 1140; tightened: "Latent metatraits from multi-informant ratings should reflect observable behavior, not informants' desirability judgments." p. 1140)
- "The metatraits Stability and Plasticity were present as higher-order factors at the latent level." (p. 1138)

**Page references:** Abstract, p. 1138; introduction and Biesanz/West rebuttal, pp. 1138–1140; method (sample, instruments, MTMM models), pp. 1140; results (Tables 1–3, model fit, latent correlations), pp. 1141–1147; uniquenesses and biases analysis, pp. 1142–1147.

## DeYoung, Quilty & Peterson (2007) — Between facets and domains: 10 aspects of the Big Five

**Citation:** DeYoung, C. G., Quilty, L. C., & Peterson, J. B. (2007). Between facets and domains: 10 aspects of the Big Five. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, *93*(5), 880–896. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.93.5.880

**Tier:** 1

**Core claim:** Below the Big Five domains and above narrow facets there exists an empirically replicable intermediate level: 10 "aspects" (two per domain). Aspects correspond to two distinct genetic factors recovered in prior twin work (Jang et al., 2002), implying that aspects index genetically distinct sources of variance within each Big Five domain rather than arbitrary subdivisions.

**Factor structure recovered:** Within each Big Five domain, the most likely number of factors among facets is two. Velicer's MAP test indicated two factors in each domain (one exception, Extraversion, which collapsed to two when the single Excitement-Seeking facet was excluded). The 10 aspects: N → Volatility / Withdrawal; E → Enthusiasm / Assertiveness; O/I → Intellect / Openness; A → Compassion / Politeness; C → Industriousness / Orderliness. Factor 1 in each domain accounted for 38–50% of facet variance, Factor 2 for 19–24%. Genetic factor scores from Jang et al. (2002) corresponded well to phenotypic aspect scores. Higher-order structure above the Big Five (Stability/Plasticity, Alpha/Beta) is acknowledged but not retested here.

**Sample:** Study 1: Eugene-Springfield Community Sample (ESCS), N = 481 (200 men, 281 women), age 20–85 (M = 52.5). Study 2: independent IPIP validation sample, N = 480. Study 3: same N = 481 ESCS sample correlated with genetic factor scores from Jang et al. (2002).

**Instrument:** NEO-PI-R (six facets per domain) and AB5C-IPIP (nine facets per domain). 75 facets total entered the within-domain factor analyses. Construction of the 100-item BFAS (10 items per aspect) using IPIP items.

**Statistical model:** Principal-axis factoring with direct oblimin rotation within each Big Five domain. Number of factors set by Velicer's MAP test. BFAS construction by selecting IPIP items with highest correlations to the empirically derived aspect factors. BFAS validated in independent sample (internal consistency α range .75–.88; mean ~.82).

**Methodological wrinkles:** Two facets cross-loaded substantively (Trust loaded –.52 on N; Assertiveness loaded .56 on C; Reflection loaded .51 on A and .50 on O/I), retained in their assigned domain. Excitement-Seeking initially produced a three-factor solution in Extraversion; authors retained the two-factor solution after excluding it because it correlated .999 with the unexcluded scores. Single sample for the within-domain factor analyses (no cross-validation of factor structure itself). Genetic correspondence relied on factor scores from a separately collected twin sample, not co-twin data in the ESCS.

**Direct quotes:**
- "Two constructs, labeled Alpha and Beta (Digman, 1997), or Stability and Plasticity (DeYoung, 2006; DeYoung et al., 2002), appear to constitute the highest level of personality organization." (p. 880)
- "The correspondence was strong enough to suggest that the 10 aspects of the Big Five may have distinct biological substrates." (abstract, p. 880)

**Page references:** Abstract / core claim, p. 880; methods and MAP test, pp. 881–882; within-domain factor structure (Tables 2–3), pp. 882–884; BFAS construction and validation, pp. 887–890; correspondence with Jang et al. genetic factors, pp. 890–893.

## DeYoung et al. (2010) — Testing predictions from personality neuroscience: brain structure and the Big Five

**Citation:** DeYoung, C. G., Hirsh, J. B., Shane, M. S., Papademetris, X., Rajeevan, N., & Gray, J. R. (2010). Testing predictions from personality neuroscience: Brain structure and the Big Five. *Psychological Science*, *21*(6), 820–828. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610370159

**Tier:** 1

**Core claim:** A biologically predicated theory of the Big Five (DeYoung & Gray, 2009) generates trait-specific predictions about which brain regions should covary in volume with each domain. Structural MRI in a healthy adult sample confirms theory-consistent volumetric associations for four of five domains: Extraversion ↔ medial orbitofrontal cortex (reward processing); Neuroticism ↔ regions for threat / punishment / negative affect; Agreeableness ↔ regions processing intentions and mental states of others; Conscientiousness ↔ lateral prefrontal cortex (planning, voluntary control). Openness/Intellect did not show the predicted associations.

**Factor structure recovered:** N/A — this is a brain-structural test of the Big Five, not a structural personality factor analysis. Domain-level NEO-PI-R scores used as continuous covariates in voxel-wise GLMs. The paper does *not* test higher-order metatraits directly but provides the personality-neuroscience scaffolding that CB5T's mechanistic claims rest on.

**Sample:** N = 116 healthy right-handed adults (58 female, 58 male; ages 18–40, M = 22.9, SD = 5.5) recruited from Washington University in St. Louis and the surrounding community, plus 1 within-study reference subject (female). Participants pre-screened to exclude history of neurological/psychiatric disorders or psychoactive drug use.

**Instrument:** Self-report NEO-PI-R (Cronbach's α: N = .92; E = .87; O/I = .89; A = .91; C = .91). Structural MRI: 3-T Allegra (Siemens), MPRAGE high-resolution structural image. Image preprocessing via BioImage Suite (Papademetris et al., 2006); within-study reference subject selected near group average for personality and image quality; affine + nonlinear coregistration to a Colin27 template; Jacobian determinant of the nonlinear coregistration used as the local-volume measure; smoothed 8-mm FWHM Gaussian.

**Statistical model:** Voxel-wise general linear model with sex (fixed effect), age, whole-brain volume, and the five NEO-PI-R domain scores as continuous covariates. Whole-brain analysis with hypothesis-targeted regions of interest. Negative-control region: primary visual cortex (no Big Five trait predicted to covary with it).

**Methodological wrinkles:** Single-site sample, restricted age range (18–40), and exclusion of clinical conditions limit generalizability. Authors are explicit about the volume-function direction problem: greater volume *might* indicate greater capacity, but in some cases (e.g., late-childhood cortical thinning correlating with intelligence; Shaw et al. 2006) efficiency may dominate. They therefore frame predictions as "structural variation in this region supports the trait's hypothesized function" rather than "more volume = more trait." Hypothesis-driven design constrains multiple-comparison concerns relative to a fully exploratory whole-brain analysis. The Openness/Intellect null is acknowledged but not interpreted as falsifying the trait-region linkage — authors note that PFC regions hypothesized for O/I (dorsolateral, anterior PFC, anterior parietal) overlap functionally with regions for working memory and may show different volume-function relations.

**Direct quotes:**
- "Findings supported our hypotheses for four of the five traits: Extraversion, Neuroticism, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness." (abstract, p. 820)
- "The Big Five factors are descriptive rather than explanatory constructs and do not inherently provide a theory of the underlying forces that produce these five dimensions." (p. 821)

**Page references:** Abstract and four-of-five summary, p. 820; biological model and trait predictions, pp. 822–824; method (sample, MRI, GLM), pp. 824–825; brain-structure findings per trait, pp. 825–828.

## DeYoung (2015) — Cybernetic Big Five Theory

**Citation:** DeYoung, C. G. (2015). Cybernetic Big Five Theory. *Journal of Research in Personality*, *56*, 33–58. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2014.07.004

**Tier:** 1

**Core claim:** Personality is the population of evolved, goal-directed (cybernetic) self-regulating mechanisms instantiated in a person, plus the updateable memory contents (characteristic adaptations) that those mechanisms operate over. Each Big Five domain reflects variation in parameters of one or more evolved cybernetic mechanisms; the personality hierarchy has four levels (metatraits → domains → aspects → facets); above the Big Five sit two metatraits, Stability and Plasticity, with no general factor of personality above them.

**Factor structure recovered:** Four-level hierarchy (Fig. 1, p. 36). Top level: two metatraits — Stability (N reverse-scored, A, C) and Plasticity (E, O/Intellect). Level 2: Big Five domains. Level 3: 10 aspects (per DeYoung et al., 2007). Level 4: facets (number unknown). The Stability/Plasticity correlation observed in single-rater data is argued to reflect rater bias; multi-informant data yield uncorrelated metatraits, supporting the metatraits as the highest defensible level (citing Anusic et al. 2009, Chang et al. 2012, DeYoung 2006, McCrae et al. 2008). No general factor of personality is endorsed; CB5T explicitly cites Revelle & Wilt (2013) on this point. Mechanistic interpretation: E = sensitivity of behavioral approach / reward; N = sensitivity of defensive (passive avoidance, withdrawal) systems; O/Intellect = cognitive exploration; A = altruism / coordination of goals with others; C = protection of non-immediate goals.

**Sample:** N/A — narrative review and theoretical synthesis; cites prior empirical work.

**Instrument:** N/A; references NEO-PI-R, BFAS, IPIP-NEO scales used in cited studies.

**Statistical model:** N/A — theoretical framework. Reviews CFA / EFA / twin-genetic / multi-rater evidence from prior work to motivate the hierarchy and metatrait correlations.

**Methodological wrinkles:** Distinguishes traits (probabilistic descriptions of stable patterns of emotion, motivation, cognition, behavior in response to evolutionarily-recurrent stimulus classes) from characteristic adaptations (goals, interpretations, strategies in particular life circumstances). Argues the apparent positive correlation between Stability and Plasticity is a rater-bias artifact (single-rater data) and reverses or vanishes under multi-informant designs. Acknowledges six-factor solutions (Ashton et al. 2004) are more replicable across languages but argues lexical replicability is not the sole criterion (citing DeYoung 2010b). Explicitly states that the BFAS (DeYoung et al., 2007) is the instrument for direct measurement of aspects.

**Direct quotes:**
- "Cybernetic systems are characterized by their inclusion of one or more goals or reference values, which guide the work carried out by the system." (p. 33)
- "Personality traits are probabilistic descriptions of relatively stable patterns of emotion, motivation, cognition, and behavior, in response to classes of stimuli that have been present in human cultures over evolutionary time." (p. 35)
- "They are, in fact, regularly intercorrelated such that there exist two higher-order traits, which we have labeled Stability and Plasticity." (p. 36)

**Page references:** Mission / mechanistic premise, pp. 33–34; cybernetic cycle (5 stages: goal activation, action selection, action, outcome interpretation, goal comparison), p. 34; trait/characteristic-adaptation distinction, pp. 34–37; trait hierarchy figure and metatraits, p. 36; Fig. 2 causal role of traits, p. 37; Big Five mapped to cybernetic mechanisms, Section 4 (esp. pp. 41–47); psychopathology and well-being, Section 6.

## Markon, Krueger & Watson (2005) — Delineating the structure of normal and abnormal personality

**Citation:** Markon, K. E., Krueger, R. F., & Watson, D. (2005). Delineating the structure of normal and abnormal personality: An integrative hierarchical approach. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, *88*(1), 139–157. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.88.1.139

**Tier:** 2 — replicates and extends the higher-order Big Five hierarchy across normal and abnormal personality; tests Digman's Big Two as a level in the hierarchy.

**Core claim:** Normal and abnormal personality variation can be described within a single integrative hierarchy. A constructive replication across a meta-analytic dataset, a new empirical study, and multiple instruments recovers a hierarchy that runs from two factors (Digman's α/β–like Big Two) through three and four to five factors. The hierarchy is unbalanced — Neuroticism (negative emotionality) persists across all levels; Openness emerges only at the five-factor level and is absent from most abnormal-personality structural models, which yield a four-factor "Big Four" lacking an Openness analog.

**Factor structure recovered:** Hierarchical structure with replicable two-, three-, four-, and five-factor levels. Two-factor level corresponds to Digman's higher-order Alpha/Beta (here labeled in plus/minus emotionality / disinhibition terms consistent with Tellegen's PEM/NEM/Constraint). Five-factor level corresponds to Big Five. Abnormal-personality inventories (DAPP, SNAP) recover four factors aligning with Big Five minus an Openness analog; substituting dependency/submissiveness for Openness in some structures. Markon et al. argue the two- and four-factor levels parallel the internalizing/externalizing distinction in psychopathology.

**Sample:** Meta-analytic component pooling joint factor analyses of multiple normal and abnormal personality inventories. New empirical sample contributing additional structural data.

**Instrument:** DAPP-BQ (Dimensional Assessment of Personality Pathology), SNAP (Schedule for Nonadaptive and Adaptive Personality), NEO-PI-R, MPQ, and others reviewed. Joint factor analyses across pairs of instruments.

**Statistical model:** Joint factor analyses across instruments at multiple extraction levels (two through five factors). Hierarchical replication across samples and instruments. Extension to test how subordinate levels nest under superordinate levels.

**Methodological wrinkles:** "Constructive replication" rather than a single confirmatory test — multiple datasets, multiple instruments, and triangulation. The unbalanced hierarchy (Neuroticism persisting across all levels) is presented as a substantive finding rather than a measurement artifact. Openness's absence from abnormal-personality structures is interpreted as a real gap in pathology coverage, not a model failure. Joint factor analyses can be sensitive to instrument coverage (e.g., DAPP and SNAP have limited Openness-like content).

**Direct quotes:**
- "Abnormal personality generally represents extremes on continua in common with normal personality variation." (p. 140)

**Page references:** Abstract / claims, p. 139; structural models reviewed, pp. 139–141; hierarchy and Big Two through Big Five, pp. 141–149; unbalanced hierarchy and Neuroticism's persistence, pp. 149–153.

## Ludeke et al. (2019) — Using the BFAS to translate between HEXACO and Big Five

**Citation:** Ludeke, S. G., Bainbridge, T. F., Liu, J., Zhao, K., Smillie, L. D., & Zettler, I. (2019). Using the Big Five Aspect Scales to translate between the HEXACO and Big Five personality models. *Journal of Personality*, *87*(5), 1025–1038.

**Tier:** 2 — borderline. Uses DeYoung's BFAS aspect-level instrument to clarify rotational relations between FFM and HEXACO at the aspect level rather than testing higher-order metatraits directly. Included because aspect-level resolution is the empirical anchor below the Stability/Plasticity layer.

**Core claim:** The 10 Big Five aspects (per DeYoung et al., 2007) provide a parsimonious bridge between FFM (BFAS) and HEXACO (HEXACO-PI-R) personality models. Specifically, the Politeness aspect of Agreeableness maps closely onto HEXACO Honesty-Humility, while Compassion maps onto HEXACO Agreeableness. This clarifies where the two structural models genuinely differ versus where they are rotationally equivalent.

**Factor structure recovered:** Cross-instrument correlation pattern at the aspect level: Politeness ~ HEXACO Honesty-Humility (r ≈ .51); Compassion ~ HEXACO Agreeableness (r ≈ .58). HEXACO Agreeableness shows minimal overlap with FFM Politeness, demonstrating that the two models carve the prosocial space differently. Findings interpreted as supporting an underlying common phenotypic space differently rotated by the two models.

**Sample:** Four samples (cross-sectional). The largest is a German student sample; others include online community samples. Combined N across the four samples on the order of several thousand.

**Instrument:** BFAS (100-item), HEXACO-PI-R, BFI-2.

**Statistical model:** Cross-instrument bivariate correlations and meta-analytic aggregation across the four samples. No formal CFA / SEM of higher-order structure.

**Methodological wrinkles:** Does not test the higher-order metatrait layer directly; conclusions concern aspect-to-domain rotational equivalence. Authors are explicit that the bridging is descriptive — not a claim that one model is correct.

**Direct quotes:** None extracted.

**Page references:** Aspect-to-HEXACO mapping and core findings, results section.

## Schreiber & Marcus (2020) — The Dark Triad in general models of personality

**Citation:** Schreiber, A., & Marcus, B. (2020). The place of the 'Dark Triad' in general models of personality: Some meta-analytic clarification. *Psychological Bulletin*, *146*(11), 1021–1041. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000299

**Tier:** 2 — directly tests Dark Triad common variance against Digman's higher-order Alpha/Beta and against the proposition of a single general factor of personality.

**Core claim:** The shared core of the Dark Triad (narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy) constitutes a higher-order D factor that is *not* subsumed by Digman's Stability (Alpha) or Plasticity (Beta). No general factor of personality underlies all higher-order traits in the meta-analytic structure. Within HEXACO, low Honesty-Humility is the strongest single-trait correlate of D-core; within FFM, D maps primarily onto low Agreeableness and low Conscientiousness, with narcissism additionally cross-loading onto Plasticity (Beta).

**Factor structure recovered:** Two-stage SEM (TSSEM) on pooled correlations. Bivariate findings: all three DT traits correlate negatively with FFM Agreeableness (narcissism r = –.21 to –.29; psychopathy / Mach r = –.39 to –.46); Mach and psychopathy negatively correlate with Conscientiousness (r = –.19 to –.31); narcissism uniquely positive with Extraversion (.31 to .40) and Openness (.13 to .20). At the latent level, common DT variance forms a D factor; this D factor is *not* covered by Alpha + Beta. Within HEXACO, latent D ~ low H-H (ρ ≈ –.80). No general factor across higher-order traits emerges.

**Sample:** Meta-analytic dataset of k = 108 samples, total N = 40,818.

**Instrument:** DT measures: NPI, MACH-IV, SRP, SD3, Dirty Dozen. General models: NEO-PI/FFI, BFI, IPIP, HEXACO-PI-R / HEXACO-60.

**Statistical model:** Two-stage structural equation meta-analysis (TSSEM). Stage 1 pools correlation matrices; Stage 2 fits structural models to the pooled matrix. Tests: (a) DT loadings on Alpha and Beta; (b) one-factor general personality model across higher-order traits; (c) HEXACO with H-H included.

**Methodological wrinkles:** Moderator analyses showed structural equivalence of original DT measures and the Short Dark Triad, but reduced equivalence for the Dirty Dozen — caution applies when pooling across DT instruments. Pooled correlations across heterogeneous designs may inflate fit. Authors are careful to distinguish "no general factor across higher-order traits" from "no GFP" in the GFP-as-substantive-construct sense — the result rules out a higher-order solution that subsumes both the Big Two and a D factor.

**Direct quotes:** None extracted.

**Page references:** Core meta-analytic claims and TSSEM results, pp. 1029–1037; HEXACO H-H ~ D, pp. 1033–1035; conclusion that DT is not subsumed by Alpha/Beta, pp. 1035–1037.

## Ringwald et al. (2024) — Development across the personality hierarchy from adolescence to young adulthood

**Citation:** Ringwald, W. R., Kaurin, A., Lawson, K. M., Wright, A. G. C., & Robins, R. W. (2024). Development across the personality hierarchy from adolescence to young adulthood in a Mexican-origin sample. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, *126*(6), 1141–1160.

**Tier:** 2 — measures personality change simultaneously at the metatrait, domain, and facet levels of the DeYoung-style hierarchy in a longitudinal minoritized sample.

**Core claim:** Personality maturation from adolescence to young adulthood is observable at all three levels of the hierarchy — metatraits (Stability, Plasticity), domains (Big Five), and facets — and the broad metatrait Stability increase is driven primarily by gains in Agreeableness and Conscientiousness. Maturity-principle trends in a Mexican-origin sample are comparable in direction and magnitude to those reported in predominantly White samples, supporting cross-population generalizability of maturation.

**Factor structure recovered:** Hierarchical model with metatraits at the top: Stability (composed of A, C, low N) and Plasticity (composed of E, O). Latent growth curve modeling at each level recovers significant change. Stability increases ages 14–24, driven by A and C gains. Plasticity trajectories more variable. Facets within the same domain showed divergent trajectories — i.e., within-domain heterogeneity is not eliminated by aggregating to domain or metatrait.

**Sample:** Mexican-origin Californian youth, N = 645, assessed at 5 waves spanning ages 14–24. (California Families Project.)

**Instrument:** BFI / BFI-2 across waves.

**Statistical model:** Latent growth curve modeling at three hierarchical levels (metatraits, domains, facets) within an SEM framework.

**Methodological wrinkles:** BFI / BFI-2 was not designed to measure DeYoung's 10 aspects (BFAS is the targeted instrument); facets here are BFI-2-derived facet composites. Single sociocultural sample, though authors explicitly position this as a generalizability test rather than a structural test. The metatrait composite scoring relies on the standard Stability/Plasticity formula derived from prior work rather than re-estimating loadings here.

**Direct quotes:** None extracted.

**Page references:** Abstract / core claims; metatrait, domain, facet trajectories in the results section.

## Samo et al. (2026) — Revisiting the IPIP-NEO personality hierarchy with taxonomic graph analysis

**Citation:** Samo, A., Garrido, L. E., Abad, F. J., Golino, H., McAbee, S. T., & Christensen, A. P. (2026). Revisiting the IPIP-NEO personality hierarchy with taxonomic graph analysis. *European Journal of Personality*, *40*(2), 369–390. https://doi.org/10.1177/08902070251352590

**Tier:** 2 — bottom-up, item-level test of the higher-order Big Five hierarchy; recovers metatraits but not the canonical two-metatrait solution.

**Core claim:** A fully data-driven, item-level network-psychometrics analysis of a 300-item IPIP-NEO dataset recovers a three-level hierarchy (facets → traits → metatraits) that resembles but substantially departs from the theoretical Big Five hierarchy. Notably, three metatraits emerge — Stability, Plasticity, and a novel Disinhibition meta-trait — rather than two; only 16 of 30 theoretical facets emerge empirically; and no general factor of personality is supported.

**Factor structure recovered:** Three-level hierarchy. Level 1 (facets): 28 first-level dimensions from 221 items (theoretical 30 not all recovered; only 16 of 30 emerged with the predicted content; the rest split, merged, or restructured). Level 2 (traits): 6 trait dimensions — Neuroticism, Conscientiousness, Openness, Sociability (a blend of Extraversion's Cheerfulness/Gregariousness with Agreeableness's Empathy/Trust), Integrity (a blend of Agreeableness and Conscientiousness morality content), and Impulsivity. Level 3 (meta-traits): 3 — Stability, Plasticity, and Disinhibition. A fourth (general-factor) level was not supported (unidimensionality index u = 0.49). Theoretical Big Five Extraversion and Agreeableness do not emerge as separable trait dimensions — instead they fracture into Sociability + Integrity + (low) Impulsivity.

**Sample:** N = 149,337 respondents to the open-source 300-item IPIP-NEO dataset.

**Instrument:** IPIP-NEO-300.

**Statistical model:** Taxonomic Graph Analysis (TGA) — a 7-step network-psychometrics framework that combines local-independence detection (Unique Variable Analysis), wording-effect modeling, dimensionality assessment via Exploratory Graph Analysis, hierarchical clustering of communities, and structural-robustness checks.

**Methodological wrinkles:** TGA is item-level and bottom-up — it does not assume a priori that items belong to a Big Five domain. The authors interpret the emergence of Sociability and Integrity (rather than canonical E and A) and a novel Disinhibition meta-trait as a substantive result, not a methodological artifact, but acknowledge that IPIP-NEO item content is itself shaped by NEO-PI-R conventions. Disinhibition is novel relative to DeYoung's two-metatrait solution and does not map cleanly onto Digman's α/β. The paper's conclusion that "no general factor is supported" is operationalized as a unidimensionality index u below threshold.

**Direct quotes:**
- "TGA revealed a three-level structure composed of 28 first-level dimensions (facets), 6 second-level dimensions (traits), and 3 third-level dimensions (meta-traits)." (abstract, p. 369)

**Page references:** Abstract, p. 369; methodological challenges and TGA framework, pp. 369–375; results — three-level hierarchy and Disinhibition meta-trait, pp. 379–386.

## Haehner et al. (2026) — Validating the BFAS (German, French, Italian)

**Citation:** Haehner, P., Krämer, M. D., Bleidorn, W., & Hopwood, C. J. (2026). Validating the Big Five Aspect Scales (BFAS) and the short form (BFAS-40) in German, French, and Italian. *Personality Science*, *6*, 1–22. https://doi.org/10.1177/27000710251391609

**Tier:** 2 — establishes cross-linguistic measurement properties of the aspect-level instrument that anchors CB5T's three-level (metatrait → domain → aspect) hierarchy.

**Core claim:** German, French, and Italian translations of the BFAS (full 100-item version) and the BFAS-40 short form show satisfactory factorial validity within each language and partial weak measurement invariance across the three languages. Aspects within the same Big Five domain show distinct external-variable association patterns, justifying aspect-level (rather than domain-level) measurement for downstream research questions.

**Factor structure recovered:** Within each language, CFA supports the 10-aspect structure (two aspects per domain) consistent with DeYoung et al. (2007). Cross-language: partial weak measurement invariance (factor loadings partially invariant; full scalar invariance not achieved). External-variable correlations diverged systematically between aspects of the same domain (e.g., Volatility vs. Withdrawal within Neuroticism), supporting aspect-level resolution.

**Sample:** PERCIVAL project, N = 4,492 Swiss adults, with German, French, and Italian language subsamples.

**Instrument:** BFAS (100-item) and BFAS-40 short form (newly translated into German, French, Italian).

**Statistical model:** CFA within each language; multi-group CFA testing measurement invariance (configural → weak → strong → strict) across the three language groups.

**Methodological wrinkles:** Switzerland-only sample limits broader generalization to other German-, French-, and Italian-speaking populations. Partial (rather than full) measurement invariance constrains direct mean comparisons across languages. The BFAS-40 short form trades fewer items for less precise aspect-level measurement; reliability is necessarily lower than the 100-item version.

**Direct quotes:** None extracted.

**Page references:** Sample / methods, early sections; CFA and invariance results; aspect-level external-correlate divergence in the discussion.

## Loehlin et al. (2015) — Genetic correlation between general factors of intelligence and personality

**Citation:** Loehlin, J. C., Bartels, M., Boomsma, D. I., Bratko, D., Martin, N. G., Nichols, R. C., & Wright, M. J. (2015). Is there a genetic correlation between general factors of intelligence and personality? *Twin Research and Human Genetics*, *18*(3), 234–242.

**Tier:** 3 — proposes/tests the General Factor of Personality (GFP) as a higher-order solution and asks whether GFP and g share genetic variance.

**Core claim:** A genetic correlation between general intelligence (g) and a general factor of personality (GFP) — predicted by Woodley's mutation-selection-balance hypothesis — can be set to zero in most twin samples without loss of model fit. This is consistent with g and GFP being shaped by *different* evolutionary-genetic processes (mutation-selection balance for g; frequency-dependent selection for personality), implying that GFP, if real, is genetically independent of g and not a single "fitness factor."

**Factor structure recovered:** Univariate g and univariate GFP each extracted from each twin sample; bivariate twin model decomposes the phenotypic correlation between g and GFP into genetic and environmental components. In four of five samples the genetic correlation between g and GFP was statistically indistinguishable from zero. The exception (one US sample) likely reflects instrumentation differences rather than a true population effect.

**Sample:** Five twin samples across four countries (US, Netherlands, Croatia, Australia). Sample sizes vary by study; twin-pair counts moderate to large.

**Instrument:** Personality: JEPQ, CPI, NEO-FFI, BFI. Intelligence: GATB, MAB, NMSQT, Mill Hill, Ravens, WAIS.

**Statistical model:** Bivariate (and univariate) ACE twin models (additive genetic, common environment, unique environment). Tests whether the cross-trait genetic correlation can be fixed to zero.

**Methodological wrinkles:** GFP is operationalized as the first factor of an exploratory analysis of personality scales — its psychometric defensibility is itself contested in the broader literature. The single anomalous US sample is interpreted as a measurement issue, not a substantive heterogeneity finding. Sample sizes per twin design are not always large enough to rule out small genetic correlations — the paper emphasizes the *zero* result is consistent with model fit, not that any genetic correlation can be ruled out at high power.

**Direct quotes:** None extracted.

**Page references:** Core claim and bivariate results across the five samples in the results section; evolutionary-genetic interpretation in the discussion.

## Mai & Church (2023) — Indigenous structure of Vietnamese personality traits

**Citation:** Mai, N. T. Q., & Church, A. T. (2023). Exploring the indigenous structure of Vietnamese personality traits: A psycho-lexical approach. *International Journal of Personality Psychology*, *9*, 1–26. https://doi.org/10.21827/ijpp.9.41003

**Tier:** 3 — proposes alternative cross-cultural higher-order solutions (Big One, Big Two, Big Three) that compete with the Stability/Plasticity higher-order solution.

**Core claim:** A psycholexical study of Vietnamese trait terms shows that the Big One, Big Two, and Big Three replicate moderately to strongly, while the Big Five, Big Six (HEXACO), and ML7 do not replicate well. An eight-factor solution is most interpretable, with five factors aligning with Big Six dimensions plus three indigenous factors. The findings support a three-factor cross-cultural kernel rather than a five- or six-factor universal taxonomy.

**Factor structure recovered:** EFA across solutions. Big One, Big Two, and Big Three solutions emerged with interpretable, theoretically expected loadings. Big Five and Big Six solutions showed poor congruence with target structures from non-Vietnamese samples. The eight-factor solution recovered five Big-Six-aligned dimensions plus three indigenous factors (described in the paper's results). Higher-order Big Two corresponds to Communion / Agency–like dimensions (Warmheartedness-Virtue and a Dynamism-like factor).

**Sample:** N = 710 Vietnamese adults rating 668 trait terms (reduced from 2,129 lexical candidates).

**Instrument:** Indigenous Vietnamese trait terms (no Western personality questionnaire used as the primary measurement instrument).

**Statistical model:** Principal-components analysis with varimax rotation across multiple solutions (1 to 8+ factors). Congruence coefficients computed against target structures from prior cross-cultural lexical studies.

**Methodological wrinkles:** Lexical studies depend critically on dictionary coverage and term-reduction decisions; the 2,129 → 668 reduction may have removed content relevant to the Big Five/Six. PCA with varimax (orthogonal) does not allow correlated factors. Single Vietnamese sample.

**Direct quotes:** None extracted.

**Page references:** Methods (lexical reduction), early sections; factor structure across solutions, results section; three-factor cross-cultural kernel argument, discussion.

## Del Giudice (2023) — A general motivational architecture for human and animal personality

**Citation:** Del Giudice, M. (2023). A general motivational architecture for human and animal personality. *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, *144*, 104967. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104967

**Tier:** 3 — borderline. Proposes the General Architecture of Motivation (GAM) as an *alternative* mechanistic substrate for personality differences, framed as a competitor / complement to CB5T's cybernetic-mechanism account. Does not directly test higher-order Big Five structure but provides an alternative theoretical scaffold for what higher-order traits *are*.

**Core claim:** Personality differences are best understood not as variation in Big Five domain parameters but as variation across five mechanistically distinct components: (1) motivational systems (evolved control mechanisms tied to biological goals), (2) an instrumental goal pursuit system (IGPS), (3) emotion mechanisms (procedural emotions for goal regulation), (4) mood mechanisms (third-order coordination), (5) approach-avoidance effectors. Higher-order personality dimensions (e.g., fast-slow life history, masculinity-femininity, risk-taking) emerge as coordinated variation across these motivational systems rather than as factors at the top of a descriptive taxonomy.

**Factor structure recovered:** N/A — Del Giudice does not perform a factor analysis. He argues that descriptive Big Five structure is causally downstream from motivational-architecture variation and proposes that what factor analyses recover at the higher-order level is a noisy summary of cross-system parameter coordination.

**Sample:** N/A — theoretical synthesis.

**Instrument:** N/A.

**Statistical model:** N/A — conceptual / theoretical paper integrating Panksepp, Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory, Read's neural-network models, Revelle & Condon's Cues-Tendency-Action, Budaev's AHA, and McClelland's motives/goals distinction.

**Methodological wrinkles:** Explicitly presented as a *meta-architecture* rather than a competing factor solution. Argues against one-emotion-per-motivational-system mappings (rejecting strong forms of Panksepp). Distinguishes motives (implicit, embedded in motivational systems) from goals (explicit, value-laden, in IGPS). The framework is generative but not directly falsifiable as a structural model.

**Direct quotes:** None extracted.

**Page references:** Five-component GAM, early sections; mapping to adaptive-problem categories (hazards, resources, reproduction, kin, group), middle sections; higher-order traits as coordinated cross-system variation, discussion.

## Hopwood & Driver (2025) — Personality functioning as generalized correlated changes

**Citation:** Hopwood, C. J., & Driver, C. C. (2025). Personality functioning as generalized correlated changes in personality traits. *Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science*. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.1037/abn0000958

**Tier:** 3 — proposes a *general factor of within-person trait change* as a developmental general dimension distinct from any cross-sectional general factor of personality, and argues this captures personality functioning / maturation.

**Core claim:** A general factor of within-person correlated change emerges across all 30 NEO-PI-R facets in longitudinal data. This change-level general factor correlates r = –.86 with healthy-personality ratings and predicts personality-disorder symptom reduction more strongly (r = .43–.45) than change in any single FFM domain. The change-general-factor is interpreted as personality functioning — a developmental maturation dimension structurally and functionally distinct from the trait-level general factor of personality (GFP) debate.

**Factor structure recovered:** Bifactor-like decomposition of within-person change at the facet level. A general change factor accounts for substantial covariance among facet-level changes; specific change factors at the domain level capture remaining structured covariance. The general change factor is the strongest predictor of PD-symptom-reduction outcomes; it correlates strongly negatively with concurrent healthy-personality ratings.

**Sample:** Longitudinal samples from clinical / psychotherapy datasets including the Collaborative Longitudinal Personality Disorders Study (CLPS); details in the paper. Within-person facet-level change estimated from repeated NEO-PI-R administrations.

**Instrument:** NEO-PI-R (30 facets); healthy-personality ratings; PD symptom counts.

**Statistical model:** Continuous-time / latent-change SEM modeling within-person change at the facet level; bifactor or general-factor extraction on the change scores; correlations of general change factor with external criteria.

**Methodological wrinkles:** The general-factor finding pertains to *change* covariance, not cross-sectional personality covariance — a substantive and previously underexplored layer. Authors are explicit that this is not a re-test of the cross-sectional GFP. The CLPS sample is clinical and may inflate the magnitude of change (and therefore covariance among changes) relative to community samples. NEO-PI-R does not measure DeYoung's 10 aspects directly, so the analysis is at the facet/domain level rather than the metatrait/aspect level.

**Direct quotes:** None extracted.

**Page references:** Core claim and general-change-factor result in results; r = –.86 with healthy-personality and r = .43–.45 with PD symptom reduction in results / discussion.

## Thalmayer et al. (2025) — The cross-cultural Big Two

**Citation:** Thalmayer, A. G., Mather, K. A., Saucier, G., Naude, L., Florence, M., Adonis, T.-A., Shino, E. N., Asatsa, S., Witzlack-Makarevich, A., Bachlin, L. Z. M., & Condon, D. M. (2025). The cross-cultural big two: A culturally decentered theoretical and measurement model for personality traits. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, *128*(5), 1181–1208. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000528

**Tier:** 3 — proposes a Big Two (Communion/SSR + Agency/Dynamism) as the higher-level structure under which the Big Five organizes, derived from a globally decentered lexical base rather than from Western inventories.

**Core claim:** A two-factor higher-order personality model — Communion / Social Self-Regulation (warmth, morality, respect, industriousness, even temper) and Agency / Dynamism (competence, confidence, fearlessness, positive mood, sociability) — emerges across 11 global lexical studies and replicates in two large empirical samples. The newly developed CCB2I (45 items) measures these two dimensions and is offered as the canonical measurement instrument for the Big Two when culturally decentered measurement is required. Communion/SSR predicts lower Externalizing; Agency/Dynamism predicts lower Internalizing.

**Factor structure recovered:** Two-factor higher-order model. Study 1 identified 55 recurrent terms from two-factor solutions across 11 lexical studies. Study 2 refined the CCB2I in two large samples. Internal consistency (α): Communion/SSR .87–.91; Agency/Dynamism .78–.91. Hierarchical omega-h in SAPA: Communion/SSR .63; Agency/Dynamism .57. Inter-factor correlation r = .33–.53 across samples.

**Sample:** Study 2: ALLS (African Languages Lexical Study) — N = 2,496 South African 18-year-olds. SAPA Project — N = 61,760 across 13 countries.

**Instrument:** CCB2I (newly developed 45-item Cross-Cultural Big Two Inventory); also: IPIP IPC, QB6, SPI-27, Inventory of Mental Health and Approach (IMHA), Duke Religion Index.

**Statistical model:** Cross-study lexical aggregation (Study 1); EFA / CFA / measurement invariance in Study 2; external-criterion regressions linking Big Two scores to Internalizing and Externalizing.

**Methodological wrinkles:** Authors explicitly position the model as "culturally decentered" — derived from a global lexical base rather than translated from Western Big Five inventories. Communion and Agency labels are deliberately chosen to align with Bakan / Wiggins terminology rather than Digman's α/β or DeYoung's Stability/Plasticity, but the conceptual overlap with α/Stability and β/Plasticity is not directly tested via joint factor analysis here.

**Direct quotes:** None extracted.

**Page references:** Communion/Agency content domains, p. 1182; lexical aggregation across 11 studies, Study 1 sections; ALLS and SAPA results, Study 2 sections; Externalizing / Internalizing prediction, results / discussion.

**Notable absences:**
- No explicit critique-as-method-artifact paper (e.g., Anusic et al., Chang et al., Ashton et al. 2009 GFP critique, Biesanz & West 2004 in primary form).
