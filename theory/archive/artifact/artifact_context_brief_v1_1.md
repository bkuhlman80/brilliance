## 1. What the whole enterprise is

Brian is building a shelf of six books in one world. Not a series — a shelf: different
genres, formats, registers, readerships.

1. ***Liona's Brief History of Personality*** — the anonymous theory book. **This project.** First
   on the shelf in publication order, which is why printing it anonymously is not a stunt
   attached to a novel. It is simply publishing book one.
2. **The YA novel** — four high-schoolers in Salt Lake build a robot with a real personality
   using that book as their bible, win a world championship, and discover their school
   district owns him. Brian's debut, under his own name.
3. **HEXACON** material — the competition, currently folded into the novel rather than split
   out.
4. **The therapy/friendship book** — Marge teaching Bramble to conserve, to make memories,
   because his personality is constantly revising.
5. **Bramble's memoir** — first love, expanding consciousness, leaving home.
6. **Mira's anime graphic novel** — over-the-top, committed to its own small-world grandiosity,
   Calvin-and-Hobbes in spirit.

The artifact serves all of them and touches none of them. It is the object that has to be
real.

## 2. Where this came from, which matters for the voice

There is a completed adult literary novel — *Loom* — covering the same material: a lab builds
a robot up twelve evolutionary rungs while a child grows up beside it, narrated by the funder
in cold retrospective reconstruction. It is finished and Brian has rated its voice a 6.

**That draft is now a quarry, not a book.** The YA is a full rewrite. But the adult project
produced a diagnosis of prose failure that governs everything here, including this manuscript,
and it is the most valuable thing to carry forward.

**The art it was reaching for:** Steinbeck/Ricketts naturalism. The withheld arrival. The
small thing given full weight. Comedy as ecology. The thought that closes in the reader's head
and not on the page.

**The two legitimate registers, and the trade each demands:**

- **COLD.** No interior, ever — and you *pay in surface*. Obsessive, inexhaustible, unsqueamish
  body. Ricketts denies you the crab's inside and pays you in the carapace. Test: could a
  reader draw her?
- **HOT.** All the way in, full commitment to the felt moment — and you *give up the surface*.
  A woman in the grip of a thing does not notice her own hair.

**The failure is the middle: no interior and no surface.** Weather and gloss. That is what most
of the adult draft does. The discriminator, applied per scene before a word is written: *what
is the reader being paid in?*

**Five named failure modes, which are one disease with five faces.** In each, the prose does
the thing, fails to trust the thing, and then performs its own relation to the thing:

1. **The caption** — the gesture lands, then a sentence explains what the gesture said.
2. **The weather** — the body is declined and the mountains handed over instead. Season-light
   as free emotion.
3. **The gloss** — the prose touches a real interior and retreats to the observation deck to
   theorize it. *It goes in. It won't stay.*
4. **The announced withhold** — *"I won't say the word."* A real withhold is invisible. The
   ellipsis is allowed; the announcement is not. The crime is never the gap — it is turning to
   the reader to be admired for leaving it.
5. **The tasteful-literary reflex** — the elegant ellipsis reached for because it is *pretty*,
   and because it covers a place where you don't have the goods. **When a withhold feels
   beautiful, check whether you actually have the thing you're declining to say.**

Two hard-won procedural lessons: **sweep for the crime, never the artifact** (a search for a
euphemism came back clean across twelve chapters and felt like an answer; the actual defect was
the *move*, and when the right query was finally run it found twenty-three instances). And
**when you kill a rule, sweep for its residue** — retirement is a document change, removal is a
sweep.

## 3. The voice problem in *this* manuscript

The framework as it stands reads careful, hedged, provenance-tagged, epistemically immaculate,
and compressed. **It reads that way because Claude helped write it.** The argument is Brian's
and it stands. The voice is a placeholder.

A previous instance was asked to build the author and did something rigorous and backwards: it
read the framework closely and derived the person who would have written it. That produces a
character optimized to generate exactly the prose we are removing. **The inference runs the
other way. Build the man, then rewrite the text as him.**

The man is John Smirker — engineer, four employers, adjunct statistics instructor two nights
a week for twenty-six years, lost his speech to throat cancer in 2013, wrote the book as a
syllabus for a course that was never scheduled. Full specification in his document. The single
inversion that fixes most of the problem: **Claude would rather be precise than clear;
Smirker would rather be clear than precise, and will tell you the analogy is rough.**

## 4. Why the object exists, and the ethical spine

In the novel, four kids find an anonymous, incomplete, coffee-stained paperback and treat it as
scripture. Brian is going to make that paperback real.

**The governing principle: findable, never purchasable.** A book you can order is a product. A
book that turns up is a rumor. Teenagers can smell merch from orbit.

**The lore that resolves the real-world act:** in the fiction, the book exists because its
*first reader* — someone in the Salt Lake collector world — came into a manuscript, believed
it, and paid for forty copies so it would exist. No author's name because the printer didn't
have one. Which means Brian is not publishing as the author. He is publishing as that person,
and every word of it is true: he is someone who caused this text to exist as an object.

**The ethical spine, and it is not decoration.** Silence is unlimited and free. A lie is not.
Brian can decline to answer any question forever; he cannot assert a falsehood. Anonymity has
a long honorable literary history; fabricated authorship does not, and the cautionary case is
JT LeRoy, where what detonated was not the pseudonym but that a *person* was constructed and
put in front of people.

This is why Rule 2 is absolute, and why it must be held against Brian when he drifts toward
it — which he has, twice, in reasonable-sounding increments ("create an author identity,"
"affordances that mislead the detectives"). Both times the resolution was a distinction, not a
refusal: **the author's identity lives inside the text and nowhere else**, and **"mislead"
means underdetermine, not lie.** The text may honestly support multiple incompatible readings.
It may not assert anything untrue.

## 5. What the object is supposed to feel like

Humble to the point of embarrassing. A perfect-bound print-on-demand paperback, default serif
title, no jacket copy, half-letter trim. The interior is a photocopy of a photocopy — you can
see the degradation, and the coffee ring on page 140 got copied so many times it is part of the
book now.

**It should look religious.** That is the Salt Lake detail doing real work: a self-published
treatise on the nature of the self, chapter titles that sound doctrinal, sitting on a thrift
shelf for eleven years because everyone who picked it up assumed it was somebody's tract. It
survived by being mistaken for the thing that would get it ignored.

**The cover is clean and the interior is filthy.** Do not degrade the cover. In the lore the
interior is a copy of something *found*; the cover was *made*, by the person who paid for the
printing, because a found manuscript doesn't come with one. That asymmetry is the most eloquent
thing about the object and it is what makes a collector look twice.

**Where a signature would go, there is nothing.** The colophon records what was done — how
many, when, where, what the source was — and never why. Facts a job printer would have, no
adjectives, faintly bureaucratic, closing out a job. One flat line noting the source ended
where it ends, so nobody assumes the bindery lost signatures. **No copyright notice at all:** a
notice is a claim, and the person in the lore had no standing to claim the text.

## 6. What the kids do with it, so the text knows what ore it's providing

The novel's B-plot mines this book. The kids never learn who wrote it; they resolve the
*provenance* instead. Three authorship theories stay live to the end, and each kid's choice is
characterological — Bart wants the crank because it means anyone can do this, Burns wants the
anonymous scientist because he wants the work to have cost somebody something, Lisa wants the
model because instruments don't lie, and Marge won't pick, which infuriates everyone.

For the manuscript, that means certain passages are the ore body. Each must be honestly what
this man would write — never a wink, never a planted falsehood:

- **Coinage before definition.** A private term used confidently for thirty pages. Marge
  notices the definition never comes.
- **The over-stretched metaphor.** The home domain pressed past its load limit. Bart takes it
  literally and builds it, and it works, which is worse.
- **The unexplained second person.** In the novel the kids argue about who *you* is. In fact
  it is a teacher's reflex, and it increases as his audience disappears.
- **Unexplained arithmetic**, shown in full, purpose assumed. Lisa's mother checked it in
  pencil in the margin. It's right. This is where the annotator becomes a real person to them.
- **Misplaced precision** — fanatical exactness about something trivial beside a large claim
  waved through. Some of it is comic on first read; **much of it plays straight**, because
  handing you a tolerance is a kindness and he knows it. Either way, on reread it is a window
  into what he could and couldn't test alone. *Corrected against the letters; see Smirker.*
- **The personal example** — the bee passage. Too specific, obviously from life, zero context.
  The juiciest passage in the book because it supports all three theories at once.
- **The temper**, three or four times, one sentence each, always at somebody who did not check.

**The annotation layer is separate.** The margin-arguer is hand-written per copy after
printing, differently in each, so no two copies match — which means comparing marginalia across
copies is the only way to know what's in someone else's, and that is the social life of the
object. The manuscript file stays clean.
