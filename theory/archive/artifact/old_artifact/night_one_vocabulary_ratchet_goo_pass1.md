## Vocabulary

Five words.  I am putting them here, ahead of the machinery they belong to, because you will hit all five before I get around to building any of it, and it is easier to carry a definition than to guess at one.

### What I Mean by Ratchet

Feynman worked through a machine that exists to show you why this word is a trap.  It is in the *Lectures*, volume one, chapter forty-six, 1963.  Smoluchowski had the objection first, in 1912, and Feynman says so.

Here is the machine.  A wheel with sawteeth cut into it.  A spring holds a pawl down into the teeth.  Turn the wheel one way and the pawl rides up over the sloped face of each tooth and drops into the next one.  Turn it the other way and the pawl catches on the vertical face and the wheel stops.  You have one of these in your socket wrench.

Now run an axle from that wheel out to a paddle sitting in a box of gas.  Molecules hit the paddle from both sides, at random, equally.  No net push in either direction.  But the pawl only lets the wheel go one way.  So the random kicks ought to accumulate into rotation, and you could tie a thread to the axle and lift a weight with it, and you would be lifting that weight out of a gas that is all one temperature.  Heat in, work out, nothing else.  If that worked, the heating bill for this building would be zero and I would have had a different career.

It does not work, and the reason it does not work is the entire content of the word.

The pawl is in the gas too.  It is getting kicked exactly as hard as the paddle is.  A pawl holds only as long as its spring keeps it seated, so every so often a molecule knocks the pawl up off a tooth at the same moment the wheel is being pushed backward, and the wheel slips back one tooth.  Work out the two rates at a single temperature and they come out equal.  Forward by ratchet, backward by slip, exactly canceling.  The wheel jitters and goes nowhere forever.

To get net motion out of it you have to hold the pawl colder than the paddle.  And holding any part of a system colder than what surrounds it is not free.  I spent a career on that bill.

So a ratchet has three parts and not two.

1. **Undirected variation.**  The kicks.  Both directions, no preference, no plan.
2. **A rectifier.**  The pawl.  Lets one direction through and blocks the other.
3. **A paid reset.**  Whatever it costs to keep the pawl in a condition to do its job.

When I use the word ratchet I mean all three.  Most uses mean the first two.  The third one is the one that gets dropped, and it is worth knowing why it gets dropped, because the reason is going to come back in this course more than once: it is a recurring cost with nothing to show for it.  It does not produce the motion.  It only makes the motion possible.  Those are different things and only one of them is easy to put on a page.  I have never seen the second one on a budget line and I have looked.

The three parts do not sit together.  The Reviser is the variation.  The gate at Retention is the rectifier.  The maintenance tax is the paid reset.  So when I call something a ratchet I am not pointing at a function.  I am pointing at a whole loop, and at the fact that somebody is paying for it.

### Each Line Ratchets a Different Kind of Goo

*Goo* is what a line works on.  The genetic line works one kind of stuff, the neuronal line another, the symbolic line a third, the digital line a fourth.  Each one takes its stuff, pushes it somewhere it does not want to go, and then spends the rest of its existence keeping it there.  I wanted one word that covered all four and the good ones were taken.

The same four functions turn up at every line — Effector, Regulator, Modeler, Reviser — built out of whatever that line has on hand to build with.  The functions do not change.  The stuff does.  So a line is a machine for ratcheting one kind of goo, and what makes one line different from another is the goo and not the architecture.

Four kinds.

**The genetic line ratchets Boltzmann goo**, which is heat and disorder, and I can show you that one with a building.  Hold a room at seventy in July.  Heat comes back in through the walls, through the glass, around the door, all day, every day, and you pay to move it back out all day, every day.  Stop paying and the room is outdoors again by suppertime.  There is no condition the building can reach where the bill stops.  Life is the same arrangement running on a longer contract.  A cell holds itself far away from where physics would leave it, by taking in energy that is ordered and putting out waste that is not, continuously, until the day it does not.  Selection keeps the arrangements that hold that gap open cheapest.

**The neuronal line ratchets Friston goo**, which is the gap between what a unit expects and what it gets.  A unit with a brain in it carries a model of its world and works to keep that gap small, and it has two ways to do that and it uses both.  Change the model until it matches the world.  Change the world until it matches the model.  There is a formalism that counts the gap, called variational free energy, and I should tell you plainly that I can follow it about halfway and I am taking the rest on somebody's word.  What the description tracks is a unit that keeps itself out of states it was not expecting, and selection keeps the units that run the gap narrower.  What that looks like from the outside, in an animal you can actually sit and watch, is temperament — a stable way of weighting threat against reward that shows up across situations and holds for life.

**The symbolic line ratchets Shannon goo**, which is the space of what could have been sent.  Take a building code.  Before the code, the set of buildings you are allowed to put up is enormous, and a great many of them fall down or burn.  The code cuts that set down to something much smaller, and what is left is a set that two strangers who have never met can agree about in advance.  A drawing standard does the same thing to drawings.  That is what a symbol is: a cut in the space of legitimate messages, which is the only reason a signal carries anything at all.  Every word is a cut.  Every grammar, every convention, every unwritten rule about what you do not say at a meeting is a further cut.  Cultural transmission keeps the cuts that let a group coordinate and drops the ones that do not, and an empire is a very large number of cuts held in place at considerable expense.

**The digital line ratchets Kolmogorov goo**, which is the length of the shortest description that will produce a thing.  I can put that one in a building too.  A set of drawings is not the building.  It is the shortest thing you can hand a crew that will get you the building.  Now take two buildings of the same square footage.  One is twelve identical floors, and the drawing set is thin, because you draw the floor once and write *typical* on it.  The other is a one-off with no two rooms alike, and the set is enormous, and there is nothing you can do about it — for a building like that you cannot beat handing over the building, give or take the cost of saying *here*.  That difference, between what compresses and what will not, is the whole of what Kolmogorov complexity measures.  It is the length of the shortest program that produces the object on a given machine.  Low for anything regular.  High for anything with no pattern in it.  And in the general case you cannot compute it, which means you never actually know you are holding the shortest set, only that you have not found a shorter one.

The digital line is what happens when the drawing set stops needing a crew.  A procedure stored somewhere that a machine carries out with nobody reading it aloud — the first time a written pattern runs itself.  What runs gets kept and copied.  What never runs gets dropped, the same way a detail nobody ever built stops appearing in the next job.

All four lines are doing the same thing at the line level.  Keep what worked, lose what did not, repeat.  What differs is the stuff being held in place, the machinery holding it, and how long one turn takes.  That is why the four lines are genuinely different and not one story told over, and it is also why the same four functions keep showing up in all of them.

*The first of those four is Deacon's and I am taking it whole — the Shannon-Boltzmann-Darwin paper, 2007.  The second one is two claims stacked and I would rather hand them to you separated.  The formalism is real, it is not mine, and nobody argues about the mathematics.  Reading it as the stuff an entire line works on is my move, and it is the kind of move that is easy to make and hard to check.  The trait surface under it is measured and I am not the one who measured it — Sih, Bell and Johnson, 2004, and Koolhaas on coping styles.  The third one is mine outright.  I think it follows from the same principle, and I have not found anybody who has said so, which is either because it is obvious or because it is wrong.  The fourth one is the one I am least sure of and I would rather tell you now than let you find it out on your own.  The formalism is not in doubt — Solomonoff had it in 1960, Kolmogorov in 1964, Chaitin in 1966, three men separately, which usually means the idea was sitting there waiting.  What is mine is reading it as the goo of an entire line.  And the piece I have not settled is what does the selecting.  I have named Execution and I have not managed to talk myself the whole way into it.*
