# # A Sociolinguistic/Dialectology Reference Library: Features and Representative Utterances for Four Speaker Profiles

**Scope and method.** This is a dialectology reference library of documented linguistic features and representative example utterances, for reference use only. Every feature is tied to a named source. Example utterances are tagged **ATTESTED** (drawn from or directly reported in a source), **ATTESTED-form** (a documented word/phrase reproduced verbatim inside a constructed frame), or **CONSTRUCTED** (a naturalistic exemplar built to illustrate a documented feature — not a corpus quotation). Neutrality is maintained throughout: no profile is framed as better or worse, and no register is softened or exaggerated. Contested or thin points are flagged in place. No fiction or target text was consulted; this rests only on linguistic scholarship and language data.

## PROFILE 1 — UTAH ENGLISH (Intermountain West)

### (a) Phonology renderable in prose

- **CARD–CORD merger** (/ɑr/–/ɔr/): historically the most-reported Utah feature; makes *port/part*, *lord/lard*, *born/barn*, *horse/hoarse* near-homophones. It is now a relic form — in David Bowie's own words the merger is "now generally relegated to speakers above 70 years of age" (Bowie, ResearchGate abstract, *Early Development of the Card-Cord Merger in Utah*), having risen to near-completion by the 1930s and then reversed once it became stigmatized (Bowie 2003, *American Speech* 78(1):31–51; Bowie 2008, *American Speech* 83(1):35–61). Eye-dialect renderings: *born* → "barn," *for* → "far," *storm* → "starm."
- **Pre-lateral mergers** (Utah's distinctive cluster): FAIL–FELL (/eɪ/–/ɛ/ before /l/), FEEL–FILL, FULL–FOOL / POOL–POLE–PULL, DULL–PULL (Di Paolo & Faber 1990; Bowie 2003; Baker & Eddington 2009). Renderable: *sale/sell*, *mail/Mel*, *fail/fell*, *feel/fill* collapse.
- **LOT/THOUGHT (cot–caught) low-back merger**: characteristic of the ANAE "West" region generally (Labov, Ash & Boberg 2006); in Utah it was still incomplete even by the 1990s (Di Paolo 1992, per Bowie 2012).
- **T-glottalization with ORAL release** in *mountain, kitten, button, satin*: the diagnostic Utah variant is not the glottal stop itself — glottalization was actually *more* common in non-Utahns (89% vs. 81%) — but releasing the stop orally rather than nasally ([mawʔən] rather than [mawʔn̩]). Per Eddington & Savage (2012), "releasing the glottal stop orally rather than nasally … is the most likely candidate for 't-dropping' since Utahns did this in 17% of the cases compared to less than 1% in non-Utahns" (*American Speech* 87(3):336–349); the oral-release pattern is led by young women. Renderable: "moun'ain," "bu'on," "ki'en."
- **T-intrusion** in words like *else* ("elts"), and g-dropping (Baker et al. 2009, per BYU *Schwa* survey).

### (b) Morphosyntax

- **Propredicate DO**: *do* as a pro-form for a whole predicate together with a modal, e.g., "Are you going? — I might do." Documented for Utah/the Intermountain West by Di Paolo 1993 ("Propredicate Do in the English of the Intermountain West," *American Speech* 68(4):339–356). Di Paolo found that LDS-affiliated speakers used the construction while other Americans did not; it was likely imported from 19th-century English LDS convert-immigrants who settled directly in Salt Lake City. Attested (dependent-clause, conservative) example reported from the paper: "…I usually kinda take a back seat, which I know I shouldn't DO but…" This differs from Standard American English, which uses bare ellipsis ("I shouldn't have ___") rather than a bare pro-predicate *do* after a modal.
- Otherwise general Western/American morphosyntax; the pre-lateral and CARD–CORD phenomena are phonological, not syntactic.

### (c) Lexicon and idiom inventory

- **LDS in-group vocabulary** (MormonWiki; churchofjesuschrist.org "Common Church Lingo"; Grace for Grace): *ward* (congregation, ~200–500 members), *stake* (grouping of wards), *calling* (assigned volunteer role), *bishop*, *testimony* / *bear one's testimony*, *fast Sunday* / *testimony meeting*, *FHE* (Family Home Evening, Monday), *RM* (returned missionary), *the MTC*, *mutual*, *fireside*, *sealing*, *Relief Society*, *Primary*, *Sunbeams*, *home teacher/ministering*, *investigator*, *BIC* (born in the covenant).
- **Minced oaths / euphemistic swearing** (Deseret News; wesclark.com "Oh My Heck, Flippin' Fetch"): *Oh my heck* (called "the quintessential Beehive colloquialism"), *oh my gosh/gash*, *dang*, *dang it*, *fetch*, *flip* ("abso-flippin-lutely"), *freak*, *scrud* ("Scrud Oh Dear!"), *Judas Priest*, *H-E-double-hockey-sticks*, *son of a biscuit*.
- **Regional slang** (iHeart; Deseret News): *sluff* (skip class), *dirty soda*.
- **Food terms** (Eliason et al., *This Is the Plate*; BYU Magazine; Visit Cedar City; Deseret News): *fry sauce* (mayo-ketchup; Arctic Circle origin), *funeral potatoes* (cheesy hash-brown casserole for post-funeral luncheons), *(Utah) scone* (fried dough, not the British pastry, served with honey butter), *dutch oven* (Utah's official state cooking pot), *green Jell-O*, *Navajo taco*, *dirty soda*.

### (d) Pragmatics and discourse style

- **Euphemism as a community norm**: minced oaths let speakers "express deep emotion by breaking the linguistic boundaries" while avoiding profanity in a high-religiosity speech community (wesclark.com).
- **Service-oriented / indirect politeness**: interaction organized around callings, service, and congregational identity (e.g., introduction by church role: "I'm their stake president … and also their friend," Grace for Grace).
- **Contested/thin**: broad claims that Utahns are uniquely "indirect" are not from a controlled pragmatics study — treat pragmatic generalizations here as ethnographic/impressionistic rather than experimentally established.

## PROFILE 2 — CALIFORNIA ENGLISH

### (a) Phonology renderable in prose

The California Vowel Shift (CVS) is largely sub-phonemic, but several elements surface in eye-dialect and lexical choice. Sources: Eckert 2008 (*J. Sociolinguistics* 12(4):453–476); D'Onofrio, Pratt & Van Hofwegen 2019 (*Language Variation and Change* 31(2)); Podesva et al. 2015; ANAE Western chapter (Labov, Ash & Boberg 2006).

- **LOT/THOUGHT (cot–caught) merger**: general in California; *cot/caught*, *Don/Dawn* homophonous.
- **TRAP split / backing**: /æ/ raised before nasals (*ban, hand* → higher) but backed/lowered elsewhere (*bat, sack* → toward "bot/sock"). The backed non-nasal /æ/ is the salient "Valley Girl" marker (D'Onofrio 2015, *J. Sociolinguistics* 19(2):241–256; Pratt & D'Onofrio 2017).
- **GOOSE- and GOAT-fronting**: *dude* → "diude"-ish; *no way* → fronted nucleus; renderable loosely as "dude" → "dewd."
- **KIT/DRESS lowering/backing**: *kit* toward "ket," *dress* toward "drass" — part of the shift/compression.

### (b) Morphosyntax and prosody

- **Uptalk / high-rising terminal (HRT)**: declaratives with final rise. In Southern California, statements bear L\* L-H% (and less often H\* H-H%), distinct from polar questions' L\* H-H%; women use it more, with steeper/later rises (Ritchart & Arvaniti 2014, *Speech Prosody*). Renderable with a "?" on a declarative.
- **Creaky voice / vocal fry**: utterance-final low creak as a discourse/boundary feature, associated with (but not exclusive to) young women; Eckert reports her students hear it as "authoritative" (NPR 2015; Podesva, Callier & Szakay 2015).
- **Quotative *be like***: "And I was like, '…'." A discourse-central quotative. *Frequency caution:* the strongest quantitative frequency data on *be like* come from Tagliamonte & D'Arcy's Canadian/British corpora, not California; California-specific frequencies should be treated as inferred rather than established.
- **Discourse *like***: unstressed filler/planning marker.
- **Existential *there's* + plural**: "There's three variants" (Krejci & Hilton 2017; Hilton 2018) — agreement variation documented in California data.

### (c) Lexicon and idiom inventory

- **Intensifiers/quantifiers**: *hella* (stereotypically Northern California; Bucholtz et al. 2007, "Hella Nor Cal or Totally So Cal?"), *so*, *totally*, *way*, *super*.
- **Discourse markers/quotatives**: *like*, *be like*, *I'm all* (older), *dude*, *bro/brah*.
- **Freeway naming**: Southern California uses definite article + number — "the 405," "the 101," "the 5"; Northern California drops it ("101," "580") (Geyer 2001; *California English*, Wikipedia; parodied in SNL's "The Californians").
- **Lexical**: *freeway* (vs. "highway"), *PCH* (Pacific Coast Highway) in SoCal, *NorCal/SoCal*, *the city* (= SF in NorCal), *trippy*, *gnarly*, *June gloom*.
- **Persona-linked vocabulary**: the Valley Girl and Surfer Dude registers (Pratt & D'Onofrio 2017); "business professional" persona (D'Onofrio 2015, 2018) — the same feature can be read differently depending on persona priming.

### (d) Pragmatics and discourse style

- **Stance and persona-indexing** (Eckert's "indexical field," 2008): features index not fixed categories but a field of stances/personae (Valley Girl, nerd, business professional). D'Onofrio (2015, 2018) shows persona priming shifts perception of the very same vowel token.
- **Mitigation/solidarity**: *like*, uptalk, and *so* soften assertions and build rapport/common ground; uptalk is also used to hold the floor and preempt interruption (Ritchart & Arvaniti 2014; Eckert via NPR).
- **Contested**: popular ideologies frame uptalk/fry as "insecure/airheaded"; sociolinguists (Eckert, Fought) reject this and note men also creak/uptalk but are less policed — report the ideology, not as fact.

## PROFILE 3 — NORTH MIDLAND / PITTSBURGH-ADJACENT, PLAIN-PRECISE REGISTER

Hallmark: **precision and completeness in few words** — monosyllabic, plain, but carrying the full semantic distinction intact, nothing lost. Sources: ANAE Midland chapter (Labov, Ash & Boberg 2006); Johnstone, Bhasin & Wittkofski 2002 ("'Dahntahn' Pittsburgh," *American Speech* 77:148–166); Johnstone & Kiesling 2008 (*J. Sociolinguistics* 12(1):5–33); Johnstone, *Speaking Pittsburghese* (2013); Murray & Simon 2006; Murray 1993; DARE.

### (a) Phonology renderable in prose

- **Monophthongal /aw/**: *downtown* → "dahntahn," *house* → "hahs," *out* → "aht," *how* → "hah"; applies to any /aw/ syllable ending in a consonant, including *deal, really* per Johnstone (Johnstone, Bhasin & Wittkofski 2002; Kiesling & Wisnosky 2003). Renderable: "dahntahn," "aht," "hahs," "Stillers" (Steelers).
- **Cot–caught (low-back) merger** with [ɔ] realization — one of two features Johnstone et al. (2002) identify as unique to the dialect.
- **L-vocalization**: *steel* → "steeool," *pail* → "paio."
- **Other renderable forms**: *worsh* (wash), *crik* (creek), *spicket* (spigot), *Iggle* (Giant Eagle).

### (b) Morphosyntax — the diagnostic bundle

- **"needs + past participle"**: "The car needs washed," "The dishes need done," "The grass needs cut"; also *wants/likes + V-en*: "The cat wants fed" (Murray & Simon 2006; Johnstone & Kiesling PBS essay). Scots-Irish origin; Midland + Appalachian.
- **Positive *anymore*** ("these days/nowadays"): "Anymore he drives to work" (Murray 1993).
- **Punctual *whenever*** ("at the one-time moment that"): "Whenever she passed away, she had pneumonia" (Scots-Irish; Midland/South).
- **Reversed *leave/let***: "Leave him go" (= let him go) (Pitt colloquialisms page; Labov, Ash & Boberg 2006).
- **Secondary contractions/clitics** and clause-final **n'at** (= "and that," ≈ et cetera).
- **Second-person plural *yinz/yunz/you'uns***.

### (c) Lexicon and idiom inventory

Sources: Johnstone; Kiesling; DARE; Heinz History Center; Pitt colloquialisms page.

### (d) Pragmatics and discourse style

- **Plain-spoken, direct, understated, information-dense, low-elaboration** — the profile's hallmark. Midland forms are valued locally as "efficient, natural, and completely ordinary."
- **Precision through the right regional word**: *redd up* ≠ vaguely "clean"; it means specifically "put in order/tidy." *Nebby* names a specific trait (prying). Single words carry fine distinctions.
- **Enregisterment/awareness paradox**: those most aware of /aw/-monophthongization as "local" are least likely to produce it (Johnstone & Kiesling 2008) — relevant to whether a speaker performs or simply uses the dialect.
- **Working-class solidarity indexing**: Pittsburghese carries local/working-class pride, mobilized especially after the steel collapse (Johnstone; Agha's enregisterment framework).

## PROFILE 4 — MIDWESTERN AFFLUENT, PLAIN-PERFORMED (VAGUE) REGISTER

*(Compact by design — the intended foil for the contrastive appendix.)* An affluent Midwestern speaker **performing** Profile 3's plainness without its precision — register-slumming / performed plainness. The diagnostic tell is **semantic loss**: the precise word is swapped for a vaguer near-synonym or folksy circumlocution that drops the distinction. The core is informational vagueness, not stance-inflation; the content leaks out even when confidence is flat. Theoretical grounding: Channell 1994 (*Vague Language*, OUP); Cutting (ed.) 2007 (*Vague Language Explored*, Palgrave); Overstreet 1999 (*Whales, Candlelight, and Stuff Like That*, OUP) on general extenders; Agha 2003 (enregisterment); Coupland 2007 (*Style*) and Coupland 2001 on stylization/performed authenticity.

### (a) Phonology renderable in prose
- Largely General American / unmarked; the performance is lexical-pragmatic, not phonological. Occasional folksy g-dropping ("doin'," "somethin'") and casual reductions ("gonna," "kinda") deployed as authenticity props (Coupland 2001 on stylization; Agha 2003).

### (b) Morphosyntax
- **Parataxis-as-authenticity**: short, additive "and… and…" clauses signal plainness (Coupland, *Style*).
- No distinctive regional constructions; the register borrows the *feel* of plainness without Profile 3's diagnostic grammar (any "needs washed" is a borrowed token, itself a performance tell).

### (c) Lexicon and idiom inventory — the core
- **General extenders** (Overstreet 1999; Channell 1994; Cutting 2007): *and stuff, and things, or whatever, or something, that kind of thing, and all that, and so on, and whatnot*.
- **Placeholder/dummy nouns** (Channell 1994; Jucker et al. 2003): *thing, stuff, deal, whatchamacallit, whatsit, thingy, a whole thing, stuff like that*.
- **Vague quantifiers/approximators**: *a bunch, a lot, kind of, sort of, a little, some, a ways, a while, more or less, ballpark*.
- **Folksy circumlocution**: "one of those… deals," "the whole… situation," "a little bit of a… thing."

### (d) Pragmatics and discourse style
- **Vagueness exploiting common ground** (Channell 1994; Overstreet & Yule 1997): the suggestion is that specificity is unnecessary and knowledge is shared — but here it substitutes for content the speaker lacks or won't specify.
- **Performed authenticity / downward style-shifting** (Coupland 2001, 2007; Agha 2003): an elite speaker adopts plain markers to index down-to-earth solidarity.
- **Key contrast with Profile 3**: flat confidence is fine; the leak is informational — the precise distinction is lost even when the tone is calm.

## CONTRASTIVE APPENDIX — PROFILE 3 (PLAIN-PRECISE) vs. PROFILE 4 (PLAIN-VAGUE)

Matched minimal pairs: the same idea rendered once in Profile 3 (monosyllabic, full distinction intact, nothing lost) and once in Profile 4 (small words / vaguer near-synonym / folksy circumlocution, a needed word swapped **at a loss**). The dominant axis is **semantic/lexical loss (precision → vagueness)**, not epistemic stance — Profile 4 is vaguer, not necessarily more or less confident. For each pair: what P3 preserves / what P4 loses.

1. **P3:** "Car won't start. Battery's dead." / **P4:** "The car's doing a thing again." — exact fault (dead battery) vs. unnamed malfunction.
2. **P3:** "It's a sprain, not a break." / **P4:** "It's kind of messed up." — sprain-vs-break distinction vs. undifferentiated "messed up."
3. **P3:** "The grass needs cut." / **P4:** "The yard needs taken care of or whatever." — the specific task (mowing) vs. blanket "taken care of."
4. **P3:** "She's not mad. Just tired." / **P4:** "She's kind of off today." — mad-vs-tired contrast vs. vague "off."
5. **P3:** "Bring the flathead. Not the Phillips." / **P4:** "Bring the screwdriver deal." — exact tool vs. tool category.
6. **P3:** "Milk's sour. Toss it." / **P4:** "The milk's kind of gross, deal with it." — spoiled (specific) + action vs. "gross" + vague directive.
7. **P3:** "Cost forty. Worth twenty. I passed." / **P4:** "It was pricey so I passed and stuff." — exact figures/valuation vs. "pricey."
8. **P3:** "He quit. Found better work in Ohio." / **P4:** "He moved on, job stuff, kind of." — cause + destination vs. unspecified "job stuff."
9. **P3:** "Furnace is out. Pilot light's gone." / **P4:** "There's a thing with the furnace." — component-level diagnosis vs. "a thing."
10. **P3:** "Left at the light. Third house. Blue door." / **P4:** "A couple houses down, the one with the door." — precise directions vs. vague ones.
11. **P3:** "It's not broke. It's just loose." / **P4:** "It's broke or loose or something." — broke-vs-loose distinction vs. collapsed "or something."
12. **P3:** "Meat's done. Take it off." / **P4:** "The meat's more or less done." — done (categorical) vs. approximated done.
13. **P3:** "He didn't lie. He just left stuff out." / **P4:** "It wasn't really a lie, more of a thing." — lie-vs-omission vs. "a thing."
14. **P3:** "Two coats. Let it dry between." / **P4:** "Do a couple coats, give it a bit." — count + interval vs. "couple/a bit."
15. **P3:** "Roof leaks over the porch. Needs patched." / **P4:** "The roof's got a whole situation." — location + fix vs. "situation."
16. **P3:** "Snow's wet, not powder. Gonna be slippy." / **P4:** "Snow's kinda bad, gonna be slick or whatever." — wet-vs-powder + slippy vs. "bad/slick."
17. **P3:** "That's his brother. Not his dad." / **P4:** "That's, like, one of his people." — kin relation vs. "his people."
18. **P3:** "She said six. It's six now." / **P4:** "She said sometime around now-ish." — exact time vs. "around now-ish."
19. **P3:** "Two eggs, over easy. Wheat toast." / **P4:** "Eggs, some toast, that kind of thing." — exact order vs. general-extender order.
20. **P3:** "Trash goes out tonight." / **P4:** "Gotta do the trash thing at some point." — tonight (specific) vs. "at some point."
21. **P3:** "Wrong bolt. Need a half-inch." / **P4:** "Wrong one — need the other size." — exact size vs. "other size."
22. **P3:** "He's late 'cause the 376's shut." / **P4:** "He's late, traffic or something." — named cause vs. "or something."
23. **P3:** "Ice it twenty minutes." / **P4:** "Put some ice on it for a while." — duration vs. "a while."
24. **P3:** "Gas is dear anymore." / **P4:** "Gas is kind of a lot these days and all." — specific (expensive) vs. "a lot… and all."
25. **P3:** "Fence needs fixed before winter." / **P4:** "We should deal with the fence eventually." — task + deadline vs. "deal with… eventually."

27. **P3:** "The dishes need done." / **P4:** "Kitchen needs a little attention." — specific chore vs. "a little attention."
28. **P3:** "Redd up the room." / **P4:** "Tidy up the space a bit, or whatever." — put-in-order (specific) vs. "tidy… a bit."
