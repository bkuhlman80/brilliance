# After the Camera Roll
---
## Chapter 1

Lisa wrapped. She closed her notebook. She gave the team the schedule for the coming quarter.

"Bart and I take Bramble — bot 3 — back to the lab next Monday for upgrade. Battery, FSM, sensors, solar panels. We will deliver the Prokaryote-tier unit in October. The other four bots stay in the house. Jasmine, please keep winding them. Mira — please keep winding them. The protocell line continues even while we are upgrading the one we picked."

She looked at the room.

"Next retrospective is Q2 — October — same place, same time. I will send out a draft agenda four weeks before."

She turned off the camcorder. The red light went out.

Jasmine stood up. She picked up Mira. Mira held Bramble in one hand and the half-empty juice box in the other. The team walked them out to the lot. The summer sky was still light. The parking-lot lights had not yet come on.

Burns hugged Mira. Bart fist-bumped her, which she took seriously and reciprocated with all four knuckles. Marge said something to Jasmine that was not on tape. Lisa said goodbye to Bramble, by name, by lifting one finger and touching the dome of the bot in Mira's hand.

Jasmine drove east on eighth south. The four other protobots were at home. They would be wound on Sunday. The kitchen floor would be warm. Bramble would, by Monday, be at the lab. The household and the lab would each have what they had agreed to have, across the next quarter, while the architecture they were both building grew up underneath them — invisibly, on tape, on Marge's notepad, on Bart's tarot-sized cards, on Lisa's running notebook, on Burns's hands when he wasn't looking at them, and in the long Salt Lake summer light that was, even at nine o'clock, refusing to go down.

## Chapter 2

The team broke. Marge cleared the cut pears. Bart folded the cards back into the cigar box and Cheryl's portfolio cards into their own slot. Burns walked Jasmine and Mira out to the lot. The lot lights were the only lights — the warehouse was dark behind them, except for the inside corner where Lisa had left a lamp on so she could pack the camcorder. The night was cold for October. Mira held Bramble in her right hand and her mother's hand with her left.

Jasmine buckled Mira into the back seat. She set Bramble on Mira's lap. The screen read STOP. The knob was on light. Jasmine had not touched the knob. Mira had not touched the knob. The unit had set itself to STOP during Burns's speech, when no light reached its panels — not on the table where it sat, and not in the minute Burns held it up in his hands. The warehouse, after six, had no light to give.

Burns kissed Jasmine on the cheek at the driver's-side door. He kissed Mira on the forehead through the open back window. He said, "I will be home by ten."

Jasmine said, "OK."

She pulled out of the lot.

The drive home from Smithers in October was not the drive it had been in July. The light was gone — there was no light, only the sodium of the city, and the high cold dark of the western desert sky over the Wasatch, and the parking-lot lights of the strip malls along eighth south giving way to the residential streets after twenty-third east. The trees on Mira's street had lost most of their leaves. The pumpkin on the neighbor's porch had been set out the day before. Inside the house, the radiant heat was on; Jasmine had left it on when they left for Smithers because she did not turn the radiant heat off in October and would not turn it off until at the very earliest June. The four protobots were where they had been when she left. Two by the island. One in the noon sunbeam that had now gone. One under the toaster, in the towel bed, in the spot where the slate had cooled by a few degrees against the slate around it.

Jasmine carried Mira and Bramble in from the car. Mira had fallen asleep in the last three blocks. Jasmine set Bramble on the counter and carried Mira upstairs and put her in bed without changing her clothes, because Mira was four and asleep, and Jasmine was thirty-four and tired. She came back down. She picked up Bramble. She walked to the kitchen.

She set Bramble on the slate in the spot where Bramble had been spending its evenings, which was the corner where the noon sunbeam was at noon, which was the spot Bramble parked when it went to STOP for the night. She did not turn the knob. The knob was on light. There would be no light until morning. The unit would stay on STOP through the night.

The four protobots were where they were.

In three months, the kitchen would look different. The four protobots and Bramble would have been put together into one larger creature at this kitchen table, by Bart, while Mira watched. The towel by the toaster would still be there, but bot four would be inside the bigger thing now, his old shape part of a new one. The slate floor would have one large creature on it instead of five small ones. The household had been a household of five small creatures since April. The next quarter, it would be a household of one larger one.

Jasmine stood in the kitchen for a long minute. She did not turn off the light.

Then she did.

## Chapter 3

"Two minutes," Lisa said, "logistics, and then we're genuinely done, I can see Mira's about out." She picked up her bramble and drank from it.
"Q4 starts tomorrow. The unit goes back to the lab for a weekend during the quarter — Bart'll come collect it, a Friday, back to you by Monday, same as the swap — and there'll be a build on it while it's with us. Sprints through the middle, the usual. Next retrospective's the spring one, end of day, here, same agenda." She paused, and looked at Mira, who was being folded into her coat by Jasmine, and something in Lisa's logistics went deliberately smooth and incurious. "I'm not going to say tonight what the build is. Some of it's a — " she chose the word over Mira's head, " — it's a birthday thing. We'll keep it a birthday thing."
"What's a birthday thing," Mira said, instantly, from inside the coat.
"Coats," Jasmine said. "Arms in."
Lisa crossed to the camcorder. "Spring, then. Camera off." She turned it off, and the small red light on the front of it went out, the way it went out at the end of every quarter, and the warehouse was just a cold room in the fog again, with the work lights on and the meeting gone out of it.

The team broke. Marge cleared the pears and put the kettle, which had never quite gotten its moment, back on its cold ring. Bart folded his clade cards into the cigar box, and Cheryl's twelve into their own slot beside them, the Eukaryote card going back into the deck with two quarters of vocabulary written small on its face. Burns walked Jasmine and Mira out across the lot. Lisa had left a lamp on in the inside corner so she could pack the camcorder, and that lamp and the smeared sodium lights at the lot's edge were the only lights, and the warehouse went dark behind them as they came out into the cold.
The fog had not lifted. It had thickened, if anything, gone grainy under the lot lights, and the cars wore beads of it, and the night smelled of the inversion — woodsmoke and cold metal held down under the lid of warm air the valley couldn't shrug off. Burns carried the unit out for Jasmine because it was too big now to carry and buckle a sleeping four-year-old at the same time, and he set it in the footwell behind the passenger seat where it had ridden up, where the heater would reach it. Jasmine buckled Mira into the back. Mira was most of the way asleep before the belt clicked, the pen still in her fist, M I R A folded into her coat pocket.
Burns kissed Jasmine on the cheek at the driver's door, and kissed Mira on the forehead through the window, and said, "I'll be home by ten," and Jasmine said, "OK," and pulled out of the lot into the fog, the lot lights letting go of the car a few feet out.
The drive home from Smithers in January was not a drive at all, in the way the summer ones had been drives — there was no valley out there to see, no Wasatch, no city spread under the bench, only the next forty feet of surface street arriving out of the gray and the taillights ahead of her swimming and the fog opening exactly as far as she needed it to and no farther, which Jasmine, who had spent a career being given exactly as much as she needed exactly when she needed it and not before, did not find as frightening as the team would have. She came home the surface way. The radiant heat was on; she had left it on; she did not turn the radiant heat off in winter and would not until June. The kitchen, when she carried Mira through it, had one creature on the slate where it had had five in the spring — the low round body's base by the dishwasher, a green pinpoint on it, waiting.
She carried Mira up first and put her in bed in her coat, because Mira was four and asleep and it was January and the coat was warm and the battle of the coat was a battle for a morning with more in it. She came back down. She lifted the unit out of the footwell — it had clicked once, settling, on the way in, a single throw of one switch somewhere inside it, one handoff, a small private accounting it did when it was moved — and she carried it in both arms to the base by the dishwasher and set it down on the base, and it found the contact and settled, and began, with the smallest sound, to take its warmth.
Jasmine stood in the dark kitchen with her coat still on. The fog was at the window, against the glass, wanting in the way it wanted in everywhere, the way the pond wanted into the cell, the way the cold wanted into the warehouse after six. The house held it out. The house held the heat in, and the sleeping child upstairs in her coat, and the man who would be home by ten, and the small round thing on its base by the dishwasher keeping its guest alive in the dark, taking the warmth it would spend tomorrow wearing its groove a little deeper into the only kitchen it had ever known. Everything the house was for, it was holding.
She did not stay long. She was tired in the particular way of a woman who had been up since two with the actual article and had still driven across town through the fog to hear four clever people she loved vote on whether a coin could feel. She put her hand flat on the dome of the thing for a second, the way Mira did, not to do anything to it, just to have her hand on it. It was warm. It was holding what it had picked up all day, and it was being held by the base, and it was holding still.
Then she went up to bed, and left it holding, and turned off the light.
