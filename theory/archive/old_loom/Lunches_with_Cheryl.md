# Lunches with Cheryl

---

## Chapter 0 - the Pitch

I had bought the warehouse eighteen months before I needed it and kept it empty the whole time. It was in the Granary district, on the corner of two of the streets the locals named the way they named streets in Salt Lake — eighth south, fourth west — and it had a fenced patio out back with a real wood trellis already standing on it and nothing growing up the trellis. On the morning of the Pitch I got there first. I let myself in and set a stool in the middle of the floor and put my thermos on a folding chair beside it and waited.

They came in two cars. I heard the doors. They brought everything in through the side door and across the concrete with their arms full — a Roomba, a stack of printed resumes, a laptop, a foam-core board with something taped to it, a cardboard tray of coffees, and a bound printout that had the look of a thing that had been carried around for two months. They set it all on the long table at the back. I did not get up. Burns crossed the floor and put out his hand and I took it, once, and it was the only time I would touch any of them for three years. Then I said, "I am Cheryl. Let's begin."

Bart opened. He set the Roomba on the table and the resumes beside it and started in on who they were and why they had assembled, building toward something that was going to be a joke, and four sentences in I put up one finger.

I did not say anything. I held the finger up and looked at the back wall, where there was a metal door and a fuse box and the corner of a window and nothing else to look at. Bart stopped mid-sentence. Lisa, on his left, went stiller than she already was. I held it about ten seconds, which is longer than it sounds, and then I put the finger down and said, "Keep going."

He kept going. The joke did not land, because they had all reset, and Bart did not push it, which told me something about Bart. He finished and handed off to Lisa.

Lisa walked me through her algorithm without once using a word that would have made it sound like software, which is a harder thing to do than it looks and which I understood, watching her do it, that she had spent the morning learning how to do. I listened with my hands in my lap and did not say anything when she finished.

Burns took the deep time. He stood up to do it and used his hands. He laid out his guess at the twelve — twelve turns in deep time, each one built on the last — and his sequence ended with us, with people, because he did not yet know that the last cards in the portfolio said cities and empires. He was good at it. He was the kind of man who warms when a room turns toward him, and the room turned toward him, and I let it, and watched him without moving my head.

Marge went last. She set a foam-core board on an easel, and there was a Venn diagram on it she had clearly been carrying a long time. Four circles. Extroversion, Intuition, Introversion, Thinking. The four places they overlapped two by two — Perception, Feeling, Sensation, Judgment. The four fallacies in the three-way overlaps, named where the literature had named them: Noble Savage, Blank Slate, Ghost in the Machine, Dealt Hand. And the center, where all four met, which she called Personality Theory.

I looked at it for a long time. It was worth looking at. I looked at it long enough that Marge, who did not strike me as a fidgeter, fidgeted, and long enough that Burns started to open his mouth and Bart put a hand on his father's wrist. Then I said, "That is a beautiful diagram."

"Thank you," she said.

"I am not going to comment on it. I want to be clear about that. I am not going to comment on any of what you have shown me. I am going to give you three things and then I am going to go. The next time you see me as a team will be at your last retrospective, three years from now. I will see each of you before that, one at a time, and I will tell you when. In between you will record the retrospectives on a camcorder and give me the tapes, and I will watch the tapes."

I lifted the portfolio from beside the stool and set it on the table.

"There are twelve cards in here. Each one has a clade on it. They are not all of the clades, and they are in no order except the one I have decided on, which you will work out for yourselves. You will build one robot per card across twelve quarterly retrospectives, and each robot will have a knob on it that controls something about its personality. You will work out what the knob does. You will work out what the personality is. You will work out everything I have not told you, which is nearly all of it. I am paying you to work it out. I am not paying you to be right."

I took the check out of the satchel and set it on top of the portfolio.

"That is the first installment. There are three more across the three years. The warehouse is yours for the duration. You will not pay rent and you will not pay utilities. You will pay for what you build."

I stood up. I had been on the stool an hour and I stood up without the small adjustments people make after sitting that long, because I had decided beforehand that I would. "I am going outside," I said. "Look at what is in the portfolio." And I went out the back door to the patio.

The patio was cold. I sat under the trellis at the metal table with my thermos and did not need to see the table inside to know what was happening at it, because I had made the cards myself and laid them in the order I wanted, and I could see them the way you see a room you have left. Protocell. Prokaryote. Eukaryote. Eumetazoa. Bilaterian. Vertebrate. Mammal. Primate. Bands. Settlements. Cities. Empires. Heavy matte stock, one word a card, the backs blank. They would be turning them over now and finding the backs blank. Twelve words I had carried up and down the canyon for a year and a half, and I had just handed them to four people I had decided were the ones who were going to do it, and I sat in the cold and let them have it.

They came out after a while. Burns had one card in his hand, the first one, Protocell.

"We have a question," he said.

"You have many questions. Pick one."

"Why these twelve."

"I am not going to tell you."

"Why not."

"Because if I tell you, you will build the robots I would have built, and I want the robots you build."

"You are seeding us."

"I am seeding you."

Marge sat down across from me and set the foam-core against the leg of the chair. "May I try the Venn against the clades," she said.

"You may try anything you want against the clades. That is what you are paid for."

She held the diagram up against the Protocell card. She looked at the four circles and the four functions and the four fallacies, and then she set it down. "This does not work," she said. "A protocell has no thinking-or-feeling preference. It has no self. The diagram assumes a self — it assumes a subject doing the typing. None of these things are subjects yet." She set the board down beside her chair with the diagram facing the trellis, the way you set down a thing you are done arguing with.

"It is a good diagram," I said. "It is a different diagram from what you are going to need."

"I know."

"You will keep it for the back of the room."

"I will keep it for the back of the room."

Bart had been watching the board go down. "We should call this place something," he said.

"Smithers," said Lisa.

"Smithers is the team. Smithers is us. We cannot also call the place Smithers."

"We can. We have been calling it Smithers for two months."

"We need a name for other people."

Marge was looking up at the trellis, the bare wood of it with nothing climbing it yet. "Trellis," she said. "The Trellis Lab. Smithers is what we call it. Trellis is what we say to everyone else."

They all looked at her. Burns looked up at the trellis and then at her and laughed from the chest and said, "Trellis. Okay."

The name was settled and the men had moved on to the rest of it, and I did not move on. I was watching Marge. She had found the word looking up at a bare frame, and she had let it go the way you let go of a thing that has left your hands cleanly, and her face when she did it was at rest for the first time all morning, and I watched her face be at rest. There was nothing left on the table to be evaluating. The diagram was down. I watched her sit back in the metal chair and let the others finish deciding, and I went on watching her after there was any reason to, and Lisa saw me do it. I felt Lisa see me. Lisa, who saw everything, saw me watch Marge a beat past the end of the meeting, and I looked at my thermos and gave her nothing else to see, and Lisa said nothing.

I stood and picked up my thermos and my satchel. The check was on the portfolio inside, where I had left it. I walked back through the warehouse, past the long table with the cards on it, and out the front door, and I did not say goodbye.

Outside it was the kind of clear cold that makes the mountains look closer than they are. I got in the car and drove out of the Granary and onto the road that goes up the canyon, and the warehouse got small behind me and then the city did. I had shaken one hand. I would not be in a room with the four of them again for three years. The tapes would come up the canyon to me, and I would sit with them in the winter dark and watch four people do the thing I had set going and would now only ever see later, at one remove, on a screen. I had wanted it that way. I drove up into the trees and left them to it.

## Chapter 2 - Burns

I got there a quarter of an hour early. I like arriving early to a thing I don't yet know the shape of, and the first lunch with Burns was a thing I did not yet know the shape of. I had driven down from Park City on I-80, which is what you drive when you want to get from Park City to Salt Lake, and the drive had not been interesting, and I had not narrated it to myself. I parked a couple of blocks from Smithers on eighth south, walked past the warehouse without looking at it — the building is mine and I did not want to stand in front of it on a Wednesday at noon as if I were my own tenant — and went in the brewery side door at Fisher's.

It was the kind of late-July Wednesday where the heat had set in by mid-morning and meant to stay past dark. I ordered a seltzer at the bar, took it out to the patio, found a table along the back fence near the food trucks. The patio faced ninth south, which had a protected bike lane down the middle of it, and the cyclists were in summer kit, bare arms and bare legs, and there were a lot of them. I watched them roll past for ten minutes. The Wasatch foothills behind the brewery stood in the flat overhead light of late July, the grass on them long burned to gold — a high hard light that would not slant and would not soften, the kind of light that did not so much fall as bear down.

Burns arrived three minutes early. I am used to people being two or three minutes late to a first lunch, and over the years I have filed early people as either nervous or already in love with their work. I did not know yet which Burns was. He came in through the brewery side door looking for me, found me at the back fence, came over.

"Cheryl."

"Burns."

He hesitated. He was deciding between a hug and a handshake. I watched him decide. He picked the handshake. I would have picked it too.

"I'm glad you found the place."

"You told me the place. It was findable."

"That's true."

He sat down across from me. He had brought nothing with him — no folder, no notepad, no rolled printout of the contest document, no nothing. I had also brought nothing. I had meant to bring a small bound notebook with three observations from the protocell tape in it, written down in case I lost the thread of them, and had, on the way out the door of the Park City house, not picked it up, and I am not sure why. I think maybe I had wanted to come to the lunch the way Burns had apparently come to it, which was without props.

"You hungry?"

"I could be."

"There's a truck doing tacos that I have ordered tacos from on probably six occasions and have been satisfied each time."

"That's a recommendation."

"It is."

"I'll get the tacos."

"I'll come with."

We walked to the truck along the back fence. It was painted yellow with green letters and the menu was hand-chalked. Burns ordered two — pork, chicken — and a side of beans, and the woman at the window called the name *Ben* without asking. I ordered the same and gave my own name. We paid separately. We walked back to the table.

"You come here a lot."

"Bart thinks they hung the moon, and it's three blocks from the lab. The beer is very good. The food is fine."

"Honest."

"The food is fine. The tacos are better than most things in this category in this city. But food-truck food in Salt Lake is tasty, never great. I have stopped expecting it to be great. I come for the beer and because Bart will come and he's good company."

He had answered without selling me on the place. The first substantive sentence Burns said at the lunch was an honest description of the food. I had not expected this and I found that I liked it.

The kitchen called his name first. He got up and brought the tray back and started to divvy. Two for him, two for me, beans in the middle. We were missing forks. He got up again and came back with forks and napkins and we unwrapped the tacos and started eating. The pork had some kind of chili thing on it that I did not identify. A cyclist on ninth south rang a bell at another cyclist; the second cyclist rang back. I did not know what either bell meant.

"You walked past the warehouse," he said.

"I did."

"You didn't stop."

"I don't stop at the warehouse between things."

"Why."

I thought about how to answer this. I wanted to answer honestly without making it weighted.

"I don't want you to feel watched between things. The tapes are enough."

He considered this. He picked up his second taco.

"That makes sense."

"Does it."

"Yes. I would feel watched."

"Most people would."

"I'm grateful for the discipline."

"It's not for you. It's for me. If I started dropping in, the lab would become a thing I had a relationship with. I don't want a relationship with it. I want to fund it and let it become what it is."

He nodded. He ate. The light kept doing what late-July light did.

"How's the paper," I said.

I watched him register the question. Bart had told me at the Pitch that Burns was working on a tiktaalik vertebra, and that the paper had been *being written* for over a year. I had filed it.

"The paper is — exists. In a file. The introduction I wrote is the wrong introduction. I am going to throw it away."

"When."

"Soon."

"Soon."

"I have been saying soon for about fourteen months."

"Where is the vertebra."

"On a velvet pad on a shelf in my office."

"How big."

"About the size of a small lemon. Maybe a large kumquat, if you're picturing a kumquat."

"I am picturing a kumquat."

"Then you have it."

"What's wrong with the introduction."

He set his taco down. He looked at me. I had asked the wrong question, which was the right question.

"The introduction frames the vertebra as part of the water-to-land transition story. Which it is. But everyone has written that introduction. The vertebra is also part of a different story — a smaller one, about how the load-bearing architecture of a vertebra changed as fish became things that pressed down on the ground. The smaller story is the one I want to tell. The introduction does not tell it."

"So write the smaller story's introduction."

"Yes."

"When."

"Before the next retrospective."

"That's three weeks."

"It is."

"Should I hold you to that."

"You should not. I will fail. But you can ask me at the next lunch."

"That's fair."

He had given himself a deadline and named the failure mode of it in the same sentence. He had not asked me to hold him; he had asked me to ask. He wanted to be asked. That was useful to know.

"What are you reading, then," I said. "Since it isn't your own paper."

"Geothermal, lately. There's a project the U runs down in Milford — drilling into hot granite to see if you can pull a grid off it. Not my field." He said it more easily than he'd said anything about the vertebra. "I read about it more than I read my own work right now. Which probably tells you something."

"Does it."

"Probably." He let it go.

I watched him eat for another minute. I had a thing to deliver and was not sure how to do it without making it weighted. I decided to just do it.

"I watched the tape twice."

"Of the retrospective."

"Of the retrospective."

He nodded. He had been waiting for this. The tape had gone to me back in May, and he had not heard a thing from me about it until now. I watched him try not to perform calm.

"What did you make of it."

"I made a lot of it. Most of what I made of it I'll say at the individual lunch with whomever it's about. But there's one thing I want to put on the table now, because it has to land at the next retrospective and I won't be there."

"Okay."

"Bart raised something at the close. His worry over Bramble having twelve knobs. The chassis-is-going-to-look-like-a-coffee-maker, or something like that."

"Yes."

"The team filed it light. You laughed."

He did not interrupt.

"He was right. The worry is real. The build is going to produce twelve knobs of differing shapes across thirty-three months and at some point soon the chassis cannot hold them. He has been carrying that worry for longer than the rest of the team has been aware of it. He has been carrying it alone."

I had said the word I had decided in advance to say. I had not made a production of it. It sat between us.

"I want you to take him seriously at the next retrospective. In front of the team. In a way the camera registers. I'm not going to tell you what that looks like; you'll know what it looks like in the room. I just want it on the record that you're going to do it."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I'm uncomfortable with the idea of staging."

"I know."

"I don't perform well to a camera. None of us do. If I try to do a thing for the camera it will read as a performance."

"I'm not asking for a performance. I'm asking for what you would have done if you hadn't laughed."

He took this in. He set his taco down again.

"Okay."

"That's it."

"Okay."

"Don't worry about it. Just have it in your head."

"I will."

I picked my own taco up. I felt the conversation could now move to anything. He, I noticed, felt it too. The five minutes had passed. The push had landed. Neither of us had marked it heavily. The bell-cyclists were back, or different bell-cyclists. The light was the same.

I had been holding something since the taco truck. I took it out now.

"She called you Ben," I said.

"Who did."

"The woman at the truck. She called your order without asking your name, and the name was Ben." I watched him. "Everyone on this side of Eighth South calls you Ben. At the lab you're Burns. On three hours of tape I never heard the team call you anything else."

He set the last of his taco down and sat back, pleased, and the flat light off Ninth South gave me the face the warehouse never did — finer under the weather than the craggy frame promised, the pale gray-green eyes for once not leaving mid-sentence.

"That's Bart," he said. "Bart named the team. I'm Mr. Burns." The chest-laugh came up a beat early, at the frame and not the joke. "Think about it. My son looks at his father — a man who signs the checks, runs the department, gets up in front of the room — and what he reaches for is the rich old fellow behind the desk. The one who owns everybody. *Excellent.*" He put his fingertips together and let them go. "He was right. That's what makes it good. He's been right every day since."

I let him have the laugh. I did not say that the cartoon he was delighting in was a portrait of a man who owns everyone and loves no one — that his son had drawn that, of him — and that he was holding it the way you hold a gift.

"And the others?"

"Oh —" and here he did the thing I would think about on the drive home, which was that he waved it off. "Bart and Lisa, that's an old story, theirs, from before the lab — they were a thing once, it's where the bug started, I never got the whole of it. Marge looked like Marge. Smithers because there's no Smithers." He was already coming back to the part he liked. "But Mr. Burns. He got me in one move."

So I asked about Bart, because I wanted to watch him do it. He did it for the better part of ten minutes. He had no idea he was doing it.

He told me Bart had the best hands on the team. He told me Bart was the one who finished — that Lisa would design something elegant and Bart would build the thing that survived contact, that you could hand the kid a problem on a Tuesday and it would still be getting solved Sunday night because he did not stop. He told me about the twelve-knob worry, and he told it now the way I had asked him to take it — seriously, as evidence — except the evidence it was in service of had quietly become *look how much he cares about the work.* Reliable. Diligent. The best hands in thirty years.

He did not, in ten minutes, tell me one thing about what Bart was *like.* Not a fear, not a pleasure, not a private joke, not a way of being in a room when the room wasn't watching. Everything he said about his son was a thing his son could *do.* He was describing a remarkable instrument, warmly, at length, and he had the whole inventory and not the man — had filled the distance where the man should be with a list of his competencies, and did not know that was what the list was, and would have been hurt past saying if I'd told him.

I was the right person to have seen it and the wrong person to do anything with it, and I knew why: I can read Bart whole off a secondhand inventory because I have spent thirty years at exactly this distance from everyone, watching instead of standing in it. The clarity and the distance are one organ. I made my peace with the trade a long time ago. Or I say I have.

Once, near the end, he started to say "I think he —" and stopped, and for half a second something opened in the weathered face: I could see him hoping I would hand him the rest of the sentence, would tell him the one thing I could not possibly know, that the son loved him. Then he closed it over. "He puts up with his old man," he said, and laughed, and turned the glass. It was a good recovery. Almost anyone would have missed it.

I did not miss it. I also did not take it. I picked at the beans.

He felt the quiet and moved us off it himself.

"My department is in some trouble," he said.

"Tell me."

"The university rebranded from Westminster College to Westminster University in twenty-three. Defensive move — small private liberal-arts schools are under structural pressure nationally and the trustees thought a name change might shift things. It hasn't shifted things. The dean has started making sounds about my research space. About my grants stream. We're not central to the university's brand and we know it."

"Are you worried."

"I'm matter-of-fact. The slow erosion under us is a thing I expect. I don't yet think it's going to take me out before the contest closes. But I notice it."

"When would you know."

"Probably by spring. The dean has to make decisions by the budget cycle."

"If they cut you, what do you do."

"I have savings. I keep showing up at Trellis. The work is the work. If I need teaching, I find teaching. We'd be tight. We wouldn't be in trouble."

"Okay."

He nodded. He had not been seeking reassurance; he had been giving me information. I liked that he had not been seeking reassurance.

Burns said, "I have a question for you that I have been carrying since the Pitch."

"Go."

"You are writing a book about this."

"Yes."

"Twelve chapters. One per clade."

"Yes."

"The team has been wondering."

"The team is right."

"OK."

He thought for a moment.

"I won't tell them you confirmed it."

"Tell them what you want. The book is a book. I am writing it. The contest is the contest. The two are not the same thing, exactly, but they share most of their content. The team can know."

"OK."

I let it sit for a beat. I had not expected him to ask; I had also not been surprised when he did.

"Tell me about Mira."

"Mira."

"Mira."

He smiled. He had not smiled yet at the lunch. The smile was small and complete.

"Mira is — being four. She has named every bot in the house. The protobots aren't Bramble, but they have names too. She's got them on the kitchen floor in a configuration she has decided is correct. She tells me when she sees me what each of them has done that day. The bots have not done very much. Mira has done a lot of telling."

"Names."

"Names."

"What are they."

"I'm not going to tell you the names. The names are hers. If she tells you at the final retrospective, that's her doing. I'm not going to give them to you secondhand."

"That's fair."

"Mira is, in a way I did not anticipate, the thing this project is about. I knew she was going to be in the room. I had not thought through that she would form a relationship with the bots the rest of us would have to take seriously. We were going to take her seriously theoretically, as the household reader. We've ended up taking her seriously practically, because Bramble is now named, and the name came from her, and the name stuck. None of the four of us would have named it. None of the four of us would have named it that. We have a robot called Bramble because a four-year-old said the word twice and we all heard her."

I did not respond. I let the sentence sit. I had heard pieces of the story from Jasmine, and heard Mira use the name across the rest of the tape; the moment itself was off-camera. There was nothing to say that would not have flattened Burns's account.

He finished his second taco. I finished mine. We ate at the beans another minute and stopped before we were done.

"What time is your next thing."

"I have a class at three."

"It's twenty past one."

"I'll bike back. Twenty minutes on the lane."

"Then sit."

He sat. The shadow of the brewery's roof had moved across the patio by about two feet. The cyclists were quieter in the afternoon.

I thought about what I had not asked him. I had not asked him about Lisa. I had not asked him about Marge. I had not asked him about Jasmine. I had not asked whether the contest was the right way for a man to be spending his late fifties, or whether he thought it was going to work. I did not need any of that today. I have years of lunches in which to ask. Today I had wanted the twelve-knob beat to land, and to see what Burns was like on a Wednesday in July, and I had done both, and I could go home.

"I should head out."

"Okay."

We stood and walked together through the brewery toward the eighth south door. He held it open for me. I walked through and turned to him. I put my arms out for a hug; he looked surprised for half a second — we had shaken hands on arrival — and then hugged me. It was brief. He stepped back.

"Next lunch in a few months."

"Yes."

"Marge is next in the rotation."

"I'll tell her."

"Tell her if it comes up."

"Okay."

I walked back to my car along eighth south. I did not turn around. I got in. I drove west and then north onto I-80 and back up the canyon to Park City. The drive was, again, not interesting, and I did not narrate it to myself.

## Chapter 3 - Marge

Marge had picked the place — Coffee Garden, a café on Ninth and Ninth with a south wall of windows and a counter of pastries nobody needed, the kind of room that had decided to be warm and then committed to it. Outside it was the tail of October, the light already low and spent by one o'clock, and the south windows took the little there was of it and laid it down the room in long copper bars. She was already in the corner when I came in, and she had ordered for both of us before I sat down, which I would learn was not rudeness but a fixed feature, like weather.

People hear *clinical psychologist* and build something cool and narrow, and the woman across from me was neither. She was broad and settled and a little rumpled, reading glasses pushed up into gray hair she had stopped coloring on principle, and her face did most of its work around the mouth — a mouth that worried even when the rest of her was at rest, that kept a small readiness for someone nearby to need something. Her cardigan was the color of a brick rained on for forty years. She held her coffee with both hands, close to her chest, the way you hold a tea you're in no hurry to finish, and I understood eventually that she held everything that way, that the posture had nothing to do with the coffee.

I'd come to talk about the vignettes — she was the one who turned the quarter's footage and logs into the small household scenes that reached me between meetings, and I wanted to know how she kept them from going soft, from making the robot into a child. The danger, she said, was not softness. It was flattery. The whole field had a bad habit of being charmed by the thing it was studying, and she spent most of her energy not being charmed. "The minute you're charmed you stop seeing," she said. "Charmed is a kind of lazy." She said it about Bramble. It was also, exactly, a thing a person could say about people, and she said it the way you say something you've had occasion to learn.

I said something then — I don't remember the shape of it, something a little too pleased with itself, about funders staying out of the science — and she set the cup down and looked at me and said, "Oh, you don't believe that," not unkindly, the way you correct a bright student who is hiding. She was right. I had not expected to be caught at my own lunch by a woman drinking drip coffee in a wet-brick cardigan. The *oh, you* had a whole mother in it. It also had a whole argument in it, folded small.

She'd been Mary until she was forty-four. I asked what happened at forty-four and she said Bart happened — that she'd known him for years, and then during the recruitment she sat across his kitchen table and felt him look at her for the better part of a minute and settle on something he didn't say. A week later she was Marge, to him first and then to all of them, and she'd decided not to fight it because the boy was so plainly delighted and so plainly sure. "He sees people as cartoons," she said. "It's alarming when it's you." She didn't tell me which cartoon. She didn't need to. I was already watching the worried mouth and the cup held against her chest like something that might wake, and I had her all at once, the way you get a likeness — the few lines that are the most of a person — and I understood that a kid with a gift had looked at this woman and seen exactly what I was seeing and called it Marge and been right.

I should say plainly that I was rendering her through something by then, because I have spent enough years being honest on tape to know the tell. I was making her larger and warmer than the drip coffee and the rained-on cardigan strictly supported. A clinical eye would have logged a tired academic in her forties, behind her cohort, ordering for strangers because it was easier than sitting still. I logged a hearth. I knew I was doing it. I did it anyway. It is the most Helen thing left in me, and I have mostly stopped apologizing for it.

I tried, once, to turn it around — asked her something real, where she was from, whether the family was still close — and watched her do the thing. A sentence and a half: Provo, big family. Then she was back across the table tending to me, asking whether I'd had anything that morning that wasn't coffee, and the redirect was so smooth and so practiced that I nearly missed it, except that it is my own move. I do it with a camcorder. She does it with a kettle. We are both people who would rather hold the cup than be the cup. I think that was the moment, if there was one.

When the check came she tried to pay — at my lunch — and I let her win the smaller half of the argument, and as she dug for her wallet a manila envelope slid halfway out of her bag: a corner of letterhead I knew from a hundred steeples, a line of form-text about the removal of records. She pushed it back down without a word. It was plainly not a thing for today, and I am good at not asking. We said the kind of goodbye two people say when they've just decided to like each other, which is to say a slightly awkward one, and I drove back up the canyon to Park City — the scrub oak gone rust on both walls of it, the aspens higher up already bare — turning it over the way you turn a stone you've already decided to keep.
