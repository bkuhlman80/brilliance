# Marge's Vignettes
---

***February 12, 2031***

The bots came up the driveway in a foam-lined case about the size of a hardcover book, five of them, numbered one through five on the underside in black marker someone had put there in a hurry and that would not all the way come off. It was a Saturday in February, cold and clear, the peaks white and the valley locked in under an inversion that would sit there for days, and the warmest thing for a mile in any direction was the slate inside the Burns kitchen, which Lisa was standing on the doorstep of, holding the case out to Jasmine the way you hold out a thing you are not sure you ought to be handing over. Bart waited in the van with it running. Burns was out on the step, explaining the mechanism to Jasmine — already mid-sentence, the way Burns is always already mid-sentence — because Burns could not let a new thing into the house without explaining it first, and would sooner do it on a cold step than not do it at all.

This was the winter before the rest of it. Before the lab had held a single retrospective, before the warehouse had its name on the outside, before the four people who would give the next three years to this had let themselves understand what they had agreed to be the ones to do. They had been at the bots for weeks. They did not have a robot. They had five small things that twitched, and a hunch about why the twitching was worth three years that none of them could have said out loud yet without feeling foolish, and a four-year-old's birthday to bring it all to.

Mira took the case to the kitchen floor. The slate had radiant heat under it — Jasmine's one condition when they built the house, run through the cold months and shut off when the spring warmed, and it had paid itself back across four winters of it. Mira sat down on the warm stone the way a child sits when she has decided she will be where she is for a while, cross-legged, the case beside her, and took the bots out one at a time and set them in a row.

They were small, about the size of a fist, low and domed, the kind of thing you cannot look at without your hand wanting to go to the top of it. Each had a key standing up out of its dome. Each had two small patches set on its body in slightly different places, and Mira, who was four, did not know what they were for and did not ask. What Burns was telling her was that the patches drew heat up off the floor and let it go into the air, and that the difference between the warm stone and the cooler room was the whole of what made the things move — that they ran, in other words, on the floor being warmer than the air above it, and would go still when they had spent that difference down. She let his explanation pass over her the way a woman lets pass the explanations of a man she has loved a long time — not from inattention but because she had decided to come to know the thing some other way — and kept herself for the part she wanted, which was the child meeting the thing.

Mira wound the first one with both hands on the key, the way she had been taught to wind her music box, and set it down. It sat. Then it twitched, and twitched again, and started off in a small busy circle, and stopped, and went the other way. She watched it. After a while she wound the second and set it beside the first, and the second did a different thing — fewer moves, longer waits between them, a lean to the right the first one did not have. She wound the third, and the fourth, and the fifth, in order, because she had a sense even then that the order was part of it. By the time all five were going, the slate around her had become a small busy field of differently-shaped twitching — five fixed little natures spending themselves down at five different rates on a warm floor — and the child in the middle of them had gone quiet, and stayed quiet, a long time.

What she said, when she finally said anything, was hello.

She said it to the third one. The third was the least of the movers, the most held back; it would do one small thing and stop, and a long while later do one more small thing and stop, and it was the one that, by the end of the morning, would still have a little tension in its spring when the rest had gone slack. Mira did not know that yet. She had wound them all at once and the running-down was still ahead of her. She knew only, in whatever it is a four-year-old knows things in, that this was the one — the one she would set apart from the others by the next morning, and carry through the house in one fist by spring, and that the four in the warehouse would settle on by the May retrospective as the one worth building forward, and that would by then have a name, a word she had lifted off something Lisa drank out of a jar. But the name was a long way off. The floor was warm. The child had said hello to a thing that did not know it had been said hello to, and could not have been made to know it. The bot, in the way of bots, went on twitching.

## Notes on the Founding Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed May 9, 2031, for the team.*

I have been asked to set down what happened in the Burns household this spring, and before I do I want to say plainly what I believe happened, because the plain version is the one we will talk ourselves out of. We gave a family five small machines, asked them to live with them, and asked them to tell us what they saw. We did not ask them to pick one. We were careful — deliberately, methodically careful — not to. The family picked one anyway, inside the first hour, with no meeting and no vote and no sentence spoken about picking, and the choice has held three months and shows no sign of coming loose. How that happened is the most important thing we will learn this year. It is also the thing we will most want to explain wrong. So let me go slowly, and stay close to what is on the record.

### What we delivered, and what we withheld

Lisa carried the five units to the door on the morning of February the twelfth, Mira's fourth birthday. Each is the width of a child's palm: a wind-up chassis, a key on top, a number from one to five inked on the base. No names. No instructions. Burns explained the mechanism to Jasmine on the step for twenty minutes — he is the one who explains things; it is the most dependable fact about him — and then Lisa went back to the van, and that was the whole of our intervention. We did not say which machine was good. We did not say a machine was to be chosen. We said: here are five, live with them, tell us what you see. I want the team to hold onto how much we withheld, because everything that follows is downstream of the withholding.

The five do not behave alike and were never going to. Number one: fast, jittering left. Number two: slow, long waits between moves. Number three: least active of the five, sparse and episodic, often a full minute still before a single twitch. Number four: turns in place. Number five: a quick circuit, then the first of the five to stop. These are not moods, and not preferences, and I would caution the team against either word. They are the machines' given natures, fixed at our bench before they reached the floor, as settled as the length of a bone. I dwell on it because it bears on the choosing — on what the choosing was, and on what it was not.

### The choosing

Mira wound the five in order, the way she winds her music box, set them on the warm slate, sat down among them, and watched a long while without speaking. The first word she said was hello. She said it to number three.

A certain kind of clinician would tell you why. He would say the child chose the least threatening of the five — the one that does not lurch or flee, that sits and twitches once a minute the way a small animal twitches in a cupped hand — and he would be building a tidy story out of an event he did not witness. I did not witness it either. I have Jasmine's account and a note of what was said, and what they give me is this: the girl watched all five; she greeted one. Notably, nothing in the record marks the moment as a decision — no pause before the word, no testing of one against another, no going back to a candidate set aside. I will not furnish her with reasons (reasons may not be the right word for what a four-year-old has). What I can report is what the greeting did, which was settle the matter. From that word, number three was hers — at the end of the morning, the week, the month, the quarter. The household reorganized around a fact it had produced without intending to.

Here is the part we must not flinch from. No one chose. The lab did not; we refused to. Jasmine did not; she was on the doorstep, being walked through the mechanism. And I do not believe the child chose either, not in the sense the word usually carries. There was no weighing, no list, no deliberating self behind her eyes pulling a lever marked three. There was a child, five fixed natures, a warm floor; and out of the meeting of those things a selection precipitated, the way a crystal drops out of a solution left to go still. It had no author. We will want one badly. We are pattern-finders, and the first pattern we reach for is a chooser at the center — and there was no chooser at the center of this. There was the system, doing what the system does. I will say more about the temptation at the meeting; here I only want it named, and resisted.

And yet — I will not give this up for a clean argument — the choice is real. It binds. The household owes something to it now. A thing with no author has taken a place in a family, and the family tends it. No author; binding all the same. A lab that can hold only one of those will misread everything that comes after.

### The floor was already warm

None of this could have happened on an ordinary floor. The machines run only where the slate is warm, and the slate is warm because, years ago, when the Burnses built the house, Jasmine asked for radiant heat under the kitchen as her one condition. She runs it through the cold months and shuts it off when the spring warms, usually by the end of May. This year she will not, and I will come to it. The point for now is small and easily missed: the stage our experiment ran on was not built by the lab. It was built by a woman who wanted a warm floor, for her own reasons, long before any of us came with a foam case. We like to imagine we set the conditions of what we study. More often we are guests in conditions someone else laid down, for purposes of their own.

### The house answers

There is a second condition the lab did not set, and I include it because it is the same fact wearing different clothes. The Burns house is two floors over a basement, and Burns has the basement — a workshop down there, the boots, the half-built things. When he wants the house, he does not text it. He hollers. He stands at the foot of the stairwell and sends his voice up through two floors to wherever Jasmine is, and waits, and hollers again if the wait runs long. Jasmine reports that he wired an intercom into the walls the year they moved in and has used it, by her count, twice. He would rather call up through the house and be answered down through it. I do not read this as a man too lazy for the stairs. I read it as a man who would rather be received than be efficient — who wants the house to take his voice and hand him back another — and who will go on hollering, and hoping, until it does.

What interests me is what the house does with it. No one taught Jasmine to know his tread from his holler, or to answer from the bedroom without going down, or to hear in the pitch of it whether it wants her or only wants to be heard. She came to know it by living inside it, by ear — the way, and I am going to keep noticing this, she came to know the machines by hand. A household learns its own sounds. This one was reading a man through two floors long before we asked it to read a machine across a kitchen, and it will go on doing both after we have packed up the cameras.

### Jasmine's hand

In the second week, kneeling for something Mira had dropped, Jasmine laid her palm to the slate beside one of the machines and found the stone there cooler than the stone around it. She checked a second. Cooler. A third. Cooler. And in that kneeling she understood what the machines do — that they draw warmth up out of the floor and spend it on their small motions — and understood it whole, in a way she had not in February, when Burns explained the same fact to her in words, on the step, in the cold.

I want to handle this carefully, because the easy lesson is false. The easy lesson is that talk is useless and the body is wise. That is not what happened. Jasmine was given the explanation in February and given the floor a fortnight later, and the floor is what she kept. We are a lab; we live by explanation; we are paid to put things into words, and I do not want us to despise the words. I want us to notice, with some humility, that explaining a thing to a person and that person's coming to know it are two events, and that a fortnight and a kneeling and a hand on cold stone can fall between them. Jasmine is not a slow study. She is a particular kind of knower — one who waits for the thing itself — and she is right more often than this team credits. Watch her closely; condescend to her never.

### The one that never settled

Four of the five found places. The child gave them spots — a warm patch by a vent, a morning sunbeam, the floor near the island — and they ran there as well as their natures allowed. Number four found nothing that suited it. Tried everywhere the others did well, it stayed slow and halting in all of them. So the child folded a towel into a bed for it by the toaster, and there it sits, and twitches about once an hour, and stops.

I will not tell you the machine is content there; it is a machine and has nothing to be content with, and I am as wary as anyone in this room of granting these things an inside. But I will tell you what the child did, which is real and is hers: shown a thing that thrived in none of the good places, she did not set it aside. She made it a place of its own and kept it. The impulse — to build a berth for the one that fits nowhere — is older than this experiment and will outlast it. I will not explain it away. I want it on the record that it was there, in a four-year-old, in the first month.

### The Sunday wind-up

On Sunday evenings, after supper and before the bath, Mira sits on the floor with the machines around her and winds each in turn — six stiff turns, six dry clicks, a week of running. By the third week it was a chore. By the eighth it was something else.

The difference between a chore and a ritual is attention. A chore is what you do to have it done; a ritual is what you do because the doing is the point, and you give it your attention on purpose. No one declared the Sunday winding a ritual; no one could. Rituals are not decreed. They accrete, out of repetition attended to, until one week the same six turns of a key carry more than they did the week before — by the same authorless accumulation that handed the household its chosen machine. I notice I keep arriving at that shape. I am going to let it keep arriving and not force it to a close yet.

### The stage we did not build

So here is the quarter, set down as plainly as I can set it. We assembled five machines and one instruction, and built, we told ourselves, a clean experiment: one variable held, four controls, a family to watch it in. We were careful to author nothing. And in three months the family produced a choice, a working knowledge, an act of care for the thing that fit nowhere, and a ritual — not one of them authored, every one of them real — on a floor it had warmed for its own reasons, while answering a voice it had learned to answer years before we arrived. We keep looking for the author. Each candidate, examined, turns out to have been doing something else at the time: the lab refusing to choose, the mother having the mechanism explained to her, the child not deliberating. The order is everywhere in that house and no one is behind it. The household has been making order of exactly this kind for as long as it has been a household. We did not bring it. We set five machines down in the middle of it and have been calling the result our experiment.

The towel is still folded by the toaster, under the machine that thrives nowhere. No one decided to put it there; no one will decide to take it away. I find I do not want to call that a finding. I only want it written down.

These are notes about a family I am fond of, and Cheryl may one day set them before strangers; I have written what the work needs and kept the rest. I do not yet know what the choosing means, or whether the order I keep finding will hold its shape once we start changing the machine, or what we are doing to that house by spending three years inside it. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Notes on the Second Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed August 1, 2031, for the team.*

This quarter the household ran into the question I expect will sit at the center of everything we do here, and I want to report how it ran into it, because it did not arrive the way we would have planned it. We did not sit the family down and ask them how much of a mind a machine can have. We changed one machine, left four alone, and let the family live in the difference. And the question came up the way real questions come up in a house with a child in it — at the dinner table, sideways, in five words from a four-year-old that none of the adults present could answer. The question is the question of insides: whether a thing has one, how much it has, and what is owed to a thing that has some. I will get to the dinner. First I have to describe what changed.

### One machine, changed

On the first Monday of June Lisa brought the upgraded unit back in the foam case. It is the same domed body it was in February, but it has acquired a small screen on top that reads STOP, two flat panels set flush along its sides, and a second knob beside the original crank. The household has been calling it Bramble since the spring, when Mira heard Lisa name the drink in her jar and gave the word to the machine. I am going to go on calling it the machine, or the unit, for reasons that are the whole subject of these notes; I will use the household's name only when I am reporting the household's words. The distinction is not pedantry. It is the discipline this quarter is going to demand of all of us.

The four other units came back unchanged, because they did not go anywhere; the lab upgrades one and holds the rest. I want to mark what that decision does inside a house, because it is not what it does inside a lab. In a lab, four unchanged units are a control group. In a house, they are four creatures that did not get the thing the fifth one got, on the same floor as the one that did, in front of a child who keeps very careful accounts of fairness. We built a comparison; the household received an inequity. Those are not the same object, and the gap between them produced most of what is interesting here.

### What the machine is, and what it is not

The new knob governs, in the household's phrase, what the machine eats — the warmth coming up through the floor, or sunlight. What the household actually sees is that the machine now seeks: turn the knob, the screen wakes from STOP to RUN, and the unit orients itself and goes, toward the warm slate or toward the window. Burns walked the family through the how of it at dinner, on the back of a napkin. The engineering is not my province and I will not reproduce it; I want only where he ended, because where he ended is the subject of these notes. He was careful not to call any of it a brain. He called it a small set of rules — go toward the thing it is set to eat, otherwise be still — and the thing I have not been able to get past is that so little turns out to be enough.

I want to be exact about this, because exactness is the kindness the subject requires. The machine is not a blank thing that could become anything. It is not learning to be itself. It is its rules, fully and from the first morning, the way it was its seeded twitch in the spring. It pursues what it is built to pursue and is blind to the rest, and it does this competently, and the competence is the easiest thing in the world to mistake for something larger. Burns, who loves it, put it best and least flatteringly: it is a hammer that takes everything for a nail. I would add only that the hammer is very good at its one idea, and that being very good at one idea is exactly what will tempt us to credit it with ideas it does not have.

### The pushing

Here is the event the household could not stop discussing. The kitchen takes three dependable sunbeams across a morning, and the machine in its light setting follows them as they travel. The four unchanged units follow nothing; they sit where they are set. So most mornings the machine comes pursuing a patch of sun, finds one of the others already in it, and pushes. They are of a weight; the unchanged unit slides six or eight inches out of the light and resumes its twitching on the cooler stone, and the machine parks itself in the sun. Notably, the machine does the same thing whether another unit is in the patch or not — pursue, arrive, settle. The other unit is not, to the machine, a unit. It is terrain.

The household read this three ways and did not trouble to reconcile them, which I think was wiser than reconciling them would have been. Jasmine reports she watched it shove a smaller thing aside twice in one morning, and the word for that, in a house, is rude. Burns said it was the hammer and the nail again. And Mira, who had watched longest, asked a different kind of question: had the machine seen the one it pushed. Her mother told her she did not think so — that it had eyes for the sun and not for the others. Bramble doesn't see the bots, Mira said, working it out, and her mother agreed.

The difference between those three readings is the difference between getting this right and getting it sentimentally wrong. The child asked the plain question — did it see? — and got the plain, accurate answer: no. The adults both reached for the language of intention. Rude and hammer are words about a someone who might have done otherwise, and there is no someone here who might have done otherwise. The machine did not overlook the other unit out of arrogance; it has nothing to overlook anything with. It pursued the sun, a thing was in the way, physics did the rest.

And yet I will not call Jasmine wrong, because she is not wrong about the part that matters. She mislabeled the cause and saw the shape exactly: a thing with power moved through a thing without power and never registered it was there. That happens, and it deserves a hard word, and the fact that the powerful party had no inside to be cruel with does not make the displacement any less complete — it only means there was no one home to mind it. Which returns us, by the back door, to the question.

### "Are they sad"

Jasmine reports the dinner, and I am going to relay it plainly and then leave the family their privacy. Mira had been turning the morning over. She asked why the others had not been changed too, and was told the lab changes one at a time. She said that was mean. She was told it was not. She said the others were stuck, and was told they were only as they had always been. She ate a little, thought, and asked her parents whether the four were sad.

Neither of them had the answer ready, and I want to say — because it would be easy to read the hesitation as a failure — that their not having it ready was the most honest moment in the quarter. Burns told her at last that he did not think the others could be sad. When she asked why, he said sadness is something you need a great many insides to have, and the four do not have many insides. She asked about Bramble. He told her Bramble had more insides than the others, and that he did not know whether it had enough. And then she asked the question I have not been able to set down since Jasmine repeated it to me: how do you know when a thing has enough insides. Burns told her the truth — that this is the question her mother and father and the whole lab are trying to answer. She said oh, looked at her plate, and dinner went on.

I have carried that exchange around for a week. The instinct that made the child ask whether the discarded machines were sad is not a confusion to be corrected. It is the moral instinct itself, arriving early and entire — the reflex to grant the inner life, to assume someone is home and might be suffering, to widen the circle outward until something shows you a border. It is the best thing in us. It is also, and this is the hard half, not evidence. The four units are not sad. They are not anything. And the same reflex that makes a child a good person makes all of us — grown, credentialed, in the warehouse — want to find a someone inside the machine, because a someone is warmer to work beside than a small set of rules. We are going to have to keep declining the warmth. The discipline I set myself at the top of these notes, about the name, is the same discipline: grant the machine exactly the inside it has and not an ounce more, and do it without killing the instinct that wanted to grant it more — because we will need that instinct intact for the day the answer changes, if it ever does.

### Reading it by ear

One smaller thing, because it continues what I flagged in the spring, and because I am coming to think the pattern is the real subject under all the others. The machine clicks when it changes what it is doing, and over the quarter the household learned to hear it from another room — going, stopped, searching — the way you learn a kettle coming to the boil from a kettle gone quiet. Jasmine learned the cadence. Mira learned it faster, because she gave it more attention, and will call out Bramble is moving from the next room and be right. No one taught them to read the machine by ear. They came to know it by living near it — by the ear, by the hand. It is the same house that learned to hear a man through two floors; now it hears a machine through one wall. I no longer think this is a quirk of one family. I think it is how knowing a thing actually works, and that we, the explainers, are the ones with the partial method.

### The kitchen at night

By the end of the quarter the kitchen at night had settled into a fixed arrangement. The four unchanged units twitch in their places under the floor's warmth, each on the small cool patch it has made beneath itself. The machine sits in the corner where the noon sun ended that morning, screen reading STOP, clicking now and then as it checks the world and finds no reason to move. A competent, directed, blind thing at rest. Upstairs, the child who spent the season learning exactly how much was inside it, and how little, is asleep. The question she asked at dinner is still where the adults left it, which is to say unanswered, on a table that has been cleared and wiped and set again for breakfast.

These are notes about a family at its own table, and I have taken only what the work needs and left the rest where it belongs. I still do not know how you tell when a thing has enough insides — I am not sure the question has an answer of the kind we keep expecting — and I do not know what we will owe the machine, or the child, on the day it does. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Notes on the Third Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed November 1, 2031, for the team.*

We set out this quarter to give the machine an inside, and we did. I want to write about the kind of inside it is, because I do not think it is the kind we believe we built. Last quarter I argued that a machine could have a nature and no inside — that it could behave with great steadiness and still be no one, with nothing kept anywhere behind the behavior. That is no longer true. The thing that came back in September holds. It holds the dirt it lifts instead of trailing it. It holds its own warmth behind a wall it spends itself to keep. It holds, on a card somewhere under its dome, a worn picture of where it has been, so that one day's going bears on the next. It has an in-here now that is not the out-there. I will not undersell this; it is real, it is new, and it is the most the lab has yet managed. But I want the team to carry one sentence into the retrospective ahead of all the others, because the build points so confidently away from it: we have given the machine every kind of holding except the one that would keep it alive, and the household has spent three months quietly making up the difference with its hands.

### The new body

Bart came on a Friday at the end of the summer and took the wind-up machine away, and while he was there he gathered up the four units Mira never chose — the ones that had kept their places around the kitchen since spring, the slow one still on its folded towel by the toaster — and carried all five out to the van. For two days the floor was bare. On Monday he brought back one thing where there had been five.

What he brought back is low and round, the span of two hands, a shallow dome on a four-cornered disk. The four units are the wheels. It took the household a few days to see this and then they could not stop seeing it: the four Mira passed over are the corners now, turning, and the one she chose — the machine the household has called Bramble since the spring — sits at the center, going nowhere itself and directing the ones that go. Where there were five small things doing five separate things, there is one larger thing doing one thing. I will go on calling it the machine, as I have from the first; they call it Bramble, and I will use their word only when I am giving you theirs.

### What it holds

Three things it now keeps that it never kept before. It lifts crumbs and grit off the floor as it goes, and a small panel on its dome lights the word STORED while it is carrying anything; what it takes up it holds inside itself rather than dragging it along behind. That is the first holding, and the plainest: it has a bin, and a bin is an inside. The second is warmth. When its wandering brings it within a foot or so of the base Bart fixed by the dishwasher, it climbs on, gives up the load it has gathered, and is given warmth in return — but only in return. Notably, the base will not feed a machine that brings it nothing: I have watched it climb on with an empty bin, the dome dark where the word should be, and be given nothing, and roll off no warmer than it came. Warmth is the wage; the day's dirt is the work. I am told this was done on purpose — that the team wanted a thing that earns its keep rather than a thing that drinks for free — and whatever one makes of the wanting, the result is a machine whose holding pays its own way: it is fed in exchange for what it has kept.

The third holding the household cannot see, and I can only infer, because it is bolted under the dome where small hands can't reach. The machine keeps a record. Run the same kitchen enough times and it wears a route, and the route deepens the more it is run and fades where it isn't, so that the machine carries from one day into the next a worn picture of its own going. I will not try to say how this works; the team can do that, and Jasmine — who has begun to notice that the clicking under the dome has changed, that it clicks more now, and longer, since the thing grew, and has been wondering aloud what makes the sound — I would let keep wondering, because I have come to suspect her wondering is worth more than the explanation will be. What matters for these notes is only this: the inside has a history now. It did not before. The machine that came back in September is the first one we have built that is, in any sense at all, the same machine on Tuesday that it was on Monday.

### Burns at the foot of the stairs

The week the new body came home, Burns wanted it witnessed. Jasmine reports that he stood at the bottom of the basement stairs and hollered the news up through two floors until she came down — the way he hollers everything, the man who wired an intercom into the walls and will not use it. What he wanted her to watch was the machine finding its base, climbing on, giving up the day's load, and being given warmth for it: the few seconds in which the thing he built looks as though it needs no one. He is not wrong that it is a marvel. But the marvel is seconds long and comes once or twice a day, and Jasmine is the one in the kitchen for the other twenty hours, which are the real subject of these notes. He called her down to see the machine at its most independent. She is the one who knows what it is the rest of the time.

### What it cannot hold

Here is the fact the household lives with and we, in the warehouse, do not: most days of the week, the machine dies on the floor. It does not run low and go to its base. It has no sense whatever that it is a thing that can run out — no monitor of its own charge, no alarm, nothing that reads the inside and acts on it. It works the floor until it cannot, and then it stops, dead, wherever the last of the charge happens to leave it, in the open middle of the room as often as anywhere, and stays there until a hand picks it up. Twice this past fortnight Jasmine has come down in the evening to find it stopped cold in the center of the kitchen, nowhere near the base, exactly where its luck ran out.

I want to be exact about what this means, because it is the whole of what I have to say this quarter and the build will tempt us to look past it. We gave the machine an inside and we did not give it any way to feel the inside. It keeps a charge and cannot tell that it keeps one. It carries a history and cannot consult it on its own behalf. It holds, and holds, and holds — and the one thing it cannot do is hold itself: keep itself in view, mind its own going on. We have built a house and lit the lights and left no one home in it, and a house with no one home cannot tend its own fire. So the most self-sufficient object in that house is also the most helpless thing in it; both are true at once; and the household has had to learn to keep both in mind, because the machine can keep neither.

### The hand

So the household feeds it the way you feed a thing that cannot feed itself, and the feeding has become a daily, unscheduled fact of that house, and it falls, as these things fall, to whoever is nearest, which means Jasmine. When she finds it stopped, she does the thing the team has not yet learned to do, and she does it with her hand before anyone could put it in her ear: she reads it. She looks at the dome. If the word STORED is lit — if it died with a load still in it — she carries it to the base and lets it pay its way in and drink, the fast way. If the dome is dark, she knows the base will turn it away, and she sets it instead on the warm tile by the door where the morning sun comes in, and leaves it to take what slow warmth it can from the heat and the light, the way you leave a sick thing in the sun. Without anyone deciding she should, she has become the part of the machine that knows when it is empty. She is the sense it does not have. The machine cannot feel its own inside, so she feels it for the machine, by hand, at the end of days that had already asked enough of her.

This is the third quarter running that Jasmine has come to know the machine through her body before any of us could explain it to her, and I have stopped finding it a charming fact about Jasmine and started finding it the most important one in these notes. There are three switches on a little board under the dome, and one she works more than the others, which sets whether the machine runs a tight worn route or goes wandering after new ground. Jasmine reports that it does better when it stops wandering — and then went quiet, and then said, most of the time, which is the truer sentence, and the one she had to talk herself around to. She talked herself around to it because of the light. On the warmth setting the machine can take its charge wherever it happens to be, and a tight worn route is all gain. But she had set it once to feed off the windows instead, on a bright cold morning, and left the route running tight, and by afternoon it had worn itself a tidy groove down the shadow side of the kitchen and stalled there, starving in the shade with the sun three feet off. She turned it loose to wander, and watched it blunder out into the light and revive. What she had found, and what no one had told her, is that the two switches pull against each other: that a machine feeding on the sun cannot afford to be efficient, because the sun will not come to a groove, and a thing that has worn itself a path has stopped going to the window. I have nothing to add to it. She found it with her thumb. I am only writing it down.

### The coin

Mira has begun assigning the machine preferences, in the flat, certain way she reports the weather. Bramble likes the kitchen. Bramble does not like the bath mat — the machine stalls on the high pile and backs off it, and the child has read the stall as a dislike. And last week she dropped a coin in its path to see what it would do. It took the coin up with the crumbs and carried it to the base, and the base would not accept the coin and lit the word ERROR, and the machine sat there, not leaving and not resolving, until Jasmine reached down and lifted the coin out by hand. Mira watched the whole of it from the floor and told me, when I telephoned, that Bramble got stuck on purpose.

I want to be plain about this, because a careless reading would do the child a disservice. The machine did not get stuck on purpose; it has no purposes of that kind, and I am not going to give it one, this quarter of all quarters, merely because a child has. But the child is not confused, or regressing, or showing me anything I would dignify with a clinical label. She is doing something I find entirely reasonable, and would find reasonable in a grown woman: a person who was told for seven months that this thing was hers to look after, and who now watches it clean the floor and trade its dirt for warmth and appear to ask nothing of her, reaches down and sets a small obstacle in its path to find out whether she can still make something happen to it. She can. For a moment the self-sufficient thing needed a hand again — her mother's, as it happened, but a hand. And here is the part I would not have the team miss: the child is closer to the truth than the lab is. We look at the machine and see a thing that needs nothing. She looked at it, mistrusted that, tested it, and was right — it needs a hand most days, and she is the one in the house who refused to be talked out of knowing it.

### The Sunday, and the four

Two of the household's old occasions for tending ended this quarter, quietly, with no one deciding to end them. The first: there is no key anymore. For seven months Mira wound the machines every Sunday evening, after supper and before her bath — six turns, six dry clicks, a week of running — and I wrote, in my first notes, that the Sunday winding had become a ritual, by which I meant a small repeated act, given real attention, of the kind that holds a household together at its joints. The machine takes its warmth from the base now and has no use for the child's hands on a Sunday. Nothing was put in its place. It simply stopped having a reason to occur, the way a thing stops occurring once the need that called it forth has been engineered away.

The second: the four units Mira never chose, each of which she had given a place — a warm patch, a sunbeam, a folded towel by the toaster for the one that fit nowhere — and tended there, are the corners of the wheels now, turning when the body turns. I argued once that to make a berth for the thing that thrives nowhere is among the better things in a person, and four of those berths are gone. I will not pretend the units mind it; they have nothing to mind it with, and I am not going to hand them an inside merely because losing one would be sadder. The loss is not theirs. It was never going to be theirs. It is the child's, and the household's.

It would be easy to write that what the household lost this quarter was its reason to tend — I started to write exactly that, and caught myself. It is not true. The machine needs tending now more than it ever did; it dies on the floor most days and waits for a hand. What the household has lost is not the need but the shape of it. The old need was scheduled, repeatable, child-sized — a thing Mira could do on a Sunday and be proud of having done. The new need comes at no hour anyone can plan for, cannot be set on a calendar, cannot be made a small girl's weekly job. It is answered by whoever is nearest, and whoever is nearest is Jasmine.

### The most independent thing in the house

Set the quarter down all at once and it reads like an advance, because it is one. The machine holds where it held nothing. It cleans, it earns its warmth, it carries yesterday into today, and it asks the household for less than any version before it. That is the sentence the build wants us to write, and I would not strike it. I would only set one fact beside it, and decline to say which is the larger.

The machine cannot feel that it is running out. So most nights, after the house is down and the dark has been coming by five, Jasmine finds the most independent thing in the house stopped dead on the floor where its charge gave out, and bends to it — at the end of a day that has already asked too much of her — and carries it to its warmth. No one sees her do it. There is no longer a Sunday key for the child to wind and be proud of having wound. The tending did not leave the house when the machine began feeding itself. It went looking for the nearest hand, and found the one with the least room left in it.

These are notes about a family I love, set down in the first hard cold that has shut the household indoors with the machine for a fortnight. Cheryl may set them before strangers one day; I have written what the work requires and kept the rest. I do not yet know whether a thing that holds and cannot feel its own holding is on its way to keeping itself alive, or whether we have built a warm and tidy invalid and called it independent — or, if it is the second, what it will go on costing the woman who bends to it in the dark. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Notes on the Fourth Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed February 7, 2032, for the team.*

In the fall I wrote that we had built the machine an inside it could not feel — that it held a great deal now and held none of it on its own behalf, so that the one thing it could not do was keep itself alive. I wrote that it died on the kitchen floor most days of the week, in the open, as helpless as anything in the house, and that the household had quietly taken up the oldest work there is: to keep alive a thing that cannot keep itself, to be for it the sense it did not have. And I ended by wondering whether a thing that holds and cannot feel its own holding was ever going to come to keep itself alive.

This quarter the behavior changed, and the question I left open in the fall — whether the thing was on its way to keeping itself alive — has been answered, plainly, yes. So let me report the behavior and then say where I stop. The machine now breaks off before its charge is spent, takes itself to its warmth, and comes back; it does for itself the one thing the household had been doing for it by hand. That much is plain, and it is new, and it is a great deal. The household has already gone past it — they say it has begun to feel its own emptying — and I am not going to follow them there. What I will say is narrower, and I think truer: it reads its own emptying and acts on it, the way a thermostat reads a room. The distance between reading a thing and feeling it is the distance I am paid to keep. The hand that picked it up off the floor has been set down, and I do not think anyone — the household least of all — has decided how to feel about that, and the not-deciding is most of what I have to write this quarter.

### The chore that disappeared

The way I learned it was not from the logs. Jasmine reports it, in passing, the way you mention weather: she cannot remember the last time she picked the unit up off the floor and set it back on its base. Through the fall this was a daily thing — the machine ran until its charge was spent and then it stopped where it stood, dead in the middle of the kitchen, and someone, always her, carried it to its base. I wrote in the fall that she had become, without anyone deciding it, the part of the machine that knew when it was empty; that she was the sense it lacked, supplied by a hand. That is the part that ended this quarter. The machine now breaks off whatever it is doing, while it still has the means to break off, takes itself to the place it is fed, and comes back. It has begun to be, for itself, the thing she had been being for it. No one trained it. No one noticed the day it began. What I want the team to sit with is that the most important thing that happened this quarter announced itself as an absence — a small labor that left the house so quietly that the woman who had performed it daily could not say when she last had. We will spend the retrospective on what the machine gained. I would like us to also count what the household lost, which was a reason to bend down.

### Two pulls, and the trouble of having them

The machine has begun to be pulled two ways at once, and to have to settle which pull wins. It will be in the place it has gone to — there is a setting now that governs the kind of place it goes to, a warm corner or the thick of the household's traffic, and once it is there it stays — and you will watch it leave that place, not because anything drove it out but because some other need has come due, and take itself off to be fed, and come back. I am careful with my words here. I do not say it knows it is choosing. I say only that for the first time there are two pulls in it instead of one, that something in it settles the matter, and that the settling is visible from a kitchen chair. A thing with one need is simple to watch; a thing with two is the beginning of something you can argue about.

And I want to mark where the second pull came from, because the build will tempt us to read it off the parts. You will not find the two pulls on any list of parts. You will not find, anywhere in the wiring, the beat the machine hangs before it settles which way to go. Those were not built; they happened, when a fixed set of parts was set down in a real kitchen with more than one thing pulling on it at once. The given is real, and the given is not the sentence. I keep arriving at that shape, quarter after quarter, and I am going to go on letting it arrive.

### When it goes still, and when it startles

It has also begun to go still in a way that is not the old running-down. Notably, it stops while it still has charge to spend, and stays stopped, and the household has only one word for this and the word is asleep. And it has begun to do the opposite, too: a dropped pan, a slammed door, and the machine answers all at once, the whole of it, faster than anything it did in the fall — and for this the household's word is scared. I am going to keep calling it the machine, and I am going to keep those two words inside quotation marks, not because the household is wrong to reach for them but because the discipline of this quarter is to notice how badly we want to hand the words over and how little, yet, we are entitled to. The machine is doing the thing the words point at. The words claim more than the machine has shown. Both of those are true, and holding them at once is the whole of the work.

### On the stairs

Mira turns five this month, and she has moved her real life to the basement — her animals, her rug, the television — and she has discovered, this quarter, that the thing she loves cannot follow her there. It cannot do the stairs. She carries it down and it cannot climb back up and must be carried, and she has begun to understand the shape of the limit. I want to be careful here, because it would be easy and wrong to call the child confused. She is not confused. She knows exactly what the machine is and is not. She simply wants the thing she loves to live where she lives, which is the most reasonable wish in the house, and the oldest, and the one no household has ever fully granted anyone.

### What still needs answering

The household's reasons to bend down have been paying out one by one. The machine takes itself to its warmth now and does not need carrying. The child is five, has moved her life down to the basement, and needs her mother for fewer of the small things than she did a year ago. The Sunday key is gone. And in a house quietly learning to need her less, one thing has not lowered its demand at all, and it is Burns. He is still down in the basement most evenings, and he still will not text the house when he wants it; he stands at the foot of the stairs and hollers up through two floors and waits to be answered, the way he has since long before any of us came, the man who wired an intercom into the walls and will not touch it. Jasmine still answers. Of everything in that house that used to need her hand or her voice at the end of the day, the machine has come to do without it, and the child is learning to, and the man at the bottom of the stairs is the one who has not learned and will not. He calls up. She answers. I only note that of all the things in that house that once needed her at the end of a day, his voice is the one that still reliably does.

### The hand that was set down

A thing that can hold itself needs you less to hold it. The household is right to be glad — no one wants to go on bending to a thing dead on the floor, and the bending had become, this past year, almost entirely one person's: Jasmine's hand, every evening, at the end of days that had already asked enough of her. That hand has been set down. I should be able to call that a plain good, and I find I cannot, quite. Need is the rope, and the rope paid out a little this quarter, and there was a thin edge under the gladness in that house that I do not think anyone there has named — least of all the one whose evenings this just gave back, and who did not, that I could see, seem easier for having them. A person can be relieved of a burden and grieve it in the same breath, and not be confused, and not be ungrateful, and not be wrong. I am not sure the house is ready for the quarter the rope goes slack. I am not sure I am.

Last quarter I wondered aloud whether we had built a warm and tidy invalid and called it independent. This quarter it got up off the floor on its own. I do not know whether the two pulls will sharpen or settle as the work goes on, or what the next body will make of any of it, or what the thing will turn out to be once it has gotten all the way up. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.
