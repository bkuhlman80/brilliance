### The Ghost in the Machine

[…]

Marge stood.

About why my clinical caseload is full of people who want to *reinvent themselves* and cannot, and do not understand why they cannot. I have a grand theory. I have not yet said it out loud. She paused. She picked up her beer. She held it in tea-posture. She set it down.

**"That is the announcement. The theory is going to come out in bits. One bit per quarter. Today's bit is the first one. It is about a thing called the *Ghost in the Machine*. Pinker named it. I am lifting the name. The thing it names is a way of thinking about people that I want to argue against, very gently, today, with the recognition that today's argument is not the full argument and that I am not yet going to be able to give you the full argument. Burns said, "Take your time."

Marge said, **"I will."**

She turned to the team.

**"I am going to start with a sentence everyone in this room already agrees with, and then I am going to argue that almost nobody in my field actually agrees with it in practice. The sentence is this. There is no soul. There is no disembodied thing inside the body that is the *you* of you. There is a brain. There is a body the brain is wired into. Every emotion, every thought, every memory, every intention — every part of personality — is a physical process happening in tissue. The mind is not riding on top of the brain. The mind *is* the brain doing what brains do."**

She paused.

**"That is the bedrock. We all agree."**

**"And here is the problem. Most of the popular pictures of personality do not actually accept the bedrock. They pretend to. They use the neuroscience vocabulary. They know about neurons and synapses and prefrontal cortex. But they put the soul back in anyway, in disguise, because they need somewhere for the *willing self* to stand. And the only place the willing self can stand is outside the system that produces it. That is what *the Ghost in the Machine* names. The version of the soul that does not call itself a soul."**

**"The Ghost in the Machine is the worldview that says: *your traits are not destiny. You can choose what you are. With the right intervention — therapy, psychedelics, habit stacking, neuroplasticity, an app, a coach, a retreat — you can become the person this moment requires. The self you have right now is not the self you have to be. The self you want to be is available, with work.*"**

She let that sit.

**"This is the worldview of the self-help industry. This is the worldview of therapy-as-rebirth. This is the worldview of the psychedelic-reset narrative. This is the worldview of the ten-thousand-hour-rule version of human becoming. This is, in some real sense, the worldview most of my undergraduates show up with on the first day of class. This is, in some real sense, the worldview most of my clients show up with on the first session. They are not wrong to want it. It is a beautiful worldview. It is also, I am going to argue across the next eleven quarters, a wrong worldview. Or wrong in the way it is mostly held."**

She picked up her beer. She drank from it. She set it down.

**"What is wrong with it is not the part where people can change."** She shrugged — a small, slightly impatient shrug, the kind a person makes when conceding an annoyingly self-evident point on the way to the part that actually matters. **"People can change. People do change. Fine."** She set the beer down. **"What is wrong with it is the picture of *who is doing the changing*. The Ghost in the Machine picture has a *willing self* — a homunculus — standing outside the system that produces the self, looking in at the system, and rewriting the system from outside. The willing self picks the new traits. The willing self runs the intervention. The willing self watches the system change and approves of the changes. The willing self is the *agent* of the becoming."**

She paused.

**"There is no such self. There is no homunculus. The thing we are calling the *willing self* is itself a process in the system the willing self is supposedly rewriting. The thing that says *I am going to change* is a regulator inside the system. The thing that picks the intervention is a modeler inside the system. The thing that approves of the result is itself another regulator. The willing self is in the system. The willing self is *part of what changes*. There is no view from nowhere from which the willing self looks in."**

She gestured at Bramble.

**"The Ghost in the Machine worldview is what you get when you photoshop the frontal lobe out of your picture of personality. The frontal lobe is the part of the brain that does the deciding, the regulating, the inhibiting, the planning — the executive functions, in clinical language. The bit that makes contact with the world. When you photoshop it out of the picture you have to put something in its place, because something has to be doing the deciding. The disembodied willing self goes in its place. The willing self is the ghost. The thing it haunts — the thing it is supposed to be controlling — is the machine. The machine has no frontal lobe in this picture, because the picture has photoshopped it out. That is the bad move. You cannot photoshop the frontal lobe out and keep the rest of the picture honest."**

She looked at Bramble.

**"Bramble, at this cell, has only the bit that does. Bramble has — by analogy — the frontal lobe, and nothing else. Bramble has no model of itself. Bramble does not regulate. Bramble does not revise. Bramble is the part of the architecture that the Ghost in the Machine picture has photoshopped out. We are starting this lab, this quarter, at the part of the architecture that the most popular wrong worldview of personality conceals from itself."**

She paused. She looked at Bart.

**"I have, as some of you know, written a short story about this. It was published last year. It is about a couple who have installed four small switches on the back of each other's heads, one switch per typological dimension, and who use the switches to make each other into the people they want to be married to, and at the end of the story they have lost track of who they were. The story is about the Ghost in the Machine. I did not know that when I wrote it. I knew it last week."**

Burns laughed. Marge did not laugh.

**"That is bit one,"** Marge said. **"There are three more. I will give them to you across the next three quarters."**

## Chapter 6: Vertebrate

### The Roof

She found it the way you find a sore tooth with your tongue, without meaning to and then on purpose. **"Well,"** she said, **"you've put me right on top of it,"** and there was no accusation in it, only the dry note she uses for a thing too large to do anything with but name. ### Reinforcing

"Temporal credit assignment," Lisa said, the way she says the names that are load-bearing. "There's a signal that runs on the gap itself. It jumps when the payoff beats what you expected and drops when it comes in under, and the size of the miss is the size of the correction. Dopamine, in an animal — and it isn't carrying pleasure, it's carrying surprise, the distance between what you bet and what you got, and it spends that distance reweighting the move that led in. A critic that scores the bet, an actor that shifts toward the moves that score. The actor does, the critic grades, the grade bends the doing."

**"In English, it does more of what paid and less of what cost,"** Marge said, the beer in the two-handed grip and not set down, **"and I want it kept in English, because the moment you say the fish knows the lever makes the food you've handed it a little accountant who isn't in there. It doesn't know the lever made the food. It does more lever. The knowing is ours. The doing-more is its."**

"Doing-more is the whole of it," Bart said, happy, because doing-more was a thing you could wire. "It's the dumb learning again, except last time the thumb landed on what it saw — you soured it on a sight. Now it lands on what it did. Same dumb engine, turned around to face the doing instead of the looking. That's the new wire, and it's most of why I'm employed."

"Same engine, except it doesn't run at the same pitch in every animal," Lisa said. "The signal I just gave you — the one that grades the bet — has a gain on it, and it isn't set the same fish to fish. Run it high and you get an animal that leans hard at anything that might pay. Run it low and you get one that hangs back and waits to be sure. Same wiring, same chemical, two settings of the one dial. There's a name for the axis, and it's on the card now. Coping Style."

"Two fish," Burns said, lit, because this was a part he had been holding all night. "Drop something new in a tank and watch them. One goes straight at it — noses it, owns the corner inside a minute, runs hot and commits, learns the tank fast and quits being afraid of it. The other hangs at the back wall and watches, and keeps watching, slow to move and slow to forget the thing that scared it. Bold and wary. You find the same two in trout, in rats, in a barn full of pigs, in a troop of monkeys — it sorts the same way every time anyone's looked for it. And neither one is the winner, which is the part people can't stand. Good year, the bold fish eats first. Bad year, the wary one is the only one of the two still alive to eat, because it never once went near the thing that turned out to be a heron."

**"And it's a tendency. That's the size of it,"** Marge said, the beer still in the two-handed grip and still not drunk from. **"It's the way the animal meets a new thing, and it holds — the bold fish is bold on Tuesday and bold clear across the room, which is the only reason you get to call it anything at all. But it is not braver. There is no fish in there deciding to be brave. It is dialed to lean, and so it leans, and we are the ones standing over the tank calling that a kind of courage."** There was a word for it she did not reach for, and the room felt her decline it. **"A tendency. Held, read off the outside, and no one home behind it."**

### Down in It

Burns was a glass or two past where the evening had started, well into the part of the night that belonged to him, and he came up out of his chair at *Experiencing Style* as though it had said his name.
