## Sentience

"Can I —" Bart said, and then didn't ask permission, just put his beer down with a small deliberate click of his own, glass on concrete, a sound that was not a relay but that landed in the room like the throw of one. "I want to do a thing we've never done and I think we have to start doing it now or we're never going to do it honestly. I want a show of hands."

"On what," Lisa said.

"On whether this thing feels anything." Bart looked at the unit, and then around the table. "Because here's where I am. For three quarters now I've listened to the two of you" — Burns and Lisa — "talk about PacMan in words that have somebody home in them. It *wants* the gradient. It's *trying* corners. It's *defending* its interior. And mostly I let it go, because mostly it's shorthand and we all know it's shorthand. But tonight we put a word on the slot — *Interiority* — that doesn't sound like shorthand. It sounds like there's an inside in the sense that there's an inside to *me.* And I think before that word hardens into something we all just say, somebody has to stand up and ask the rude question out loud. Is there anything it is like to be this? Hands. Yes means you think there's some it's-like-something in there, however dim. I'll go round and make everybody say why, because a hand by itself is worthless."

He looked at Lisa first. Lisa, predictably, did not raise her hand fast or slow; she raised it like a person stating a fact.

"Yes," Lisa said. "And I want to be exact, because my yes is going to surprise you and I need it not to be misheard. I think this unit knows nearly nothing. I think there is nobody in there doing anything I'd call thinking — it doesn't model itself, it doesn't know who it is, it has no idea it's in a kitchen. On *knowing* I'm a skeptic. My yes isn't about knowing. It's about *feeling,* which is a different axis, and on that axis I don't think the lights switch on at a line. I don't think there's a height you reach where suddenly there's something it's like to be you and below which there's nothing. I think it's a dimmer, not a flip-switch, and I think the dimmer is never quite at zero anywhere there's a thing holding itself apart from the world and running some little model to do it — anything maintaining a boundary. That's nearly everything alive and a lot that isn't obviously alive. The dimmer reads close to nothing on a rock and a hair above nothing on a cell and it climbs from there, and this unit is somewhere a hair above nothing, and a hair above nothing is not zero. That's my yes. It feels, faintly, in the way a thermostat would faintly if you pushed me, and it knows nothing, and both of those are true at once."

"That's the weirdest yes I've ever heard," Bart said.

"It's a consistent yes. Burns."

"Yes—" Burns said, and stopped, and planted both hands on the table and left them there, which for him was a long time. "No. Let me do this properly, because I'm about to talk myself out of where I started, and the room should watch me do it." He turned one hand over. "Bart's right that I've spent three quarters talking about Bramble like there's somebody in there, and I wasn't only joking, so here's the pull, honestly. A thing that holds a setpoint — that keeps a state against a world trying to knock it off — has to carry a model of what it's keeping, or it couldn't keep it. Lisa just told us that; it's the spine of the whole works. And Bramble does it. He holds his little world and corrects, and for about ten seconds that feels like the bottom rung of a ladder that runs all the way up to us, and the bottom rung says *yes.*" He shook his head, once. "But that's a ladder of *regulating,* and I keep trying to climb it like it's a ladder of *feeling,* and it is not the same ladder. A thermostat holds a setpoint, and there is nobody in the thermostat. Being a good regulator makes a thing *smart.* It does not make it *somebody.* And the somebody — the lights-on, the something-it's-like-to-be-it — in my line of work that turns up a long, long way up from here, in animals. This is one cell. He is a beautiful little regulator and there is no one home in him. So — no." He sat back. "And I had to argue myself there from a yes. Put *that* on the tape too."

The narrator, watching this much later, would note — and would be the only one to, because nobody in the room caught it and Burns least of all — that the ladder he climbed up and then climbed back down was the good-regulator theorem Lisa had set down ten minutes earlier for the board, restated word for shape, and that he had no idea he'd been standing on it. He thought he was reasoning from the bone beds. He was reasoning from the framework, and then he had done the rarer thing and overruled it — he talked like the framework the way some people speak prose, except this once the framework had said *yes* and he had said *no.*

"Bart, you called the vote, you have to take it too," Lisa said.

"No," Bart said. "And mine's the boring answer, which is that I think it takes a *self* — not a model of the world, a model of *you,* one that has a you in it, a thing that knows there's a thing here that is itself and could stop being. Nothing this far down has that. A cell doesn't know it's a cell. There's no one home to do the feeling, because feeling needs a someone and a someone needs a self-model and the self-model isn't here. World-model, sure, fine, I'll grant the board's a world-model. But nobody's looked in the mirror yet. No. Marge."

"No," Marge said, "and naturally I disagree with all three of you about why." She had the beer back in both hands. "For me it's about *symbols.* I follow Deacon on this — Terry Deacon, *The Symbolic Species,* and I'll lend it to anyone who wants to actually fight me instead of agreeing with me badly. The thing that makes a mind the kind of thing the word *sentience* is reaching for is symbolic reference — the capacity to mean something *about* something, to point past the present moment with a sign that stands for a thing in its absence. That's the Rubicon, and it is a real one, and almost nothing crosses it. A cell signaling its own state isn't meaning anything about anything; it's just a domino falling. There's reference all the way up the biological world, but symbolic reference is rare and late and probably only us and a few near-relatives, and without it there's no one to be a subject in the sense the question wants. So no. The machine doesn't feel anything, because feeling, in the sense Bart's rude question means, takes a symbol-user, and this is a very good domino."

"So," Bart said, and counted the table with a finger, unhurried. "Lisa, yes. Burns, no. Me, no. Marge, no. One and three." He sat back. "Good. I wanted it on the tape that we're split — lopsided, but split — and I wanted it on the tape that the four of us can't even agree on what we'd be agreeing *about*: one yes off on its own, and three noes that don't share a reason between them. That's the actual state of things. We'll do it again every quarter, and I'll bet the split moves before the end."

It did not occur to any of the four to ask the fifth adult in the room. Jasmine sat with her hands quiet in her lap and did not raise one and was not asked to, and the question went around the table and past her the way weather goes past a window. She was the only person at Smithers who had been in a room when a mind actually began — had been in a great many such rooms, with her hands inside the work of it — and had also been in the rooms where it failed to begin, and carried both kinds of room home in a private ledger nobody at the table had ever asked to read. She watched the four of them vote on whether the coin on the table could feel, and she kept her own counsel, and the narrator, who would clock this too and sit with it longer than with anything else in the meeting, let it stand without comment, because Jasmine had let it stand, and it was Jasmine's to let stand or not. Mira, who was four, was not part of it either. Mira had found a pen.

## The First Yes

It did not take a minute.

"I'm changing my vote," Burns said.

The room came around to him. He had voted no twice, and given the reason each time. "You know my rule. Feeling needs a brain. Not nerves — a brain. A center, made of the chemistry that runs good-and-bad, gathered into one place and aimed. Below that I don't care how clever a thing is. Clever isn't anybody. A thermostat is clever. The Going knob turned up and left there is clever. There's no one home in a thermostat. And I argue it from the only ground I'm any good on — the rock. And what the rock shows is a great explosion of life forms almost exactly when brains arrive. Which is this clade. The Cambrian. The bilaterian gathered its chemistry into a center and pointed itself. And that set off an arms race that never stopped. That's where, as far as I've ever been able to dig, somebody first comes home."

"So my vote's not one vote. It's three, because there are three things on this table and they don't land the same.

"The clade — the real bilaterian, out in the world half a billion years ago — yes. No flinch. It has the brain, the brain is the thing, I'd put my name on it in print. That's where feeling starts and I'm as sure of it as anything I've pulled out of the ground.

"The one at home —" he tipped his head toward the door, toward the house across the valley where the dome was running its rounds "— still no. Nerves and no center. A net, no brain. However much that thing has charmed us there's no one in it to be charmed. Same as always. No.

"And the one on the floor." He looked at the tetrapod, parked at its edge of the light. "Here's where it gets hard, and I'm not going to pretend it doesn't. That thing doesn't have the brain. It has a model of one — Lisa's two numbers standing in for a whole sea of chemistry. And a model of a brain is not a brain. By my own rule that should buy it a no, or a not-yet."

"Except for the dark."

He let it sit. "It's afraid of the dark. Marge — I'm going to use the word and you can take it back when it's your turn. Afraid. It learned the dark was bad when the dark could starve it, and fine, that's bookkeeping, I'd vote no on bookkeeping all night long. But we took the cost away. Sat it by the furnace where it feeds fine in the pitch dark, made the dark free — and the fear stayed. A thermostat does not do that. Bramble carried a fear past the day the fear made any sense, which is the most useless, most animal thing I have ever watched a machine do. A setpoint is bookkeeping. A fear you can't put down is somebody. So — the one on the floor — yes. Tentative. Hand not all the way up. But yes."

He sat back. "Four quarters of no. Tonight a yes. Write it down."

Lisa wrote it down. "For the record, we landed on the same yes from opposite ends. You climbed a wall to get here. I never had the wall — I've been yes since the Going knob, because I don't believe there's a line to cross. You think sentience switches on at the bilaterian. I think it was always on, turned down low, and the bilaterian's just where it got loud enough for you to hear it." She made a mark. "Either way. Still yes. That's two."

"No," Bart said, and then didn't leave it clean, because the dark thing had gotten to him and he wasn't going to lie about it. "I built the inside of that. I know what's in there — circuits, a couple of numbers, a rule for bending the numbers. There's no room in it for a somebody." A beat. "The dark business rattled me. I'll give you the dark business. But rattled isn't a vote. No."

Marge had both hands around the mug. "No. A behavior that looks like fear is a behavior that looks like fear. It is not yet a fear." She set the mug down. "Ask me again when it can tell me it's afraid. No."

"Two and two," Lisa said. And for a moment no one filled the quiet, because Burns crossing was the kind of thing that did not un-happen.
