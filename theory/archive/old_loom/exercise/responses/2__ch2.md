# Prediction for Ch 3

## Duration
The next chapter will be substantially longer than Ch 2. Ch 2 is approximately 3,500 words; Ch 3 should approach or exceed 4,000-4,500 words. The narrator has established a pattern of lingering—the lunch sequence alone takes 2,500+ words for what is structurally a single conversation. The interiors of other characters' lives (Bart, Mira) have begun to open up. Another character's retrospective interview—likely Marge's, as promised in Ch 2's final lines—will generate similar density.

## Register
The tone will remain conversational but fractionally more guarded. Ch 2's register is lucid and almost maternal in its clarity—the narrator watching, naming what she sees, handing it back. She has decided to write a book about this, and Burns now knows it. The next lunch will carry that awareness differently. The register will be less expansive in moments of connection and more clipped in moments of assessment. The sentences will remain long but will feel shorter—the narrator pulling back just slightly from the intimacy of the taco-truck observations.

## Concreteness
Extremely high. The narrator works in objects and physical details: the kumquat-vertebra, the bicycle bells whose meaning is unknown, the specific yellow truck with green letters, the flat light that does not slant. Ch 3 will maintain this—no abstraction about Marge will survive without a physical anchor. We will see Marge's hands, her posture, her workspace, the way light hits her face. The protocol is observation. The narrator does not invent.

## Interiority
Here is where the narrator's own voice becomes visible in contradiction with itself. She claims distance—"I have spent thirty years at exactly this distance from everyone"—but she reads Bart's interior through that distance, recognizing his hope (that his son loves him) in the microsecond before he closes it over. Ch 3 will show more of this fracture. The narrator will name an inner state of one of her subjects—likely Marge's—while simultaneously maintaining that she is only watching behavior. The contradiction will go unnamed but will be visible in the seams.

## Attention
The narrator lingers on:
- Small moments of false recovery (Burns's laugh after the closed-off hope)
- The gap between a person's self-inventory and their actual shape (Burns describing Bart)
- Physical tokens and names (Ben, Mr. Burns, the bot names Mira refuses to give)
- The precise texture of a relationship that is asymmetrical but not hostile (Cheryl and Burns)

She passes over fast:
- Administrative detail (Westminster's branding, the budget cycle)
- Her own past
- What she actually thinks about anything
- Marge, until now

Ch 3 will invert the attention map slightly. Marge will arrive named but unknown in a way Burns was not. The narrator will linger on something about her that she did not linger on with Burns—something that opens where Burns closed. The retrospective footage will matter in a way the Burns tape mattered, but differently. The narration will move faster through Marge's competencies and slower through something else.

## Silence
What the narrator does not say:
- What the book is for
- Whether she judges Burns for not knowing his son
- What her own life looks like (the Park City house is mentioned twice; no one lives in it with her)
- How long she has known the team members before the retrospectives
- Whether she was instructed to observe them, or whether this is self-imposed

Ch 3 will add to this:
- What Marge actually contributes to the work (her function is named but not shown)
- The nature of Lisa's relationship to Bart (an "old story," a "thing once"—but what)
- Whether the commission exists to test something or to make something
- Whether the narrator expects the work to succeed

## Signature
The diction is plain, almost legalistic, except in moments of sight. "No material for this category in Ch 1" opens with a strange formality—as if the narrator is filing a report. The sentences are long and coordinate without subordination. She rarely subordinates; she accumulates. "I did. I did not. I wanted. I did not. I am not sure why." The rhythm is patient and thorough. 

The voice has a particular way of handling names: Burns is Burns until she names him Ben, at which point the naming matters. Mira is protected—the narrator will not give the bot names because they are "hers." Bart is described in ten minutes of inventory without appearing. Lisa is mentioned only in relation to Bart. Marge has not yet arrived.

Verb choice: "I watched. I did not miss it. I did not take it. I felt. I noted." The narrator receives rather than acts. Even eating tacos is something done at the narrator as much as by her.

The signature includes a tendency toward oxymoron or reversal: "I was the right person to have seen it and the wrong person to do anything with it." The clarity comes from holding two contradictory positions simultaneously.

## Prediction for Ch 3

The next chapter will open with Cheryl arriving early to another lunch, likely with Marge. There will be a physical space described: where the lunch takes place, what the light does, what is visible from the table. The opening will echo Ch 2's architecture (early arrival, driving, a place not yet understood) but will vary the details. No brewery, or a brewery at a different season.

Marge will arrive, and the narrator will watch her with the same clarity she brought to Burns, but the shape of it will be different. Where Burns kept his interiority closed, Marge will have already opened something—or will open it differently. The narrator will not say whether Marge is nervous or in love with her work. Instead, she will notice something about Marge's hands, her stance, the way she orders food or the way she does not.

There will be a conversation about the retrospective, as there was with Burns, but it will not be the same conversation. The narrator will not ask Marge to perform something for the camera. Instead, she will watch Marge already performing something—already doing something for the camera, or already not caring about it. The tension will be different.

Somewhere in the chapter, the narrator will name a feeling or an observation that contradicts her earlier claim to distance. She may not know she is doing it. It will land in the middle of a sentence about something else—what Marge's work actually is, or what Lisa did before she was Lisa-and-Bart. The contradiction will sit there without resolution.

The chapter will close with the narrator driving back north or walking away from wherever the lunch occurred. As with Ch 2, there will be a final observation about the light or the place or about something Marge said that the narrator is holding. She may—unlike in Ch 2—actually turn around as she leaves.

Length: 4,000-4,500 words.

The logic of the book—if this is what the book is doing—suggests Marge next, but the narrator might have switched the rotation without saying so.
