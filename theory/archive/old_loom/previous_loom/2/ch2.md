## Fisher Brewing Company
*July 27, 2031*

I got there a quarter of an hour early. I like arriving early to a thing I don't yet know the shape of, and the first lunch with Burns was a thing I did not yet know the shape of. I had driven down from Park City on I-80, which is what you drive when you want to get from Park City to Salt Lake, and the drive had not been interesting, and I had not narrated it to myself. I parked a couple of blocks from Smithers on eighth south, walked past the warehouse without looking at it — the building is mine and I did not want to stand in front of it on a Wednesday at noon as if I were my own tenant — and went in the brewery side door at Fisher.

It was the kind of late-July Wednesday where the heat had set in by mid-morning and meant to stay until dark. I ordered a seltzer at the bar, took it out to the patio, found a table along the back fence near the food trucks. The patio faced ninth south, which had a protected bike lane down the side of it, and the cyclists were in summer kit, bare arms and bare legs, and there were a lot of them. I watched them roll past for ten minutes. The Wasatch foothills stood in the flat overhead light of late July, the grass on them long burned to gold — a high hard light that would not slant and would not soften, the kind of light that did not so much fall as bear down.

Burns arrived three minutes early. I am used to people being two or three minutes late to a first lunch, and over the years I have filed early people as either nervous or already in love with their work. I did not know yet which Burns was. He came in through the brewery side door looking for me, found me at the back fence, came over.

"Cheryl."

"Burns."

He hesitated. He was deciding between a hug and a handshake. I watched him decide. He picked the handshake. I would have picked it too.

He sat down across from me. He had brought nothing with him — no folder, no notepad, no rolled printout of the call, no nothing. I had also brought nothing. I had meant to bring a small bound notebook with three observations from the protocell tape in it, written down in case I lost the thread of them, and had, on the way out the door of the Park City house, not picked it up, and I am not sure why. I think maybe I had wanted to come to the lunch the way Burns had apparently come to it, which was without props.

"You hungry?"

"I could be."

"There's a truck that I have ordered tacos from on probably six occasions and have been satisfied each time."

"That's a recommendation."

"It is. I'd put money on it — I've eaten my way down this whole block and this is the one."

"I'll get the tacos."

"I'll come with."

We walked to the truck along the back fence. It was painted yellow with green letters and the menu was hand-chalked. Burns ordered two — pork, chicken — and a side of beans, and the woman at the window called the name *Ben* without asking. I ordered the same and gave my own name. We paid separately. We walked back to the table.

"You come here a lot."

"Bart thinks they hung the moon, and it's three blocks from the lab. The beer is very good. The food is fine."

"Honest."

"The food is fine. The tacos are better than most things in this category in this city. But food-truck food in Salt Lake is tasty, never great. I have stopped expecting it to be great. I come for the beer and because Bart will come and he's good company."

He had answered without selling me on the place. The first substantive sentence Burns said at the lunch was an honest description. I had not expected this and I found that I liked it.

The kitchen called his name first. He got up and brought the tray back and started to divvy. Two for him, two for me, beans in the middle. We were missing forks. He got up again and came back with forks and napkins and we unwrapped the tacos and started eating. The pork had some kind of chili thing on it that I did not identify. A cyclist on ninth south rang a bell at another cyclist; the second cyclist rang back. I did not know what either bell meant.

"You walked past the warehouse," he said.

"I did."

"You didn't stop."

"I don't stop at the warehouse between things."

"Why."

I thought about how to answer this. I wanted to answer honestly without making it weighted.

"I don't want you to feel watched between things. The tapes are enough."

He considered this. He picked up his second taco.

"That makes sense."

"Does it."

"Yes. I would feel watched."

"Most people would."

"I'm grateful for the discipline."

"It's not for you. It's for me. If I started dropping in, the lab would become a thing I had a relationship with. I don't want a relationship with it. I want to fund it and let it become what it is."

He nodded. He ate. The light kept doing what late-July light did.

"How's the paper," I said.

I watched him register the question. Bart had told me at the Pitch that Burns was working on a tiktaalik vertebra, and that the paper had been *being written* for over a year. I had filed it.

"The paper is — exists. In a file. The introduction I wrote is the wrong introduction. It needs thrown out."

"When."

"Soon."

"Soon."

"I have been saying soon for about fourteen months."

"Where is the vertebra."

"On a velvet pad on a shelf in my office."

"How big."

"About the size of a small lemon. Maybe a large kumquat, if you're picturing a kumquat."

"I am picturing a kumquat."

"Then you have it."

"What's wrong with the introduction."

He set his taco down. He looked at me. I had asked the wrong question, which was the right question.

"The introduction frames the vertebra as part of the water-to-land transition story. Which it is. But everyone has written that introduction. The vertebra is also part of a different story — a smaller one, about how the load-bearing architecture of a vertebra changed as fish became things that pressed down on the ground. The smaller story is the one I want to tell. The introduction does not tell it."

"So write the smaller story's introduction."

"Yes. And I know exactly how it goes — I've known for a year. That's the maddening part."

"When."

"Before the next retrospective."

"That's three weeks."

"It is. Three weeks is real time. I keep treating it like a suggestion."

"Should I hold you to that."

"You should not. I will fail. But you can ask me at the next lunch."

"That's fair."

He had given himself a deadline and named the failure mode of it in the same sentence. He had not asked me to hold him; he had asked me to ask. He wanted to be asked. That was useful to know.

"What are you reading, then," I said. "Since it isn't your own paper."

"Geothermal, lately. There's a project the U runs down in Milford — drilling into hot granite to see if you can pull a grid off it. Not my field." He said it more easily than he'd said anything about the vertebra. "I read about it more than I read my own work right now. Which probably tells you something."

"Does it."

"Probably." He let it go.

I watched him eat for another minute. I had a thing to deliver and was not sure how to do it without making it weighted. I decided to just do it.

"I watched the tape twice."

"Of the retrospective."

"There's only been the one."

He nodded. He had been waiting for this. The tape had gone to me back in May, and he had not heard a thing from me about it until now. I watched him try not to perform calm.

"What did you make of it."

"I made a lot of it. Most of what I made of it I'll say at the individual lunch with whomever it's about. But there's one thing I want to put on the table now, because it has to land at the next retrospective and I won't be there."

"Okay."

"Bart raised something at the close. His worry over Bramble having twelve knobs. The chassis-is-going-to-look-like-a-cactus, or something like that."

"Something like that."

"The team filed it light. You laughed."

He did not interrupt.

"He was right. The worry is real. The build is going to produce twelve knobs across three fast years and at some point soon the chassis cannot hold them. He has been carrying that worry for longer than the rest of the team has been aware of it. He has been carrying it alone."

I had said the word I had decided in advance to say. I had not made a production of it. It sat between us.

"I want you to take him seriously at the next retrospective. In front of the team. In a way the camera registers. I'm not going to tell you what that looks like; you'll know what it looks like in the room. I just want it on the record that you're going to do it."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I'm uncomfortable with the idea of staging."

"I know."

"I don't perform well to a camera. None of us do. If I try to do a thing for the camera it will read as a performance."

"I'm not asking for a performance. I'm asking for what you would have done if you hadn't laughed."

He took this in. He set his taco down again.

"Okay."

"That's it."

"Okay."

"Don't worry about it. Just have it in your head."

"I will."

I picked my own taco up. I felt the conversation could now move to anything. He, I noticed, felt it too. The five minutes had passed. The push had landed. Neither of us had marked it heavily. The bell-cyclists were back, or different bell-cyclists. The light was the same.

I had been holding something since the taco truck. I took it out now.

"She called you Ben," I said.

"Who did."

"The woman at the truck. She called your order without asking your name, and the name was Ben." I watched him. "Over here they don't say Burns — you're Ben on this side of Eighth South, Ben to the taco woman, Ben the way she'd say it. At the lab you're Burns. On three hours of tape I never heard the team call you anything else."

He set the last of his taco down and sat back, pleased, and the flat light off Ninth South gave me the face the warehouse never did — finer under the weather than the craggy frame promised, the pale gray-green eyes for once not leaving mid-sentence.

"That's Bart," he said. "Bart named the team. I'm Mr. Burns." The chest-laugh came up a beat early, at the frame and not the joke. "Think about it. My son looks at his father — a man who signs the checks, runs the department, gets up in front of the room — and what he reaches for is the rich old fellow behind the desk. The one who owns everybody. *Excellent.*" He put his fingertips together and let them go. "He was right. That's what makes it good. He's been doing that since he was a kid."

I let him have the laugh. I did not say that the cartoon he was delighting in was a portrait of a man who owns everyone and loves no one — that his son had drawn that, of him — and that he was holding it the way you hold a gift.

"And the others?"

"Oh —" and here he did the thing I would think about on the drive home, which was that he waved it off. "Bart and Lisa, that's an old story, theirs, from before the lab — they were a thing once, it's where the bug started, I never got the whole of it. Marge looked like Marge. Smithers because there's no Smithers." He was already coming back to the part he liked. "But Mr. Burns. He got me in one move."

So I asked about Bart, because I wanted to watch him do it. He did it for the better part of ten minutes. He had no idea he was doing it.

He told me Bart had the best hands on the team. He told me Bart was the one who finished — that Lisa would design something elegant and Bart would build the thing that survived contact, that you could hand the kid a problem on a Tuesday and it would still be getting solved Sunday night because he did not stop. He told me about the twelve-knob worry, and he told it now the way I had asked him to take it — seriously, as evidence — except the evidence it was in service of had quietly become *look how much he cares about the work.* Reliable. Diligent. The best hands in thirty years.

He did not, in ten minutes, tell me one thing about what Bart was *like.* Not a fear, not a pleasure, not a private joke, not a way of being in a room when the room wasn't watching. Everything he said about his son was a thing his son could *do.* He was describing a remarkable instrument, warmly, at length, and he had the whole inventory and not the man — had filled the distance where the man should be with a list of his competencies, and did not know that was what the list was, and would have been hurt past saying if I'd told him.

I was the right person to have seen it and the wrong person to do anything with it, and I knew why: I can read Bart whole off a secondhand inventory because I have spent thirty years at exactly this distance from everyone, watching instead of standing in it. The clarity and the distance are one organ. I made my peace with the trade a long time ago. Or I say I have.

Once, near the end, he started to say "I think he —" and stopped, and for half a second something opened in the weathered face: I could see him hoping I would hand him the rest of the sentence, would tell him the one thing I could not possibly know, that the son loved him. Then he closed it over. "He puts up with his old man," he said, and laughed, and turned the glass. It was a good recovery. Almost anyone would have missed it.

I did not miss it. I also did not take it. I picked at the beans.

He felt the quiet and moved us off it himself.

"My department is in some trouble," he said.

"Tell me."

"The university rebranded from Westminster College to Westminster University in twenty-three. Defensive move — small private liberal-arts schools are under structural pressure nationally and the trustees thought a name change might shift things. It hasn't shifted things. The dean has started making sounds about my research space. About my grants stream. We're not central to the university's brand and we know it."

"Are you worried."

"I'm matter-of-fact. The slow erosion under us is a thing I expect. I don't yet think it's going to take me out before the commission closes. But I notice it."

"When would you know."

"Probably by spring. The dean has to make decisions by the budget cycle."

"If they cut you, what do you do."

"I have savings. I keep showing up at Trellis. The work is the work. If I need teaching, I find teaching. We'd be tight. We wouldn't be in trouble."

"Okay."

He nodded. He had not been seeking reassurance; he had been giving me information. I liked that he had not been seeking reassurance.

Burns said, "I have a question for you that I have been carrying since the Pitch."

"Go."

"You are writing a book about this."

"Yes."

"Twelve chapters. One per clade."

"Yes."

"The team has been wondering."

"The team is right."

"OK."

He thought for a moment.

"I won't tell them you confirmed it."

"Tell them what you want. The book is a book. I am writing it. The commission is the commission. The two are not the same thing, exactly, but they share most of their content. The team can know."

"OK."

I let it sit for a beat. I had not expected him to ask; I had also not been surprised when he did.

"Tell me about Mira."

"Mira."

"Mira."

He smiled. He had not smiled yet at the lunch. The smile was small and complete.

"Mira is — being four. She has named every bot in the house. The protobots aren't Bramble, but they have names too. She's got them on the kitchen floor in a configuration she has decided is correct. She tells me when she sees me what each of them has done that day. The bots have not done very much. Mira has done a lot of telling."

"Names."

"Names."

"What are they."

"I'm not going to tell you the names. The names are hers. If she tells you at the final retrospective, that's her doing. I'm not going to give them to you secondhand."

"That's fair."

"Mira is, in a way I did not anticipate, the thing this project is about. I knew she was going to be in the room. I had not thought through that she would form a relationship with the bots the rest of us would have to take seriously."

He finished his second taco. I finished mine. We ate at the beans another minute and stopped before we were done.

"What time is your next thing."

"I have a class at three."

"It's twenty past one."

"I'll bike back. Twenty minutes on the lane."

"Then sit."

He sat. The shadow of the brewery's roof had moved across the patio by about two feet. The cyclists were quieter in the afternoon.

I thought about what I had not asked him. I had not asked him about Lisa. I had not asked him about Marge. I had not asked him about Jasmine. I had not asked whether the commission was the right way for a man to be spending his late fifties, or whether he thought it was going to work. I did not need any of that today. 

"I should head out."

"Okay."

We stood and walked together through the brewery toward the eighth south door. He held it open for me. I walked through and turned to him. I put my arms out for a hug; he looked surprised for half a second — we had shaken hands on arrival — and then hugged me. It was brief. He stepped back.

"Next lunch in a few months."

"Yes."

"Marge is next in the rotation."

"I'll tell her."

"Tell her if it comes up."

"Okay."

I walked back to my car along eighth south. I did not turn around. I got in. I drove west and then north onto I-80 and back up the canyon to Park City. The drive was, again, not interesting, and I did not narrate it to myself.
