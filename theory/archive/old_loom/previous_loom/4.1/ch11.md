# Notes on the Founding Retrospective

The team had been at Smithers since morning. By the time Jasmine pulled into the lot in the late afternoon and walked across the gravel with Mira on one hip and the foam case under her other arm, Burns had been pacing for an hour and Lisa was on her second pass through the agenda and Bart was sitting on the corner of the long workbench with one of the unfinished protobots in his lap, doing nothing visible with it but turning it over in his hands the way a person turns a worry stone. Marge had brought a plate of cut fruit out from the back kitchen and set it on the table and gone back for the kettle, which she put on the counter behind her where she could see it.

The light in the warehouse was the long bright light of a May afternoon, coming through the high industrial windows and falling in long slabs across the concrete. Mira walked toward the table without looking at any of the adults. She had the protobot in her right fist — Bot 3, the one from the founding quarter, the one Marge had written about, the one she had picked. The team had been calling them protobots in the lab and protocell bots in the agenda and the bots in the household, and which name was right was a question they had not bothered to settle. Protobot 3 had been wound that morning and was twitching very slightly against Mira's palm, which she did not mind.

Burns came over and crouched down to his daughter's height. He greeted her by name, not pet name, kissed Jasmine on the cheek and asked her how the drive had been. This was the version of hello they had agreed on for the lab, friendly, brief, slightly formal, because Burns was a man who could not be in his own lab without organizing the room into colleagues, and Jasmine, in the lab, was a colleague.

Behind him Lisa had the camcorder open on its tripod in the corner and was checking the battery. She had it ready to record but had not started it. She had also poured herself a drink from a thermos she had brought from home — a small jelly jar with a finger of something purple in it, ice clinking, and a sprig of something on top that she had picked from the back patio on her way in. It was her cocktail. Lisa had been drinking this cocktail for years; the team had seen her drink it twenty times across her thirties, sometimes at faculty parties, sometimes at her own kitchen table, once at a department picnic in a thermos that had still had a faint metallic taste. The household had not seen it before. The household (Mira, Jasmine) saw it now.

Mira walked up to Lisa, who was crouched at the camcorder, and stood beside her and stared at the jelly jar.

"What's that," she said.

Lisa, who was on her knees, sat back on her heels. She held the jar up.

"It's a bramble," she said.

Mira frowned. She had not heard the word.

"A bramble," she said.

"Yep."

"Bramble."

"Bramble. Yes."

Mira looked at the jar. Then she looked at the bot in her fist. Then she looked at the jar again. She said, "Bramble," to no one. Then she said, "Bramble bramble bramble," to herself, working the consonants. She liked the word. The word had two stops in it that her lips could move between, and the word also sounded like something small and good. She turned around and lifted the protobot up to her mother's face, both hands now.

"This is Bramble," she said.

Jasmine, who had spent four years being told things by Mira and had learned that the things she was told were sometimes the things, looked at the bot. She looked at Lisa. She looked at the bot again.

"This is Bramble," she said back.

Marge, who had returned with the kettle and was setting it on the counter, paused. Burns, who had been about to say something about the agenda, did not say it. Bart looked up from the protobot in his lap. Lisa, still on her knees by the camcorder, closed the battery hatch and stood up.

The protobot had a name. The protobot had been number 3 or the unit or the prototype or, in the household, the one Mira had said hello to first, for three months, and in less than thirty seconds, it was Bramble. Nobody had voted on this. Nobody had objected. The bot continued to twitch slightly in Mira's hands. The drink in Lisa's jar continued to fizz.

It is one of the only things in the book about which there is no surviving footage. The camcorder was three feet away and not running. Cheryl reconstructed it later from Jasmine's account, from Mira's continued use of the word for the rest of the day on tape, and from Lisa's own retelling, which Lisa offered without prompting at a lunch in Q5 because she had been thinking about it for a year by then and wanted to be sure Cheryl had the version that was most likely true.

# Notes on the Second Retrospective
*August 14, 2031*

The light in the warehouse in August came through the high windows in long flat slabs and lay across the floor without moving, the warehouse holding the day's heat the way it would hold it until the sun was down. The team had been there since two. Bart had laid the cards out at his end. Lisa had taped the agenda to the inside of the pull-up door, and had revised one line of it in red between her own arrival and Burns's. Marge had set the kettle on the counter behind her and a plate of cut pears on the table, and was reading. And Burns was pacing, and had been pacing for an hour. He had a thing to carry into the room that he had not had in the spring, and it was not on the agenda. 

Lisa watched him for a while.

"Burns."

"Yes."

"Sit down."

"I will."

"Now would help."

He sat on the corner of the workbench where Bart usually sat — Bart was at the cards — and from there he could watch the side door, which was the part of the room he was waiting on. A slab of late sun lay across his knee. He looked at it as if he could not have said what month it was.

The side door opened and Jasmine came in out of the heat of the lot with Mira on one hip and Bramble in her free hand, the bot's screen reading STOP above her fingers. She set Mira down on the concrete. Burns got up and crossed to her.

"Jasmine."

"Burns."

He kissed her on the cheek and asked about the drive — the friendly-brief-formal hello they kept inside the lab. She said that the lane work on twenty-third east was finally done and the protected lane was safe now without weaving — which was the colleague-version of the drive was fine. She handed him Bramble. It was warm, and it had clicked twice in the car, which she'd heard from the front seat. She had stopped flinching at the clicks a while ago; they were a thing that happened in the car now, the way the seatbelt chime was a thing that happened in the car.

Burns stood holding the warm machine a second longer than the handoff needed — the small real weight of it against the cold abstract thing he had been turning over for three weeks.

Mira came past her mother's knee at her own pace. She was carrying nothing — Bramble had ridden up front in Jasmine's hand — and the empty hand seemed new to her; she held it at her side as if she had been handed something and could not find where it went. Bart crossed from the cards and crouched and fist-bumped her, and she bumped him back with all four knuckles, and the bump went into the hand that had had nothing in it. Bart noticed. He didn't say anything.

Lisa lifted her bramble from the floor by the camcorder by way of hello — a finger of purple in a jelly jar, a sprig — and did not get up. Mira looked at the jar for a long second, the way she had looked at it the first time, in the spring, and said, "Bramble," and pointed.

"That's the other Bramble," Lisa said.

"There's only one Bramble."

"That's right. There's the bot, and there's the drink. The drink and the bot have the same name."

Mira accepted this. She went to Burns and held up her hand, and he gave the bot down to her, and she took it and held it the way her hand had decided months ago a bot was to be held — the same hold, whatever was inside it. She walked Bramble to Marge.

"Marge."

"Mira."

"This is Bramble."

"I have met Bramble. Bramble looks good."

"Bramble's screen says stop."

"It does."

"Bramble is stopped."

"I see that Bramble is stopped."

She climbed into the seat next to Marge — the seat that had been hers last quarter and was hers now by the kind of default a four-year-old establishes without anyone agreeing to it — and set the bot on the table in front of her. She did not pick up the juice box that was already there. The juice box was Marge's doing.

Burns had not sat back down. He stood near the table where Mira had parked Bramble, the warmth of the bot gone off his hands and onto hers, and watched Bart at the far end with his head down over the cards, not waiting on anything. Lisa reached over and checked the battery and the flip-out screen. The meeting, without anyone calling it, began.

## The Bigger Picture

Lisa got up from the camcorder. She walked to the agenda on the door. She tapped it.

"Open. Clade block. Bramble block." She tapped the fourth line, which last quarter had been nothing but a hand lifted at the ceiling. "And this one. Last quarter I called it the bigger picture — I didn't have a better word, and I didn't yet know what it was for. I know now. It's where we stand back and watch what the bot's grown into this quarter, and where that sits on the structure we've been stacking since the Pitch. A structure a growing thing climbs." She looked at Marge. "You gave us the word for it already. The morning of the Pitch, on the patio."

Marge looked up — the way she had looked up at the bare trellis that first morning, when nothing climbed it. Something climbed it now.

"The Trellis block," Marge said.

"The Trellis block." Lisa tapped the line once more and left it. "Close, after. The shape's the same as last quarter."

Burns had not sat down — he was leaning against the table near where Mira had Bramble parked. "Trellis," he said. "Okay." He had said it the same way the first time he heard it, on the patio, and he said it that way now.

She looked at the team. Marge was in tea-posture. Bart had taken his seat next to Lisa's.

"One change from last quarter. Burns, your deep-time slot is twenty minutes."

Burns said, "Twenty minutes."

"Twenty."

"Twenty is short."

"Twenty is what I need to give the Trellis block its time."

"Last quarter I had —"

"Last quarter it didn't need an hour. This quarter it does. I am taking twenty minutes from the Clade block to give it. The twenty minutes are coming from you."

"You're taking deep-time."

"I'm taking your part of deep-time. Lisa keeps her share of the Clade block — there are bot-side things to cover that need the time. The pre-Cambrian gets twenty minutes. We can pick another quarter where the pre-Cambrian gets more."

Burns looked at her. The chest-laugh came up out of him in two stages — a partial, then a full. Marge watched him laugh. Marge had not seen Lisa cut Burns's deep-time before. 

"Twenty minutes," Burns said.

"Yes."

"Strelley Pool gets two of them."

"Strelley Pool gets whatever you give it inside the twenty."

"Strelley Pool gets two."

"Fine."

Bart said, under his breath, "Strelley Pool gets two."

Marge wrote *Strelley Pool gets two* on her notepad without looking at it. Lisa went back to the camcorder. The red light came on. The flip-out screen showed the table.

## Thank You

Burns stood up. He had been standing, in fact, since he sat on the bench — he had not really sat down. He was at the head of the table now. The team had configured itself around him without anyone asking it to. Bart was at Lisa's right, Lisa at the camcorder end, Marge across from Bart, Jasmine and Mira at the foot near Marge. The table was, the team had noticed, the same configuration as the last meeting; the configuration had become a thing the room did automatically.

"Cheryl review."

He had a sip of his beer. The beer was a Fisher's IPA in a growler that Bart had brought.

"I had lunch with Cheryl in late July. It was the first time we have been across a table from each other since the Pitch. The lunch was at Fisher's, on the patio."

He looked at Bart. He looked away from Bart.

"She watched our first tape twice. I want to say that out loud because it changes how I should be talking about what she said. She has thought about us. She has spent more time on the tape than I would have expected. The things she pushed on in the conversation, she had been thinking about for a few weeks before we sat down. They were not improvised pushes."

He took a breath.

"There is one thing she pushed on that has to come back today. I want to say it before the Clade block opens, because it has to be on the tape and in the room before we work the agenda."

He looked at Bart.

"At the close of last quarter you raised the twelve-knob worry. You said the chassis was going to look like a cactus. You said you wanted it on the record that you'd raised it. I laughed."

Bart said nothing. Bart had set both hands on the table palms-down. Bart's hands rarely stopped during a meeting. They had stopped.

"Cheryl disagreed with how I took it. She said you have been carrying the worry for longer than the team has been aware of it. She said you had been carrying it, alone. She said *alone*."

He looked at the camera. He did not look away from it.

"The twelve-knob worry is in the room as a thing the team is working on collectively, not a thing you raise and the rest of us laugh at. When you bring it up later in the meeting — which I assume you are going to — I am going to be listening differently than I was listening last quarter."

The room was quiet. Marge picked up her beer. She had not been holding her beer in tea-posture. She did now. She did not drink. The kettle, behind her on the counter, had not been turned on yet. It was not, at this point in the year, a beer-and-kettle evening; it was, by six, a beer-and-kettle evening; by eight, the kettle would be on. 

Bart said, "Thank you."

He said it once. He did not say it again. He put his hands back into motion — one hand on the clade card he had been holding, the other on his beer. The room exhaled in a way the room did not narrate.

Burns nodded at Bart. He sat down. He sat at his end of the table — the end opposite Lisa's. The lamp at his elbow was off. The warehouse was still full of the long flat August light, and it would be a while yet before the evening took it down and anyone reached for the lamp.

# Notes on the Third Retrospective
*November 13, 2031*

By the time the team had been at Smithers a few hours the early dark of mid-November had come down — the sun behind the Oquirrhs by five — and the first cold haze of the season had settled into the valley, so the sodium lights at the lot's edge did not shine so much as smear, each wearing a soft sphere of its own light like a thing breathed on. In May the high windows had held the long spring light, and in August the flat slabs of summer; now in November they threw the inside of the building back at itself, black glass with the work lights doubled in it.

Marge had got there at two and had the kettle going by half-past, because the warehouse after six gives back the cold it has taken in all day, and she had her one warm corner going against it — her own kitchen, this warehouse going black at the windows, the same hearth wherever the cold finds her. She sat with a tea held in both hands against her chest, close, as if it might want comforting, and a paperback with the cover folded back. The worried mouth was at rest and worried anyway. The cardigan was the brick-red of forty years of rain. She would spend the meeting guarding the room against the Noble Savage — against the warm lie that the thing on the table was good because it was unspoiled — and she had brought a warm corner and a folded-back book and a juice box for the child: the conservative who tends, holding the line and the kettle in the same two hands.

The side door opened and the cold came in around Jasmine's knees. She had him in both arms — this was new this quarter, too big now to ride in one hand, a low round body she carried against her chest like a sleeping cat that has outgrown being a kitten. Mira came in behind at her own pace, hands free. Burns got up and crossed to her, and asked about the drive, and she said it had got dark fast and the first haze was down past twenty-first south, and she'd come up the surface streets because the interstate in the haze was a faith exercise — the colleague version of *the drive was fine*. She handed him Bramble; he was warm, he'd ridden in the footwell with the heater on him and clicked the whole way up. Bart came over and fist-bumped Mira's four knuckles. Lisa lifted her jelly jar by way of hello and Mira pointed and went and put one flat hand on his dome where Burns had set him, not to do anything to him, only to have her hand there. Bart handed out the beer. Marge put hers in the tea-grip. There was a juice box for Mira, and Bart bent the straw, and Mira sat down in the chair that had become her chair, close enough to him to keep a hand on him. Across the table Marge set her book down, cover folded back to hold her place, and reached for the kettle, and Lisa crossed to the camcorder, and the red light came on.

*February 12, 2032*

It was dark at the warehouse windows by the time they came, because it was the twelfth of February and the dark came down at five now, and Jasmine crossed the lot with the cake held flat in both hands, her daughter running the cold out ahead of her, Burns behind with the dome under one arm like a man carrying a sleeping animal in from the car.

Inside was the lab being the lab, and she took it in the way you take in your own family's noise. Bart at the bench with his caffeine grenade already unpinned, and the four-knuckle thing he and Mira did instead of a hello. Lisa's drink waiting at Lisa's place, dark and exact. # Notes on the Fifth Retrospective
*May 14, 2032*

Lisa came to the warehouse before the others, the way she did on the retrospective days, but not in her own car. A grey sedan let her out at the mouth of the lot on fourth west — a man saying something light through the open window, a parking tag swinging from the mirror, the car around the corner before she had crossed the gravel. She did not watch it go. She came in the thin grey zip-up and the Garmin, alone and entirely from the outside — a surface you could have whole or not at all.

The camcorder was already up on its corner of the bench, framed low on the patch of floor where Bramble would sit, and the red light was off. She ran the record the way she ran the room — by deciding what was allowed in. She did the walk she did before every meeting, the one that put her eyes on each thing that would have to behave once the light was on. 

As if they could sense her impatience, everyone seemed to arrive at once. Mira wanted the tetrapod out in the cleared middle of the floor and was running the game she ran with it now — setting a wooden block down a few feet off and letting the thing find it, the bot swinging its whole body around to face the block and crossing to it head-first, Mira moving the block, the bot hunting it again, around and around with no end she could see and the child needing none.

The back patio held the last of the long spring light, and Jasmine was on her knees at the foot of the trellis with her sleeves shoved up and a flat of canes beside her in burlap, putting blackberry into the ground. The trellis had stood bare on that patio since before the lab had a name; it was the bare trellis Marge had looked up at the Pitch and made the name out of. Jasmine was the only one of them who had ever really looked at it. She had meant for a year to bring something down for it — a clematis, she'd said once, something that flowered — and hadn't, because it had felt like a thing done for the building and not for herself, and she had run out of those. What she had finally brought was not the clematis. It was bramble: the thorned, low, unkillable thing, the same word as the robot her daughter was chasing around the floor, the same word as the drink Lisa would set by the camera within the hour. You do not train a bramble up a trellis. You give it the foot of the thing and a season, and after that it goes where it goes.

Lisa walked over. Jasmine looked up and said it would either take the whole fence or die inside a month, she couldn't tell which, and seemed at peace with both. Lisa said the fence was the landlord's problem, which was as close as Lisa came to a blessing, and Jasmine laughed and went back to the dirt. It was an easy exchange and it cost neither of them anything, which was the kind Lisa was built for.

Inside, Marge had the kettle going on its ring in the cold corner — but she had made the corner and then drifted off from it, standing at the far cold end of the bench with her bag at her feet and one hand flat on its closed flap, looking at nothing the room held. The kettle climbed to its whistle and she let it run a beat past the point she would have caught it on any other day. Lisa marked it the way she marked anything that might not keep to time, and planned around it, and had no one to hand the why to, because the person she handed that kind of question to was the person standing slow at the end of the bench.

Then she made her bramble and set it down at the exact place on the bench where it went, an inch from the camera and not touching it. Jasmine came in wiping her hands on her jeans. Mira left the tetrapod in the middle of the floor and it went still, having nothing left to chase. Burns and Bart were already bent over the cigar box at the far end, arguing the order of the cards. The room assembled itself the way it did, and when it had, Lisa put her thumb to the light.

# Notes on the Eighth Retrospective
*February 12, 2033*

The twelfth of February came in cold and early dark, the mountains gone to flat blue paper against the last of the light and the Granary holding its few lit windows the way a held breath holds warmth. The lab counted its own age off this date the way it counted Mira's, because they were the same date — the warehouse had had its first morning two years before, on the morning she turned four — and nobody at Smithers had ever sorted out which of the two anniversaries the night belonged to, which is the kind of overlap a careful person leaves alone.

She came in through the front with her mother, six years old as of that morning and carrying nothing, which was the new thing. The warm one came in beside her on its own four legs, clicking on the concrete, swinging its head to read the cold room for somewhere to put itself — and finding the radiator under the front windows before it had well cleared the door, and going to stand over it the way it stood over the stove vent at home, because Bart had built it a furnace, and a furnace burns hardest in the cold, and the warm could not wait on the meeting. Mira let it go and did not look back to see it settle, the way you stop watching a thing you have stopped having to carry. Last year she would have had it against her hip the whole way in. The year before that she would not have let it walk.

Inside it was warm and bright and smelled faintly of sugar. Lisa was at the camcorder end where she lived, the red light dark under her thumb and a bramble already going to water in its jar at her elbow, reading the battery charge once. Bart had the cigar box at his place and a pint of something green going flat beside it and both hands already busy in a sensor. Marge held the cold end with the kettle behind her and a beer in the two-handed grip, and a white bakery box she had carried in herself and set where Bart could not reach it, the candles being her department and having been since the child was old enough to have a number on a cake. Burns crossed the floor to Jasmine, the cheek and the question about the drive, and she told him there was ice on the 201 and they'd come around the long way — true, and sized exactly to the asking.

Jasmine did not sit yet. She went to the back door instead and opened it on the cold, to look at the trellis on the patio — the only one of them who ever looked at it — and Mira went with her. The bramble had taken the cold the way the thing was built to take it, which is to say it had done nothing you would call thriving and had not died either: still gripping the slats it was given, dark and dormant, nothing showing.
