Ch 9

## Worm to Band

The shelving left behind it the small hush a ceremony leaves, and Bart, who did not let a thing like that sit one beat past its use, stood up into the hush and said he had something to show them. Not a build — nobody had asked for it. He'd made it because the new one had been the only job on his bench all quarter, and it had come off that bench weeks back the best of everything he'd made, and a man cannot stand at a finished bench three months running without making trouble; the trouble he'd made was a slideshow. He said *slideshow* the way you say a word to let the air out of it, and then crossed the kitchen and killed the lights, which put the air straight back. What he had was the four shapes — the Reactor, the Conservator, the Planner, the Pursuer, the four corners every clade card carried on its back — and a way to take any one of them and watch it hold still while everything around it changed: you ran the shape up the tree, a rung to a clade, worm to wherever the quarter had reached, and you put a cartoon on every slide, because the cartoons were how he'd sorted the shapes in his head since before there was a lab to sort them in. The room knew whose cartoons they were. It did not yet know which cartoon went with which shape. That was the show.

"First one's mine," he said. "Get it out of the way." He threw a flatworm up on the wall with a cartoon face on it and was off, fast, pointing. "The Reactor. Acts. Doesn't poll the room, doesn't ask how anybody feels about it, good at what it does and runs the whole thing off its own two hands. Worm to band, five rungs." He tapped the worm. "Credit goes to whatever just fired — light, food, a poke. No plan, no memory worth the name, no notion there's a world past the next twitch. It moves on the most recent thing, and the most recent thing is plenty."

A fish next, an ambush fish hung still in cartoon water, the same face on it. "Pike. Barracuda. The kind that waits, and the thing it's eating never sees the strike start. Brilliant at it — doesn't need a picture of the fish it's hunting, never once wonders what the fish might want. The hunting's the whole animal." Then a cat alone on a cartoon ridge. "Snow leopard. Wolverine. Brain's big enough now to lay a real ambush, hold a valley's worth of map in its head, plan a day ahead. Points every bit of it at the hunt and not a bit at company. No pack. Nobody to keep track of. Best thing on the mountain and the only thing on it."

The fourth slide had no animal on it. It was the cartoon, full frame — the spike-headed boy himself — and Bart let it sit a second, which for him was a long time. "Primate. Just the cartoon, this rung. Acts first, pulls the prank, takes the dare — and not once on the way to it does he run the picture of how the other kid's going to take it. Not mean. The machine for thinking about the other kid is switched off, that's all." He didn't dwell on it. "Same name I go by. That's not the joke — that's the deal. You go by the shape you are." And he was on to the last one before the room could decide what to do with that: a raiding crew on a cartoon deck, the face on the captain. "The band. Not a tribe — a crew. Pirates. Mercenaries. They split the take and not much past it. Coordinate fine, trade fine, turn up a new trick fine — everything a band does but the one thing, because there's no *us* in it anywhere. Every man aboard is a job the next man needs done, and the day the hold's empty they're strangers on a beach."

He brought the lights up, worm and fish and cat and cartoon and crew gone in the one switch. "Reactor, worm to band. Same move every rung: go, don't poll the room, take the other guy for a thing that does something, never let a bond slow the hands. That's the shape." A beat, dry as a chip. "That's me. Three more of these coming, one a quarter, and the last one runs the whole tree."

Burns had loosed the chest-laugh a beat early, at the worm; Lisa had laughed once down her nose at how clean the thing recursed and gone on writing; Marge had set her pen down at the first slide and left it down. She was not watching the cartoons; she was watching the four people who were going to find themselves in them. Mira, who did not know what the grown-ups knew, knew it was funny that a worm had a face, and said so.

And the narrator kept the part the room could not quite reach. The man had built a machine for stepping outside a shape and watching it hold still, and he had aimed it first at his own, and run his own faster and flatter than he would run any of theirs in the quarters to come — *got mine out of the way,* he'd said, and did. The one move the Reactor never makes is the step outside — the turn to look at itself the way the machine looked at everything else. He could stand the whole room in front of its shapes and walk them through rung by rung, dead accurate, and he would. The slide he ran quickest, and cleanest, and with the least left over, was the one wearing his own name.

Ch 10

## Worm to Settlement

The shelving left the small hush a ceremony leaves, and Lisa did not let it sit — she moved them into the Trellis block and slotted the thing that opened it now, a line item where a surprise had been in the spring. "Shape-shifter," she said, and Bart was already up and crossing to the wall, because this was the part he'd built himself and would not be hurried through. He hit the lights. The warehouse dropped to the camcorder's standby glow and the lit square of the wall, and he threw up the first slide.

A cartoon flatworm with a cartoon face laid over it, and the room knew the face before he named the shape, because they'd watched him run his own up this same wall in the spring and could do the arithmetic now. "Second shape. The Conservator," Bart said, fast, the performer's clip. "Stable, good at what it does, reads people right down to the floor — and doesn't bother revising any of it. Marge said go ahead. Same rules as mine: cartoon's the cover, six levels, worm to settlement." He tapped the worm. "Smallest scale. It's learned what predicts what, and now it won't let a new thing overwrite the old lesson. Guards what it's got. Efficiency by holding still."

An old draft horse next, heavy in the traces, the same face on it. "Workhorse. Knows the route, runs it, doesn't spook — set off a gun and it keeps pulling. Steady all the way down." Then a dog asleep on a porch. "Old Yeller. Lassie. Bonded to the bone, real skills, and nothing new moves the needle. Loyal isn't a mood on this one. It's the whole build."

He put up the cartoon character full-frame, no animal under it this time, just the blue tower of hair, and the room made the sound a room makes when the joke arrives where it was always going. "Marge. The cartoon." The labmate at the cold end smiled, a small thing, and let it land. "Plays a cop one week, sells houses the next, whatever the episode needs — and then the show absorbs it. Hair's the same. Voice is the same. What she thinks is right is the same. Takes a run at being somebody new and it never takes; one episode and the shape snaps back to true."

A foraging band on a green coast. "A band that's kept the same crafts, the same bonds, the same way of doing a thing for longer than anyone's counted, and hands every bit of it down whole, no drift. The deep-skill people who just go on being themselves." And last, a walled town on a hill, the face on an old one at the center of it, an elder in a doorway. "And a settlement that does the same at scale — hands the tools and the calendar and the rule down intact, three hundred years of it, a marvel to watch." He let it ride a beat, which he did not usually do. "Right up until the river jumps its bank, or the trade road moves two valleys over, and the same holding-on that kept the place whole for three centuries is the one thing it cannot put down. Doesn't update. Can't. The form outlasts the world it was cut to fit, and goes on being kept anyway, because keeping is the whole of what it knows." He brought the lights back up. "Conservator, worm to settlement. Hold what works, don't let the new thing scribble over the old reliable, trust the form. That's the second one. Marge."

The room read it as warm, because it was, and because the woman it ran up the wall took it the way the shape takes everything, which is without needing to do a thing about it. She'd smiled at the cartoon and let the rest go by, and the only place the narrator caught a catch was the last rung — the held form outliving the world it fit, the exact cost Marge had been building her own quiet machine around for a year, landing on the one who types the way a thing lands when it has, just this once, typed her back. Half a second. Then the Conservator did the Conservator thing and settled, no revision required, comfortable in the position, the *oh, you* warmth closing over the place the catch had been. The room saw a woman at ease with being described. The narrator saw the half-second, and let it go, because the not-revising was the shape proving itself in the one spot the shape can never watch itself do it.
