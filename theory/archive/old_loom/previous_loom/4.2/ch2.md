Ch 1

## The Protocell

Bart took the next slot. He'd brought a small stack of cards he'd been making for two months — tarot-sized, heavyweight matte stock, the same stock Cheryl had used for her own twelve, which he'd identified by feel within ten minutes of receiving them. He'd matched her stock and her dimensions, because if these cards were going to live alongside Cheryl's across three years he wanted them to live in the same hand.

He held up the Protocell card — a drawing he'd made of one of the bots, mouse-sized, in elevation, the wind-up crank sticking up from the dome. The back was blank.

"This is the Protocell," he said. "The first clade. The first thing capable of being selected on. Not yet a prokaryote. Not yet life as we're used to thinking about it. A small bubble of fat around some chemistry that had figured out, for the first time, how to keep being itself."

He set the card down.

"The thing it figured out — here's the part to keep — is selection. Before the protocell there's chemistry. After it, biology. In between is a thing where the chemistry inside one bubble is a little different from the chemistry in the next, and one holds together better, and the one that holds together better is the one that gets to make more bubbles. The variation-selection cycle. That's the new thing the protocell brought, the thing the clade before it didn't have." He looked at Burns.

Ch 2

## Braid

Bart took the cards out of the cigar box he kept them in. He laid Cheryl's down: the bare word PROKARYOTE on heavyweight matte, the back blank. He laid his own beside it — a pen-and-ink schematic of the upgraded Bramble, the fist-sized dome, the screen, the panels flush along the sides, the crank on one side and the Effort knob on the other, the four light sensors around the seam.

"Pro-kary-OTE," he said, in his best cereal-commercial voice.

Marge squinted. Burns laughed the chest-laugh. Mira said *Pro-kary-OTE* under her breath in a four-year-old's approximation of it.

"A prokaryote is a bacterium or an archaeon. A cell with no nucleus — the DNA loose in the cytoplasm, a small genome, fast division. It senses, it moves, it eats, it can be eaten. And it can take up DNA from another cell, from a stranger, from a virus carrying some — so inheritance runs sideways as well as forward. The lineage isn't the tidy tree people like to draw. It's a *braid.* Not a line."

Burns said, "Good."

"That's what a prokaryote is. That's the kind of thing it is."

He reached for Jasmine's phrase — half a joke and half because it was exact — and used it on himself, with relish.

"The plane I want to land is one thing. Predation. At the Protocell there's no predation; there's nothing yet to eat. By the Prokaryote there is, and the main pressure on a thing becomes — for the first time on the planet — *other things.* Before, the environment selects you. Now the environment selects you and other organisms select you too. The protocell's hardest problem was not stopping. The prokaryote's is not getting eaten, and where it can, eating. That's the breakthrough. Cellular predation. The arms race opens."

He wrote *capacities* on the back of his card and underlined it, with the ceremony he wasn't going to acknowledge.

"Four capacities. One example each."

He took the first two the way a person runs through the examples a room already half-knows. The ribosome: the molecular machine in every cell that reads RNA and assembles proteins, built to a precision no cell ever earned — refined by the lineage across four billion years of can't-afford-mistakes, and merely *used* by whatever cell happens to have one. And sporulation: the soil bacterium that, when the world stops being livable, wraps itself in a coat, dries down to almost nothing, dismantles its own machinery, and becomes a spore — dry, hard, shelf-stable, a coffee bean of a thing that is to the eye not alive — and then, when water comes back, opens, rehydrates, reassembles, and picks up almost exactly where it set down. A do-over. Inherited, both of them; mechanical, both of them; the lineage's long patient work, run by a cell that did none of it.

"That's two," Bart said. "Same point each time. Three." He looked at Lisa. "Chemotaxis. You want this one?"

"You take it."

"You're going to interrupt me."

"I might."

"OK." He tapped the back of the card. "Chemotaxis is how a bacterium decides which way to swim. *E. coli* carries hundreds of receptor proteins clustered at one pole — the *receptor array* — each tuned to one kind of molecule out in the medium. A receptor binds its target and changes shape; the change ripples across the whole coupled array and gets amplified, and the amplified signal lands on a kinase at the base of the cluster called CheA. CheA is the integrator. It takes every receptor's input and puts out one signal. Run, or tumble."

He looked at Mira.

"Hey. Mira. *Run* and *Tumble.* Those words on top of Bramble. That's where they come from."

Mira looked up.

Bart stood, pushed his chair back, stepped into the open floor between the table and the pull-up door, and put both arms out, fingers spread.

"Pretend my whole body is one *E. coli.* My head — sensors, the receptor array, reading the world. My arms and legs — flagella, the little tails it swims with." He waggled his fingers. "Watch."

He walked forward in a straight smooth line, arms out, fingers going.

"*This* is RUN. Clear gradient. Food that way. The array says go, CheA says run, the flagella all turn the same way and the cell goes."

He stopped, pulled his arms in, and spun slowly in place.

"And *this* is TUMBLE. Confused part of the world, no clear gradient. CheA says tumble, the flagella un-coordinate, the cell flips and points somewhere new." He stopped and pointed at his head. "And then it samples again. Clear now? Run. Still confused? Tumble again. Run, sample, tumble. That's how a bacterium gets where it's going."

Mira watched with her mouth slightly open, then turned Bramble so the screen faced her. It still read STOP.

"Bramble is stopped," she said.

"Bramble's charging," Bart said. "It runs and tumbles when the stores aren't filling and there's a gradient. We'll get to that."

"OK," Mira said.

Bart came back to his chair. "That's three."

He set the card down, picked it up, set it down again.

"Four. Sequential responding. I'm going to use a word in this one Marge is going to wince at. Flagging it first."

"Use the word," Marge said.

"The word is *anticipates.* And I'm going to use it with a bacterium as the subject. So, before the bacterium-sentence: *the predictions belong to the lineage, not the individual.* The cell isn't doing anything but running an inherited program. The anticipating is in the program. The lineage anticipated, generations back, and built the program out of the anticipating. The cell just runs it."

"Thank you," Marge said.

"In the gut, lactose usually shows up before maltose does. When lactose arrives, the bacterium switches on the lac operon to eat it — and on the strength of the lactose alone, before any maltose is there, it switches on the maltose machinery too, because across its lineage the order has held reliably enough that betting on it beats waiting. The cell has no picture of the meal. The lineage wired a link between the two switches, and the link runs." 

"Four capacities. Ribosome, sporulation, chemotaxis, the two sugars. Precision, gating, run and tumble, sequencing. Each one a thing the cell does and did not earn — the lineage worked each out on its own schedule, and the cell carries all four at once."

He paused.

"Four braids in the braid."

He sat down.
