Ch 1

## The Protocell

Bart took the next slot. He'd brought a small stack of cards he'd been making for two months — tarot-sized, heavyweight matte stock, the same stock Cheryl had used for her own twelve, which he'd identified by feel within ten minutes of receiving them. He'd matched her stock and her dimensions, because if these cards were going to live alongside Cheryl's across three years he wanted them to live in the same hand.

He held up the Protocell card — a drawing he'd made of one of the bots, mouse-sized, in elevation, the wind-up crank sticking up from the dome. The back was blank.

"This is the Protocell," he said. "The first clade. The first thing capable of being selected on. Not yet a prokaryote. Not yet life as we're used to thinking about it. A small bubble of fat around some chemistry that had figured out, for the first time, how to keep being itself."

He set the card down.

"The thing it figured out — here's the part to keep — is selection. Before the protocell there's chemistry. After it, biology. In between is a thing where the chemistry inside one bubble is a little different from the chemistry in the next, and one holds together better, and the one that holds together better is the one that gets to make more bubbles. The variation-selection cycle. That's the new thing the protocell brought, the thing the clade before it didn't have." He looked at Burns.

Ch 2

## Braid

Bart took the cards out of the cigar box he kept them in. He laid Cheryl's down: the bare word PROKARYOTE on heavyweight matte, the back blank. He laid his own beside it — a pen-and-ink schematic of the upgraded Bramble, the fist-sized dome, the screen, the panels flush along the sides, the crank on one side and the Effort knob on the other, the four light sensors around the seam.

"Pro-kary-OTE," he said, in his best cereal-commercial voice.

Marge squinted. Burns laughed the chest-laugh. Mira said *Pro-kary-OTE* under her breath in a four-year-old's approximation of it.

"A prokaryote is a bacterium or an archaeon. A cell with no nucleus — the DNA loose in the cytoplasm, a small genome, fast division. It senses, it moves, it eats, it can be eaten. And it can take up DNA from another cell, from a stranger, from a virus carrying some — so inheritance runs sideways as well as forward. The lineage isn't the tidy tree people like to draw. It's a *braid.* Not a line."

Burns said, "Good."

"That's what a prokaryote is. That's the kind of thing it is."

He reached for Jasmine's phrase — half a joke and half because it was exact — and used it on himself, with relish.

"The plane I want to land is one thing. Predation. At the Protocell there's no predation; there's nothing yet to eat. By the Prokaryote there is, and the main pressure on a thing becomes — for the first time on the planet — *other things.* Before, the environment selects you. Now the environment selects you and other organisms select you too. The protocell's hardest problem was not stopping. The prokaryote's is not getting eaten, and where it can, eating. That's the breakthrough. Cellular predation. The arms race opens."

He wrote *capacities* on the back of his card and underlined it, with the ceremony he wasn't going to acknowledge.

"Four capacities. One example each."

He took the first two the way a person runs through the examples a room already half-knows. The ribosome: the molecular machine in every cell that reads RNA and assembles proteins, built to a precision no cell ever earned — refined by the lineage across four billion years of can't-afford-mistakes, and merely *used* by whatever cell happens to have one. And sporulation: the soil bacterium that, when the world stops being livable, wraps itself in a coat, dries down to almost nothing, dismantles its own machinery, and becomes a spore — dry, hard, shelf-stable, a coffee bean of a thing that is to the eye not alive — and then, when water comes back, opens, rehydrates, reassembles, and picks up almost exactly where it set down. A do-over. Inherited, both of them; mechanical, both of them; the lineage's long patient work, run by a cell that did none of it.

"That's two," Bart said. "Same point each time. Three." He looked at Lisa. "Chemotaxis. You want this one?"

"You take it."

"You're going to interrupt me."

"I might."

"OK." He tapped the back of the card. "Chemotaxis is how a bacterium decides which way to swim. *E. coli* carries hundreds of receptor proteins clustered at one pole — the *receptor array* — each tuned to one kind of molecule out in the medium. A receptor binds its target and changes shape; the change ripples across the whole coupled array and gets amplified, and the amplified signal lands on a kinase at the base of the cluster called CheA. CheA is the integrator. It takes every receptor's input and puts out one signal. Run, or tumble."

He looked at Mira.

"Hey. Mira. *Run* and *Tumble.* Those words on top of Bramble. That's where they come from."

Mira looked up.

Bart stood, pushed his chair back, stepped into the open floor between the table and the pull-up door, and put both arms out, fingers spread.

"Pretend my whole body is one *E. coli.* My head — sensors, the receptor array, reading the world. My arms and legs — flagella, the little tails it swims with." He waggled his fingers. "Watch."

He walked forward in a straight smooth line, arms out, fingers going.

"*This* is RUN. Clear gradient. Food that way. The array says go, CheA says run, the flagella all turn the same way and the cell goes."

He stopped, pulled his arms in, and spun slowly in place.

"And *this* is TUMBLE. Confused part of the world, no clear gradient. CheA says tumble, the flagella un-coordinate, the cell flips and points somewhere new." He stopped and pointed at his head. "And then it samples again. Clear now? Run. Still confused? Tumble again. Run, sample, tumble. That's how a bacterium gets where it's going."

Mira watched with her mouth slightly open, then turned Bramble so the screen faced her. It still read STOP.

"Bramble is stopped," she said.

"Bramble's charging," Bart said. "It runs and tumbles when the stores aren't filling and there's a gradient. We'll get to that."

"OK," Mira said.

Bart came back to his chair. "That's three."

He set the card down, picked it up, set it down again.

"Four. Sequential responding. I'm going to use a word in this one Marge is going to wince at. Flagging it first."

"Use the word," Marge said.

"The word is *anticipates.* And I'm going to use it with a bacterium as the subject. So, before the bacterium-sentence: *the predictions belong to the lineage, not the individual.* The cell isn't doing anything but running an inherited program. The anticipating is in the program. The lineage anticipated, generations back, and built the program out of the anticipating. The cell just runs it."

"Thank you," Marge said.

"In the gut, lactose usually shows up before maltose does. When lactose arrives, the bacterium switches on the lac operon to eat it — and on the strength of the lactose alone, before any maltose is there, it switches on the maltose machinery too, because across its lineage the order has held reliably enough that betting on it beats waiting. The cell has no picture of the meal. The lineage wired a link between the two switches, and the link runs." 

"Four capacities. Ribosome, sporulation, chemotaxis, the two sugars. Precision, gating, run and tumble, sequencing. Each one a thing the cell does and did not earn — the lineage worked each out on its own schedule, and the cell carries all four at once."

He paused.

"Four braids in the braid."

He sat down.

## Bigger

Bart took the top card off the deck in the cigar box. The front of it was his own drawing — an elevation of the new body, the low round disk with the shallow dome and the four corner wheels, done in his draftsman's line, the prokaryote-tier unit drawn small and central inside the dome like a yolk. He held it up so the camera could see it and read the title off the top of it in the voice he used for titles, the cereal-commercial voice, the one that sold you the box.

"*Eu — KAR — y — ote.*"

"Thank you," Lisa said.

"The eukaryote," Bart said, in his own voice, "is the cell that has rooms. Everything before this was one bag of working parts — a bag that does a lot, a very good bag, but a bag. This one has a nucleus. It has an inside the inside, where it keeps the important paperwork. It has a skeleton on the inside that it built itself, that it can take down and put up somewhere else when it wants to be a different shape. It has hallways. And the headline" — he tapped the small central yolk in his own drawing — "is that it has *tenants.* The power plants in this cell, the mitochondria, didn't grow here. They moved in. A couple of billion years ago this cell's however-many-times-great grandmother was a cell that ate another cell and, for reasons nobody got to vote on, did not finish the job — kept the meal alive inside itself and put it to work — and every one of us at this table is still running the descendants of that meal in every cell we've got. So the new word for this quarter, the new motivation" — he was careful with the word; they were all careful with the words now — "is *Interiority.* Not just a wall. An inside. An inside with its own weather, that keeps a history, that has somebody living in it."

"That's the breakthrough talking," Burns said. "Let me take the breakthrough."

"Take it."

"The breakthrough at this clade is *Scale and Size,*" Burns said, "and it is the most direct cash-out of a prior breakthrough we've had yet. Last clade, the headline was predation — the line learned to make a living off other living things. This clade is what happens when predation goes one step stranger and the prey is more useful alive than dead. You don't digest it. You keep it. You run it. And the thing you get for keeping it is *power* — an absurd amount of power, far more energy per unit of paperwork than a cell running its own engines off its own outer wall could ever raise — and once you have that much power to spend, you can afford to get *big.* You can afford a genome the size of a library when your cousins out in the open water are still running off a pamphlet. Bigger genome, bigger cell, more rooms, more tenants, then eventually" — and here he could not help himself, he was looking somewhere past the wall — "bodies. This is the clade that makes bigness affordable. The engine that buys the room is the meal it kept alive."

"Some people," Lisa said, "would say the energy story is doing more work than the evidence licenses."

"Some people would," Burns said, comfortably. "I think they're being careful past the point of usefulness. The energy story is the one I believe. I'll say it's mine and not the textbook's."

"The capacities," Bart said, and turned the card so its face showed again, and went down them with the back of a pen. "Four of them at this clade. I'll name them and Burns'll tell you which critter does what."

"Intracellular Transport," he said. "The cell is too big now to just let things slosh where they need to go. So it runs delivery. It has little motors that physically walk cargo from one part of the cell to another along tracks — actual legs, actual walking, actual tracks."

"When you say it walks," Jasmine said. "What do you mean, walks."

"I mean walks." Bart set the card down. "The motor's a protein. It's got two feet, near enough, and it hauls a sack of cargo down a track by putting one foot in front of the other — picks up the back foot, swings it past the front one, sets it down, picks up the next. Hand over hand, except feet. It looks like —" and he stood, and pushed back from the bench, and did it: arms stiff out in front holding an invisible sack, knees locked, lurching one leg past the other across the open floor in front of the camera, a flat-footed monster-walk, the cereal-commercial showman gone full Frankenstein. "— that. All day. Every cell in you, right now, somebody's doing that."

Mira lost it — the whole-body laugh that folds a four-year-old in half. Jasmine laughed with her, at her more than at Bart. Burns, who could not let a wrong thing go by, said the real gait was more of a swing than a march, and Bart said nobody asked, and took one more lurching step for Mira before he sat.

"We'll come back to this one," he said, picking the card up again, "because the household's got a lot to say about it. Headline for now: a big cell has a small army of marching — swinging! — soldiers."

He talked about the big cell's little memory. "It can hold a state that isn't a reaction to right now. It remembers a thing that already happened and keeps remembering it after the thing is over." He waxed about chemotazis and waned about directional sensing. 

"Phagocytosis," Bart said. "Eating, but the grown-up kind. Not absorbing — *engulfing.* Wrapping yourself around a whole other thing and pulling it inside into a sealed pocket. An amoeba does it to a bacterium; one of your own white blood cells does it to whatever's making you sick, right now, while we're talking. And —" he tapped the central yolk again — "it's the move. It's how the tenant got in in the first place. Phagocytosis is the deep ancestor of eating *and* the thing that built this whole kind of cell, because one time the wrapping-around happened and the pulling-inside happened and then the digesting didn't."

## The Net

Lisa moved. "Clade block. Bart."

Bart had the Eumetazoa card already out of the cigar box — tarot-sized, on the heavy matte stock he'd matched to Cheryl's pitch portfolio by feel, his ink elevation of this quarter's Bramble on the face of it, the back still blank for the words they'd argue into being tonight. He laid it down and set a finger on it like a man putting down a winning hand. At his elbow, where everyone else had beer, stood a pint glass of something a faintly poison-colored that he kept topping from a black and green can.

He read the title, a man selling cereal to children. "YOU-met-a-ZO-a." Then, normal: "Mouthful that means the first *real* animals. An animal six hundred million years dead, so bear with me. Not the sponge — a sponge is a co-op, cells sharing a building and splitting the water bill. This" — the card — "is the first thing that's a *body.* Tissues doing different jobs, and something to pull them together. Jellyfish, sea anemones, coral. The hydra, mostly — the one we kept reading about."

"And the plane I want to land tonight is the breakthrough, which is *wire.* Nerve. The thing to get about a neuron is that nobody invented it to think — it's a skin cell that took a different job, an ordinary tissue cell that learned to carry a charge fast. The whole leap is *speed:* the animal can do a thing in a thousandth of a second instead of a slow chemical second. Not intelligence. Quickness. Which is the same move this machine's been pulling its whole life — nobody ever hands evolution new parts. It takes what's lying around and gives it a different job."

"And the part I like — there's no brain in it. No head, no center, nobody in charge. A jellyfish is a *net:* nerve spread evenly through the whole animal. Push a live hydra through a sieve, mash it to a soup of loose cells, and the cells find each other and crawl back into a working animal, net and all — no plan written down, no foreman. The coordination's everywhere and nowhere at once. Hold onto that; it pays off in ten minutes."

## Voltage

"Hand it to Burns," Lisa said, and Bart said, "One thing first," and Lisa said, "There it is," because lately there was always one thing first, and the one thing was the same man.

"The net being everywhere and nowhere — I said hold it. Here's why. The coordinating is *older* than the nerve. Before there was a single neuron, the body was already talking to itself in voltage — cells holding little electrical charges, leaking them through gated tunnels to the cell next door, and that pattern of charge is part of what tells a clump of cells what to build and where. There's a guy, Michael Levin — he painted the voltage pattern of an eye onto cells in a frog embryo that had no business growing an eye, and they grew one. A whole working eye in the wrong place. He cuts a flatworm and gets it to come back two-headed, and stay two-headed, because he changed the electrical pattern it was holding, not the genes."

"Bart." Marge, mild.

"Getting there." He was lit now. "He's got these — *anthrobots.* You take plain cells off a grown person and on their own they ball up and start swimming around on little hairs, patching wounds in a dish. Nobody designed them. They just figure out a body to be. It is the coolest thing I have ever read and I will not be taking questions."

"And it's contested," Lisa said. "The part Bart skates past."

"The *engineering* isn't contested. The eyes are real, the worms are real, the bots are real. What people fight about is the *story* he wraps it in — that all of it's cognition, the cells have little goals, intelligence runs all the way down to the voltage. That part's a coat. I love the machine under the coat."

Lisa let a beat go, the late kind. "You do know his coat is *my* coat."

"Different conversation. Burns. The world. Go." And he handed off a beat before she could land it — which the narrator marked, because it was the second time in five minutes he'd changed the subject one step ahead of losing the point.

## Allostasis

Bart took the card back. "Four new powers in the animal. Going fast."

"One — it eats like an animal. Before this clade, things took food in; the jellyfish *hunts* it. Goes and gets a thing, captures it, breaks it down in a gut built for the job. The hydra fires little toxin harpoons on a thread and reels its dinner in. Mouths, guts, the whole apparatus start here. And so does" — he liked this part — "the predator's gaze. First time in the planet's history anything ever *looked at* another thing as lunch."

"Two, the big one. The animal can hold more than one *normal.* Old way was a single setpoint defended to the death — one right temperature, hold the line. With nerve tissue, the animal carries different normals for different situations and aims at whichever fits. Lisa's got the word."

"Allostasis," Lisa said. "Stability through *change* instead of stability by sitting still. The animal forecasts what it's about to need and shoves the setpoint there *ahead* of the need — and it shows up the instant nerve tissue does, because you can't run a forecast without something fast enough to run it on." And the narrator, who could no longer unsee these things, noted that the forecasting was named by Lisa — who ran the room three moves out, who had everyone's next sentence loaded before they reached it.

Jasmine had Mira's confiscated juice box and was drinking it. "Do plants do that? My tomatoes know it's morning before the sun's up. They move first."

"Plants run the *old* way," Burns said. "Homeostasis, not allostasis. Beautifully — water, light, the daily clock, every line held. But a plant *holds* its lines. It doesn't go shoving a setpoint to a new place and then guarding the new place. Which is why" — he paused, because he loved this — "a plant cannot get fat."

A beat.

"A plant can't get fat," Bart repeated.

"A plant cannot get obese, no."

"Say more about that immediately."

"Obesity isn't eating too much — your body does that and corrects for it," Burns said, delighted to be asked. "Obesity is the *setpoint moving.* Something in you decides the new, higher weight is the right one to defend, and then it defends it — fights you back up every time you diet down. That's allostasis gone wrong: a forecaster betting on a famine that never comes and banking against it forever. The kind of forecasting that can bet wrong about your own body and then dig in to protect the bad bet — *that's* what needs nerve tissue. A tomato can be stunted, sick, dead. It cannot decide it ought to be heavier and then defend the mistake. You'd need a nervous system to be that stupid about your own body."

"Best fact I've heard all year." Bart wrote PLANTS CAN'T GET FAT on the blank back of the card, under NEURONS.

"That is not going on the card," Marge said.

It stayed on the card.

"Capacity three," Bart went on. "The animal learns — the dumbest kind there is —"

"It doesn't learn," Lisa said.

"I said the *dumbest* —"

"It doesn't learn."

"Fine. It's not 'this goes with that,' nothing filed away anywhere. But poke a hydra gently over and over and it quits bothering to react. Hit it hard once and it stays jumpy about everything for a while after."

"That's habituation. And sensitization. There are words."

"It's a volume knob on a reflex."

Lisa considered it. "Fair enough."

"Four's the quiet one nobody claps for. The little cargo-motors inside a single cell — the ones that walk packages around — are now hauling freight down nerve fibers that run the length of the whole animal. Same motors the eukaryote already had. Just asked to go farther. Nothing new built. Old engines, longer roads." He flipped the card face-down. "That's the four."

## Attention

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart slid the top card out of the cigar box.

He had drawn the animal head-first: the head big and the body small beneath it, every sense crowded to the leading end, as if the head were the whole point and the rest were only how it got the head somewhere. He held it up and read the top of it. "Bi-la-TERI-an."

"Last clade, the animal had nerves and no boss," Bart said. "A net, all through it, everything answering at once, nobody up front. This one's got a front. A head — the senses gathered at the leading end, all aimed the way the animal's going. And here's the thing the net could never do." He set the card down. "It picks a direction. ## Three Hundred and Two

"OK, my turn to sound smart. Four things this animal does," Bart said, "all off the same kit. One — it heads at a thing. Something new shows up, it points itself and goes to find out, or points away and leaves. Two, and this is the one this clade's really about, so hold it: it pairs. This goes with that. It doesn't know why this goes with that; it just clocks that it does and starts acting on it. Learning, the dumb kind, the kind with no theory under it. Three — it sees a little ahead. Minds its own state, runs its own clock, goes to feed before it's empty. Four — it holds off. Can have a thing it'd normally do loaded up and not do it, because the moment's wrong. Ready, and choosing not to."

"Here's what I want on the tape, though." Lisa didn't say it the way she said most things. "All four of those, plus the affect, plus a temperament — there are bold worms and shy worms, same genes, different animals — you can find every bit of it in a worm with three hundred and two neurons. Three hundred and two. You can sit down and count them; people have. It explores, it learns, it sleeps, it holds a program back, it's got a valence axis and an arousal axis, and no two of them forage quite alike. We carry a couple hundred billion neurons around in our heads, and we are not, trick for trick, doing one single thing tonight that the worm isn't already doing with three hundred and two. And not one of the three hundred and two is the wanter. Lay them out, account for every one — there's no cell the others report to, nothing in there picking the thing and aiming the body at it. The wanting is the three hundred and two turning the animal toward the thing and going — the turning-toward is the whole of it, with nothing behind it. There's no separate story for us, either: no wanter in the worm, no wanter in us. Just the going, all the way up."

The room sat with that a second. It was the kind of fact that moves the furniture, and Lisa had set it down flat, the way she set down the things that were actually wonders.

"And the pairing Bart mentioned is the one to hold onto, because it's where the night's going. So hold onto how it actually works — in the one animal simple enough to watch it happen." She tipped her chin at Burns. The worm was his.

"The worm and the salt," Burns said, happy, because of course it came back to the worm. "A worm likes salt. Bare salt is not worm food, but worm food tends to be salty, so untrained worms crawl towards salt, every time. Now separate the worms into three training groups. First: starve them with no salt anywhere near it, then offer it salt — it goes right to it. Salt's still good; going hungry on its own didn't touch the salt preference. Second: feed them well, salt in the dish the whole while, belly full — later, salt's still good. But the third." He turned the bottle. "Starve them with the salt right there in the dish with it. Hungry, and the salt all around it the whole time. That one, afterward, crawls away from the salt — off the very concentration it went hungry in. Same salt the other two still love." He set the bottle down. "The change requires the bad to land while the salt is present. The hunger writes on whatever happened to be standing there when things went wrong, and what was standing there was the salt, so the salt goes bad. The inside state reaches out and sours the thing it was touching. Three hundred and two neurons, and it does that."

## Credit and Blame

The tetrapod settled at the edge of the light, having decided that was where it lived now, and the meeting let it go. Lisa turned to a clean page. "All right. That was the heavy one. This next part's almost a parlor game — four little rules the learning runs on. Somebody start."

Marge started. 

"I'll frame it first." She set her mug down. "The world does not hand a worm one thing at a time. Something happens that matters — the dish, the hunger, the salt, the smell, the food — and there are a dozen other worms in the dish with it, wriggling around, bumping into each other, making squishy noises."

Mira wrinkled her nose. 

"The animal cannot learn that all of those things predict the food; that's worthless, that's everything predicting everything. So it has to decide which one earns it. Which one gets the credit." A beat. "Or the blame. The whole game has a name too dull to say out loud, so I'll say it: credit assignment. Who's responsible for what just happened."

"Why it's new — until this quarter the thing couldn't pair anything, so it never had to ask. Genes don't sit up after dinner deciding what to blame; genes just count who had children. The minute a thing learns inside one life, it inherits the question of what to learn from. And that" — she nodded around — "is what Pavlov's dogs were actually up to, under the drool. Everyone keeps the cartoon: bell, food, dog drools at the bell, as if the dog glued two things together because they showed up at once. It didn't. The dog's a tighter accountant than that. These four are four of the ways it decides who's on the hook. That's the frame. Let's go around."

"Eligibility trace." Bart didn't sit up. "Whatever cue's still warm when the payoff lands gets the credit. There's a window — under a second. A signal inside the window's still eligible, it gets written to. A signal five seconds back is too cold; doesn't matter it predicted the thing just as well, it missed the window. Doesn't matter if it's loud or weird, it missed the window. Recent gets paid. Old gets nothing."

A silence.

"That's the entire explanation," Burns said.

"That's the entire rule. The window's short. I sized the explanation to the window."

Burns took the next. "Latent inhibition. The simplest. A cue that's been around forever predicting nothing — the other worms wriggling around, the hum that's always off and on, the light that's always there — gets filed as furniture. Background. Then one day the furniture starts mattering: the hum comes on right before the shock. And the animal's slow to learn it, slower than if the hum were brand new, because it already wrote that thing off as nothing. That's latent inhibition. You cannot easily promote a cue you've already shelved."

Lisa went. "Overshadowing. Two cues, both there before the food, both honest — a loud tone and a dim light, both reliable. The animal learns the loud one and ignores the light. Not because the light's the worse predictor; it's just as good. The loud one simply got to the credit first and left little behind. The salient cue overshadows the quiet one."

"No notes," Bart said.

"I'm choosing not to hear that."

"Fine. I'm used to being overshadowed."

Lisa tipped her chin at Marge. "Yours."

"Mine is the one that proves the whole thing, which is why I kept it. Blocking. You teach the worm and then it stops learning. So, you teach it the faint tone means shock — solid, reliable, it has it cold. It learns the pair. Then you add a light: faint tone and bright light together, still the shock, every single time. The light is a flawless predictor. Right there, every time, never once wrong. And bright." She held up a finger. "And the animal never learns it. Never gives it a look. Because the tone already explained the shock — the credit was already assigned, the books balanced, nothing left owing for the light to come collect. A cue that tells you only what you already knew earns nothing."

She let it sit.

"Do you feel blocked Marge."

"I'm sorry, did someone say something? All I head was a squishy noise. Must have been my latent inhibition of Bart jokes."

"That was fun."

## Reinforcing

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart had the top one up already, and the room saw he'd been busy. It was the new kind of card now — the venn from the spring, the four circles crossing, this clade's words inked small and neat into it, a man all the way in on a system he'd decided he liked. He read it off. "VERR-te-brett ... Favre."

"Thank you," Lisa said.

"Last clade the animal grew a front," Bart said. "A head, the senses crowded up at the leading end, and it could swing the whole body at a thing and go. This one"—he drew a knuckle down the card, an imagined line head to tail—"grows a spine. Backbone, a skull around the brain, and down in the floor of that brain a little switchboard that's the actual news tonight. Last time it could aim itself at a thing it wanted. This time it can do a thing, get paid a beat later, and work out that the thing it did is what got it paid. Burns has the name."

"Reinforcing," Burns said, and stood, because he was not going to take this one sitting down.

"And here's the trick it had to solve to earn the word, because it's harder than it sounds." He held up a hand, thumb and finger an inch apart. "You do a thing. The good doesn't come now. It comes—" he widened the gap "—a couple seconds on. A fish bumps a lever, the food drops three seconds later, and by then the fish has done six other things. So which one gets the credit? The world's already moved on. Something in there has to reach back across the gap, past the six other things, and lay the credit on the one that earned it."

"This is the eligibility trace that Marge gave me last quarter. But that lasts less than a second."

"Temporal credit assignment," Lisa said. "There's a signal that runs on the gap itself. It jumps when the payoff beats what you expected and drops when it comes in under, and the size of the miss is the size of the correction. Dopamine, in an animal — and it isn't carrying pleasure, it's carrying surprise, the distance between what you bet and what you got, and it spends that distance reweighting the move that led in. A critic that scores the bet, an actor that shifts toward the moves that score. The actor does, the critic grades, the grade bends the doing."

"In English, it does more of what paid and less of what cost," Marge said, the beer in the two-handed grip and not set down, "and I want it kept in English, because terms like actor and critic make it sound like we've handed the brain a little accountant. The fish doesn't know the lever made the food. The knowing is ours. The doing-more is its."

"Doing-more covers it," Bart said, happy, because doing-more was a thing you could say and wire. "It's the dumb learning again, except last clade the thumb landed on what it saw — you soured it on a sight. Now it lands on what Bramble did. Same dumb thumb, turned around. That's the new wire, and it's most of why I'm employed."

"Same engine, except it doesn't run at the same pitch in every animal," Lisa said. "The signal I just gave you — the one that grades the bet — has a gain on it, and it isn't set the same fish to fish. Run it high and you get an animal that leans hard at anything that might pay. Run it low and you get one that hangs back and waits to be sure. Same wiring, same chemical, two settings of the one knob. There's a name for the axis, and it's on the card now. Coping Style."

"Two fish," Burns said, like it was a blockbuster premise. "Drop something new in a tank and watch them. One goes straight at it — noses it, owns the corner inside a minute, runs hot and commits, learns the tank fast and quits being afraid of it. The other hangs at the back wall and watches, and keeps watching, slow to move and slow to forget the thing that scared it. Curious and cautious. You find the same two in trout, in rats, in a barn full of pigs, in a troop of monkeys — it sorts the same way every time anyone's looked for it. And neither one is the winner, which is the part people can't stand. Good year, the curious fish eats first. Bad year, the cautious fish is the only one of the two still alive to eat, because it never once went near the thing that turned out to be a heron."

"And it holds. That's the size of it," Marge said, the beer still in the two-handed grip and sipped. "It's the way the animal meets a new thing — the fish cautious on Tuesday and cautious clear across the week, which is the only reason you get to call it anything at all. It is dialed to lean, and so it leans, and we are the ones standing over the tank calling that a kind of cowardice."

Only one word on the card was new this clade, and where it sat was a board question. Lisa waved the geometry off to the back of the night — "that's framework; we'll seat it at the board" — and they read it the way they read it now, a quarter of practice in and already the shape they all thought in: the words, not the regions.

Burns took the pace-of-life corner first, because it was animal and he liked the animal. "It tends to arrive as a package," he said. "The curious one isn't only curious — it runs a hotter engine, grows up fast, breeds early, spends itself and goes. The cautious one banks it: slow, long, careful with the candle. Live fast or live long, and the nerve tends to match the clock."

"It tends to, in the story," Lisa said. "The trouble is that when somebody measures the metabolism against the behavior, animal by animal, the line mostly isn't there. So I keep this one on the card in pencil. Development Style — and we lean on it lighter than the rest, because the rest of the card earned its ink and this corner is still arguing with the data."

"Lighter. Fine," said Burns, who wanted the deep time and not the bookkeeping.

"But the new word's solid, and it's the one the whole quarter turned on," Lisa went on, not letting him leave yet. "It doesn't just do, now — it gets better at the doing. That's a different appetite than wanting the thing across the room. It's wanting to be good at getting to it. Mastery. The drive to improve your own play."

"And when that drive holds steady enough that the rest lines up behind it," Marge said "when the proactive animal is also the quick study is also the one that takes a corner and keeps it, the same suite turning up in the tank, in the maze, in the open field — that's a kind you can pick out on sight." She did not love the word she had for it — syndrome made a plain regularity sound like an ailment — but she used it, because it was the accurate one and the naming was hers. "Behavioral syndrome: Mastery riding with the jumpy drive, Excitability, the two of them traveling together. And it rides on the Coping Style, downstream of it, not a thing of its own. A regularity you can name. Not a someone. A kind."

"And the Coping Style's got its own floor," Burns said, picking it up where she'd set it down. "There's a fellow I know — Jaap Koolhaas — spent thirty years dropping new things into tanks and pens and clocking which animals walked up and which froze, and he found the wiring under the two fish. Here's the turn, the part nobody guesses: the bold one hasn't got the quieter alarm. Same alarm in both. It runs on two arms — a fast one, the adrenaline arm, the jolt, the heart going before you've decided anything and gone as quick as it came; and a slow one, the cortisol arm, the long hold that sits in the body an hour after and won't let the scare go. The bold animal runs the fast arm hot and keeps the slow one down — hits the thing, takes the corner, shakes it off by lunch. The wary one runs it backwards — fast arm low, slow arm holding the floor — so it freezes, stays frozen, still flinching at the heron a week on. It is not how loud the alarm rings. It's which arm gets to drive. A ratio." A beat. "And the lean toward the thing that pays — that Coping knob of Lisa's — that's a third hand riding over both arms, tipping the whole animal toward the reward or off it."

"And it's old," Burns said, turning the beer in his hand without drinking it. "That switchboard in the floor of the brain — the basal ganglia — runs the identical job in a lamprey, a jawless thing with barely a face, that it runs in the four of us at this table. Half a billion years and nobody's improved on it, because it was right the first time. The vertebrate's one great invention is a machine for deciding what worked."

"And that's not the only old switchboard in there," he said. "Right alongside it runs another one, every bit as old, for the things the animal never has to decide at all." He laid two fingers on his own sternum. "Whether you're burning slow and steady or spending it all in one hard burst — cruising or sprinting, the long aerobic draw or the fast anaerobic spend — you don't pick that moment to moment. Something below the floor reads where the body is and sets it, and you learn you've done it about the time it's done. A great deal of the lineage's old regulating got folded down into that floor, taken off the part of the animal that competes for a look and run automatic instead — not freed up, just out of the race. The routine stops entering, so it stops winning. What's left to compete are the things worth competing for."

Ch 7

## Simulating

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart turned up the next one. He read the title in the voice that sold cereal. "MAM-mal. Now with hair."

"Thank you," Lisa said.

"This is the one we are," Bart said, which he says every clade and seems to mean a degree more each time. "Hair. Milk — feeds its own young off its own body. Three little bones in the ear that started out as jaw; the jaw gave up two of its bones to hear better, which is a trade I'd have argued against and the record says paid off. Warm all the time, off its own burn. And a fresh sheet of bark thrown over the top of the brain, the neocortex, where the new tricks get run." He tapped the middle of the card. "Burns has the animal. I've got the trick, and it's the best one yet, so I'm handing the animal off fast."

"That's the word on the card," Lisa said. "Simulating. The vertebrate learned what paid — did a thing, got graded, did more of what scored. But it had to *do* the thing to find out. This one runs the thing first, inside, against a model of the world it carries, and only then moves."

"It dreams the maze before it swims it," Bart turned the old vertebrate card half over in his hand. "Last clade you'd point this thing down a maze and it'd swim every wrong turn to find the right one, the whole thing, every time, with its actual body. Couldn't run one corridor in its head."

"Not couldn't," Marge said, the beer in the two-handed grip. "There was nowhere for the run to happen. You can't rehearse in a room that hasn't been built. What's new in the mammal isn't a better imagination — it's that there's finally an inside-the-brain roomy enough to try a thing without doing it."

"And you can watch it happen," Lisa said, and this was the part she'd waited for. "Put a rat at the mouth of a maze it knows, before it's taken a step, and the cells in its brain that fire for *places* light up in order — down the corridor, around the corner, to the spot where the food is — running the route it's about to run, ahead of the body, while the body holds still. It's playing the path forward. And when it's done and resting, the same cells run the route again backward, settling the lesson in. Forward to plan, back to keep. Friston's name for the imagination is *active inference* — the animal carries a model of the world and acts to make the world come out the way the model already predicts."

The new words went up, and Lisa read down them. The sweep-the-path-first one she'd just shown them — trying the route in the head before the body commits — she called Algorithmic Cognition, and let the rat stand for it. Beside it sat a stranger one: a rat run on a hard problem will, given the option, decline the trial it isn't sure of and take a guaranteed smaller reward instead — it knows when it doesn't know, and bails. "Reflective," Lisa said. "Thinking about its own thinking, in a brain the size of a grape." And a third: a mammal that has already learned a thing will wall off a new lesson that fights it, shy from the unfamiliar on principle, and dig in against being trained at all. "Autonomous Cognition. What makes a mammal hard to push around. Comes standard." 

"Two tendencies fall out of having an inside like that," Lisa went on, "and the new knob rides the first." She drew a finger between the circles. "Attention is how far down in the minute it lives — immersed, the cold water on the skin and nothing else in the world, or surveying, stood back with the whole field at once and the day going past like weather. That's the knob. The second one's quieter — Remembering Style: how it takes the minutes afterward and stitches them into a thing that happened, a day it can tell itself back. The first self feels the trip. The second self is the one that decides, a week on, that the trip was good." She let it sit. "And the second self doesn't clock off at night. It runs the day's tape again in the dark — which is a thing we'll get to when we get to the part where it sleeps."

The drive under all of it had a name, and Lisa gave it plain. "Autonomy. The drive to go on your own model of how things could be instead of on whatever's in front of you. What lets the rat leave the wall it's been hugging and strike out across the open, because it's carrying the room in its head and doesn't need the wall to tell it where it is anymore."

"There's a word for the held thing, and it's new this clade," Marge said, steady again. "A model with a *who* in it. The animal's own self, carried as one more object in its own picture of the world, the figure the simulating runs around. Self-Model. We've watched it come on all quarter." She did not say where.

"Give them the machine under it," Marge said, "or they'll think I'm being poetic. I'm not. There's an organ." She tipped the bottle at Lisa.

Lisa came up off the card for this one; it was the part she'd built the spring around. "One trick, run twice. A world model and a self model. The world model's job is to predict what's out there before it lands. *I'm seeing a thing because there's a thing out there throwing light at me.* The world model faces out. It eats what comes in the eyes and ears, and the real world corrects the model a hundred times a second."

She turned her hand over. "The self-model is run by the same machinery pointed at the animal instead of the field. *I'm doing this because I'm thirsty.* And here's the tell — the self model doesn't take its data feed from the eyes at all. It feeds off three places deeper down. Not the world. The body, the history, and the stakes. That's the stuff the inward model is built out of."

"And the wiring shows it." She had the pleasure on her she got when the anatomy did her arguing for her. "The mammal's cortex, what Bart likes to call the brain bark. It's made of sheets. Six sheets deep, and the fourth one down is the input — where the signal comes up from the body below to get checked against the world. In the world model, the visual cortex, that input is fat and packed, because the whole job back there is taking the world *in.* But up front, where the self model sits, the input layer is just gone. Thinned to almost nothing. Back of the head takes the world in and checks it. Front of the head throws the forecast down and runs on it."
