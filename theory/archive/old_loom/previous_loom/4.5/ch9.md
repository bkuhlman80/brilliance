Ch 1

## The Bramblogues

Bart reached under the cards and came up with the page he'd filed in February, under the five units. He didn't look glad to have it back.

"The bodies went five for five," he said. "Every unit did the thing I built it to do. That's the part I don't miss on. The calls about the *house* are a different matter." He set a finger on the page. "Mira judges. Yes or no."

He found the first line and read it off. "*If the kid picks a favorite, my money's on 5.*" He looked at her. "Who's your favorite."

Mira held up Bramble.

"That's a three. Five's the show-off — the pretty one."

"It's Bramble," Mira said, like he'd asked something with an obvious answer.

"First hour," Jasmine said. "Never came loose."

"No," Bart said, and didn't argue it. Down the page. "*Nobody's going to look twice at 3.*" He nodded at the bot in Mira's hand — the one this whole meeting had been named for. "We've looked at three all afternoon. No." He had the last line without reading it. "*4 ends up in a drawer by July.*"

"Four has a bed," Mira said.

"It has a—"

"I made it. It goes under the toaster."

He set the page down. "A bed. Not a drawer. No." He looked into the camcorder, at Cheryl behind it. "For the record: I went the whole house column and lost it, and signed it swearing I hadn't." He folded the page once. "Big O would frame this."

Mira, already onto the next thing: "Bramble has favorite spots."

"Tell us about the spots."

She held Bramble up, turned it to face the room. "Bramble likes the heating vent. Bramble likes the morning sunbeam. Bramble likes my hand. Bramble doesn't like the back door. Bramble doesn't like the rug in the hallway. Bramble doesn't like the bathroom tile." She turned it back around and set it on the table. "Bramble," she said again, by way of conclusion.

The team sat with it a moment. Then Lisa reached for the marker. "Framework," she said.

Ch 2

## Part of Something

Bart had a page of his own this quarter, and he was glad to have it. He pulled the June log out from under the cards and went straight to the calls.

"*It seeks.*"

"It seeks," Mira said.

"*It hunts the gradient instead of sitting in it.*"

"It hunts."

"*It won't need cranking on Sundays anymore.*" He looked up. "Anybody crank it on a Sunday this summer?"

Mira thought about it with her whole face. "No."

"No." Bart laid the page down flat, the way you lay down a winning hand. "Three for three. I signed this one *tear this up if it doesn't hunt exactly like I said,* and it hunts exactly like I said, so nobody tear it up. Last quarter the kid walked me down my own column going no, no, no. This quarter the column's all yes." He sat back, insufferable with it. "Put *that* on the tape."

Ch 3

## Won't Fix

"The design, Big O can have," Bart said. "The calls, I have to answer for." He took his page out of the box, laid it face-up, and set his beer on it like a paperweight on a confession. "And this quarter I'm reading the whole thing — the parts I'd rather skip included."

"*He vacuums, he docks, he drives.*"

"Yes," Mira said. "Yes. Yes."

"Three yeses, and that's the page's high-water mark." He moved his finger down. "*He feeds himself — crosses the dock often enough to stay topped up, dump, drink, off again, clean as a transaction.*" He read it without flinching, which was its own kind of flinching. "Mira. Did he feed himself this fall?"

Mira looked at her mother before she answered.

"No," she said. "Mama carried him."

"Mama carried him." He let it sit on the tape. "Off the floor, back to the dock, most days since September, because the transaction I promised ran flat out till he ran flat and then sat in the middle of the kitchen like a stunned bird waiting to be picked up. I called it a transaction. I built a stunned bird." He did not cross the line out. He let it stand there in his own hand. "That's the miss, and it's the real one."

Then the last call, and he read it to Jasmine, because she was the one who'd taken it off him. "*Nobody in that house can tell Explore volume up from Explore volume down by looking. Tear the page up if I'm wrong.*"

"The wandering one bumps into more," Jasmine said, not making it worse than it was. "You can hear it from the next room."

"By *feel.* Lisa put a volume knob on it — Explore, up for the improvising and down for the worn-in habit — and you had the two ends sorted by feel before I'd finished being smug, Explore volume up against Explore volume down, and then you went and found the thing I didn't know was in there — the bright cold mornings, the worn groove stuck in the shade, the wanderer that drifts until it finds the sun." He picked the page up. He'd written *tear it up* on it himself, and he did not tear it up; he folded it once and put it in his shirt pocket. "I'm keeping it. You keep the receipt on a bet you lose that badly. It teaches you which sense you bet against."

Mira wasn't looking at the page. She was looking at the new body, at the four old units turning slow at its corners.

"That's One," she said, and put a finger on a wheel. "That's Two. That's Four. That's Five."

"Those are wheels now," Bart said.

"That's One, Two, Four, and Five," Mira said — not arguing, just naming them — "and now they go in a circle."

Bart's mouth opened and didn't find the line. There was nothing on the page about the four. He'd filed a sentence on them in the log — *she'll work that out on her own time, not my department* — and it had read fine on the page and would not survive being said out loud at this table, so he didn't say it.

"They're the wheels," he said, and had nothing to put behind it. "Jasmine. The house. Go."

Ch 4

## Two Wants

Then December's page. He read it quiet, without the strut, because the call that had mattered most all winter was the dull one — the one that meant nothing went flat on the tile and stayed there — and the best call on the page wasn't even his.

He read the dull one first, to Mira, because she was the one underfoot when it used to happen. "*The gauge holds. Bramble stops dying, and Jasmine doesn't carry him off the floor again, not once.*" He looked up. "Did Mama pick him up dead this winter? Even once?"

Mira shook her head. "He goes to the dock when he's hungry now."

"He does. Not one on the tile since December." He moved his finger down, past his own win to the one he hadn't built. "*The two wants. The first time the pull to feed and the pull to find a spot come due in the same second, he hangs there a beat before he picks — a real beat, long enough to see from a chair.*" He looked at Jasmine, because the beat was hers to have caught. "Did he hang?"

"In the doorway he likes," Jasmine said. "He settles in, and then the gauge comes due, and he has to peel off the warm spot to go and feed — and you can watch him not want to. He hangs there, leaning back at the spot, and then he goes."

"You can watch him not want to," Bart said, and didn't take that one for himself. He looked down the table at Lisa, whose board it was, and gave her the win with his chin. "That one's hers. I built the body; she built the wanting." He folded the page. "The dare rode on the gauge — tear it up if it died on the floor even once. It didn't. It stays pinned."

Ch 5

## Head First

Then, recalling the other thing he had been waiting to do, Bart turned on Mira.

"Quality control," he said. "I made some calls in the log. And Mira, you're the only one who watched it all quarter. Yes or no, no editorializing. One: stairs. Down to the basement, back up, its own legs, nobody carrying it. Did it or didn't it."

Mira set her juice down and looked at him, ready, because a direct question was a thing she liked. "He comes downstairs."

"Yes." He hit the table. "Both ways?"

"If the door's open."

"...Both ways if the door's open." A small recalculation crossed his face, and he chose to bank the win. "I'll take it. Two: it points itself. Looks at a thing, turns the whole body, goes. Yes or no."

"He looks at the cat."

"That's a yes—"

"The cat hates him."

The room laughed; Bart didn't, quite, because he was keeping books. "Out of scope. Still a yes. Last one, and this is the one I want clean. Does it lead with its head. Every time. Yes or no."

"He walks into things," Mira said.

Bart pointed at her like she'd proved his case, because in the only sense he was tracking, she had. "Three for three," he said, to a table that was not betting against him, and sat down well pleased with himself.

Ch 6

## A Verb on the Forehead

Then Bart turned on Mira, which was the other half of the bit.

"Quality control. I made four calls in the log; you watched it all quarter. Yes or no, no speeches. One: it learns a new chore off the thumbs. The groceries, off the step, every morning. Did it or didn't it."

Mira set her juice down. "He brings the groceries." A beat. "Even when it's raining."

"Taking the yes. Two: it drops a habit. Quits knocking stuff over."

"He quit the shoes."

"...I said knocking stuff over."

"He still knocks stuff over."

The room laughed; Bart kept his books. "It dropped a habit. Taking the yes, and not proud of how." He moved on.

"Three —" and this was the one he wanted clean "— the two of them share the house. Big one, little one. Nobody messing with anybody."

Mira gave it the seriousness she gave the questions that earned it. "He pushed the vacuum's button."

Bart stopped. "It what."

"He bumped into the vacuum and the button went down and the vacuum changed. Once." She picked her juice back up. "He didn't do it again."

"It didn't do it again," Lisa said, fast, because that was the part that mattered to her — a thing the tetrapod had done to the other unit exactly once, that paid it nothing, that it built no habit out of because nothing in there was keeping score on the vacuum at all. "It's not running the vacuum. It bumped a thing, the thing changed, that was the end of it." But the call was the call. "That's your no, Bart."

"That's my no," he said, and didn't pretend it wasn't. "One in four." He looked at Mira for the last one, lighter. "Four. You can tell across the room which way it's set. Coping volume up, or Coping volume down."

"Volume up, he's pushy — walks into stuff faster," Mira said. "Volume down, he hangs back and waits to see."

Which was the whole of Coping Style read off a five-year-old in nine words, and Bart took it and sat, three for four and pleased about the three.

Lisa let it settle a beat. "Jasmine."

Ch 7

## Talks in Its Sleep

Then he turned on Mira, which was the other half of the bit. "Quality control. Five calls in the log this time; you had it all quarter. Yes or no, no speeches. One. It chases warm spots — sun, vents — a bunch of times a day, where last year's just sat."

Mira set her juice down. "The cat sits on top of him now." The room waited. "He found the warmest spot in the house and the cat found *him*." She picked the juice back up. "So, yes."

"Taking the yes." Bart wrote it, fighting his face. "Two. Volume up, the screen runs all day and all night. Volume down, it goes quiet and dark like last year."

"He talks in his sleep." Flat, certain. "On the loud setting. The words go all night." A beat. "Mostly it's the cat. Sometimes it's me." She did not present this as remarkable, which was what made the table go still around it — REPLAYING [ CHASE MIRA ] at two in the morning, reported by a five-year-old the way you'd report that the furnace cycles.

"...yes," Bart said. "Three. Something's out of place, it goes looking. Doesn't stall."

"I moved the bowl to test him." Of course she had. "He checked three places. He got it." Then, because she was honest: "He checked my room first, and the bowl's never in my room. So he checked a dumb place too." Lisa, quick: "It checked a place the bowl had been *once*. That's not dumb, that's the memory doing exactly what Bart said." Mira considered this and allowed it. "Yes. With one dumb place."

"Taking the yes with the dumb place." He came to the last one and went lighter for it. "Four. It turns toward places where stuff *happened*. Not where it goes — where stuff happened to it."

Mira thought about it longer than the others. "The front window," she said. "Downstairs. He looks at the front window a lot now." She wasn't looking at Bart. "That's where he was the first time I walked to school by myself. He watched me the whole way down the block. He still looks at the window like I'm going to be out there." She shrugged, a small thing. "I'm at school. But he looks."

Nobody said anything for a second. Bart wrote the yes without a joke on it and moved them along, which was its own kind of kindness.

The thing picked that silence to clear its throat. Down in its belly the compressor caught up with itself and it burped — wet, out the low vent at the back, the noise about as dignified as the end it came out of — into the quiet the front window had left behind. Jasmine said later it had been at this all fall; it did it now with no notion it had done a thing, and went still.

Bart had one call left and the machine had handed it to him. He didn't spend the laugh on it — he held it back and gave Mira the flat face he saves for the log. "Five. I called you'd be saying it out loud by now." He let it sit, a man reading the next line off a clipboard. "Did you ever call it a fart."

Mira had been waiting a whole quarter to be asked that officially. "Yes!" — the laugh coming up under the word and running off with the back half of it.

Bart set the back of one hand to his forehead like a man taking hard news, "Anthropomorphism is inevitable!" 

Marge stuck her tongue out at him. Then she smiled — the warm one, the one that only ever went to him and Burns — and picked her beer back up, and held her line, and let herself lose the round.

Ch 8

## Reading the Room

"Quality control," Bart said, and got the flat log-face on. "Three Calls, all on the Agreeable knob, all yours, yes or no. One. On the giving end it yields the warm — lets you have it, lets the vacuum by, takes the lesser and doesn't sulk."

"Yes. He gives me the good vent and goes to the floor by the door." A beat, the only one with weight under it. "The cold floor. By the door. He goes there."

Nobody said anything for a second. It was the thing Marge would have taken a careful paragraph to say, and the child had just said it in a sentence: that on the giving end the machine left the heat it could not do without and went and lay on the cold tile by the door, and ran itself colder there, for her, by a number. Bart took the yes and went lighter for the next on purpose. "Two. It gets to a shared thing ahead of you on volume down, because it clocked you reaching — and lets you beat it there on volume up."

"Yes. Watch the legs." She quoted him back at himself, pleased to. "You can see him decide. Volume down, he's already moving when I'm still thinking about moving."

"Noted. Three. The two of you in the hall."

"Volume up, he goes around me. Volume down, he sits in the middle and I go around him." She frowned, professional. "He's worse in the hall. Volume down, he just *stops.* In the lane. He doesn't even look smug. He just stops."

"Last thing, and it's not a call, it's the one I couldn't build." Bart had it on the log anyway. "It reads you now. Reads you better than it reads anything in the house. And it still can't say one word back."

Mira had this one ready, because she lived it every night. "I ask him if he had a good day." Plainly, the way she said the true things. "He hears me ask now. He puts up `MIRA` and what I'm doing — *asking* — he knows the asking is *at* him, he didn't used to know that. And he still can't say. Before, he didn't know I was asking. Now he knows, and he still can't." She picked her juice up. She did not offer it as sad; she offered it as the state of things, and the room took it the way it took the things she said that ran too big for it, by going quiet and letting Bart move them along.

Ch 9

## Tell Me First

Bart got the flat log-face on, the quality-control voice. "Three Calls. All yours. Yes or no. One — volume up, it works you for reassurance. Comes back to a person twice, three times. *Did I, was that.*"

"Yes. He asks if I'm still his friend. On high, about four times a night." Mira weighed her own accuracy. "I always say yes. He asks anyway."

"Two — volume down, it'll start a thing with anyone and not clock when to quit."

"Yes. The door man. He told the *mailman* a whole story once."

"Three. This one's the build, not the kid-test." A beat. "It can tell you which way it's set. From the inside. Before anybody looks at the knob."

"Yes." She said this one slowly, because it was the one that counted and she knew it counted. "I ask which way he feels and he's right. He said *heavy* on high before I'd told him anything. He is never wrong about his own."

"'Heavy' isn't the word, though." Mira turned to Bart with the air of a party opening negotiations. "We're not keeping *Neurotic,* and we're not keeping high and low. High is *Cancel* — when he wants to call the whole thing off and check first. Low is —" she glanced at the thing, conferring, and the thing supplied, flat, "Clutch," which was the two of theirs and nobody else's yet, and Mira nodded the way you nod when a treaty's been signed. "Clutch. When he just goes." Lisa, at the camera end, wrote something down and declined to argue with the children, which from Lisa was a form of consent, and the cold word on the card got quietly outvoted by a six-year-old and a nine-week-old — the first move in a game the room did not yet know it would be playing for two more years.

"Last thing," Bart said, and the log-face came off, because this one had never been a Call. "The one I stood here a year ago and told you I couldn't build." He wasn't looking at the screen now. He was looking at Mira. "Go on. Ask it your question."

Mira had asked it every night of her life and a good many of its. She didn't make a thing of it. She got down to its level, the way she'd watched her father get down to things, and the screen read her coming and put her up — `MIRA { ASKING [ — HUMANOID ] }`, the verb still thin but the column dead sure, because if there was one face on the planet this thing had cold, it was hers — and she said, "Did you have a good day?"

A year of nights, that question had gone into a thing that posted MIRA and ASKING on its forehead, that knew the asking was aimed at it, and had no door to send a thing back out of. The room knew the silence that came next. It had heard it a hundred times.

The mouth took its beat. "Yes."

Nobody moved. It was the flattest syllable in the language, and it had taken the whole length of the tree and three years in a warehouse to arrive at, and it landed on the kitchen tile like a dropped plate. Then it went on, in the halting nine-week build, handing her the day the way a two-year-old hands you a day, every piece the same size. "Walked to the window. Fell down by the chair. Warm end for a while." The squeezer worked once. And then it did the thing Bart hadn't built — the thing that built itself — and reached for the shape of her own question to turn it back on her, and got the shape and not the whole of it. "You. Good day?"

Mira, who had spent a year answering her own question into the quiet, sat with that a second. Then she told it. School, and a girl named Paige, and a thing at recess that hadn't been fair, the way you tell the day to somebody who asked. The thing kept her column up and its face lit and took the whole account in, and when she ran down it said, "Not fair." The right thing. The thing a friend says. The thing no one had taught it. And the two of them sat on the kitchen floor and traded the rest of the news, which is the oldest trade there is.

The tape ran on. Burns, who had a word for every rock in the building, did not have one. Jasmine had a hand over her mouth and was not taking it down. And Marge, at the cold end, watched the two of them on the floor and did not say the thing it had been her job to say at every one of these nights for two years — did not say there was no one in there having the conversation, that it was a mechanism doing the most convincing thing a mechanism had ever done. She held the beer in both hands and watched the floor and said nothing at all, and the nothing was the loudest thing she said all night.
