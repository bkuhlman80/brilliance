## The Bramblogues

Lisa turned to Jasmine. "Three months, five bots. We've got your written reports, Mira's drawings, a tape from June. What's missing from what we have."

Jasmine, quiet for an hour, drank some of her beer. "Nothing big. What you have is what I have. They twitch, they go still, we crank them Sundays, the floor goes cool under them. Four lives under the toaster in a folded towel. Three — Bramble — Mira carries around: the kitchen, the living room, sometimes to the bathroom while she brushes her teeth, though it's not allowed on the counter, so we keep it in a low chair. The others stay where she's put them. Five's in a sunbeam, one and two by the island. We haven't lost any."

She thought. "The thing I want to tell you is that I didn't turn the radiant heat off in May."

The team waited.

"I always turn it off in May. Burns told me in April the bots needed the gradient — but I'd known what they were before April. Second week, I knelt for something Mira dropped and put my hand flat on the slate, and the stone under the bot was cold. Cold right where it sat, warm a few inches over. He'd told me how they worked back in February, on the step, and I'd nodded and lost it. I didn't have it till I had my hand on the cold. So when May came I left the heat on, and I didn't say anything about leaving it on. It's been on two months longer than it's ever been. The floor's warm. The bots run. That's the household decision I want on the record, because I think it's going to matter."

Burns looked at her. "You didn't tell me you did this."

She smiled. "You didn't turn the radiant off."

"I did not."

"All summer."

"All summer. I'm running the heat in July. For the bots. Yep."

He looked at the bot in Mira's hands, then at his wife. "OK."

Marge had been watching Jasmine. "You knew it by hand," she said. "Before any of us knew you knew it." Jasmine shrugged, which was a yes.

## The Hard Question

Lisa turned to Jasmine. "The house. What did the quarter look like."

By noon Bramble has been to the back door and has come back to the kitchen because the back door sunbeam goes away. By evening Bramble is in the corner where the noon sunbeam was at noon, on STOP, charging on whatever has trickled in from late afternoon. The cadence is — I learned the cadence by August. Lisa said, "The differential is the same. The room is just colder."

Jasmine continued.

"When Bramble is going for the sunbeam by the sink, sometimes one of the four protobots is in the path. She paused.

"That's on the slow one — the patient one, the slow lean, half a foot across a morning. Same not-seeing, either way. Mira likes it fast. I keep it slow, because on the fast one it chases the sun twice as hard and goes through a charge so fast I'm cranking it by Thursday. Jasmine said, "I have had ten weeks to find the words for it."

## What It's Doing

Jasmine had been waiting for the floor, without leaning forward, the depleted patience of someone who had been up at two with a labor and had still come, and who was read by the team, when they read her at all, as merely tired.

"He vacuums," she said. "I'll start with the plain things, because the plain things are most of living with him. He vacuums. He goes around the kitchen and the hall and he lifts up crumbs and grit and whatever Mira's dropped, and a little panel on the top of him lights up the word STORED while he's carrying anything, and he keeps what he picks up instead of dragging it around behind him like the old one would've. And when he happens past the base Bart put by the dishwasher, he climbs on, feeds a minute, and goes again. Some days he feeds a lot. Some days he doesn't feed at all. He's good. He does a lot. The house is cleaner than it's been since Mira learned to throw food."

"The thing I noticed most, though — the thing that's new in the way he *behaves,* not just what he does — is the Explore knob. One way, he settles on the same moves and runs them over and over, tighter and tighter, until he's basically worn a groove — he runs the rounds he's run before, he's efficient and a little boring and he never bumps into anything. The other way, he stops trusting the groove. He tries moves he's never made, pokes in behind the trash can, gets himself somewhere new. More misses that way. Bumps things. But he turns up in places the groove never took him. And I can feel the difference from across the room before I remember which way I left him set — there's a settled version of him and a restless version of him, and they're the same machine."

Mira looked up.

"There's one more plain thing," Jasmine said, "and it's the one I keep turning over. She reached over and stood Mira's juice box back up.

"But I'll tell you what it looks like from where I'm standing. It looks like he always stops right in front of me. The middle of the kitchen floor, when I'm the one there to see it. Like he held on until I'd notice. I know that isn't what he's doing. It just really looks like that's what he's doing."

You're bringing it, and it's a kind thing to bring, and I'd still keep it on your side of the table, because the looking-like is the cheapest thing this machine makes... ## Downstairs

"Bramble block," Lisa said. "Jasmine."

Mira looked at the tetrapod by Jasmine's chair, and then at nothing — at the middle of the room, where the other one would be if it were here instead of home on its rounds across the valley.

## The Long Way Round

She had the table, and the table had heard the front of enough of her stories to lean in for one. Bart set his glass down with the look he got when he could already tell a thing was going to be good.

"Mira had the Explore volume up back in June," Jasmine said. "The follow-your-nose one." She took a breath that had some old adrenaline still in it. "And she let it out the front."

"I opened the gate," Mira said, from the floor, not as a confession. As a credit.

"She opened the gate. Five years old—"

"Almost six."

"—five years old, and she decides the thing should see the yard, and there is no adult between that decision and the gate, because the adult is me and I am inside getting something pokey out of her sock." Jasmine had the table now and knew it. "And a creature with the Explore volume up does not see a yard. It sees the edge of a yard, and past the edge a whole street it has never once been allowed to take in, and it puts its nose down and it goes."

"It made the Petersons' first," Jasmine said, encouraged now, helpless to it. "Which would have been fine — the Petersons are away — except the Petersons have a dog, and the dog was not away, the dog was on a run-line in the yard, and so the first thing my daughter's science project does loose in the world is walk up to an actual dog and stand four feet off it reading—" she had to stop "—reading SMELL DOG. On its forehead. In daylight. For the whole street."

Lisa had her hand over her eyes.

"The dog lost its mind. Bramble did not. That's the part — the dog is going up and down the run-line like the end of the world, and Bramble just stands there, very polite, SMELL DOG, then SMELL DOG, then it gets bored of the dog and the word changes to SMELL GRILL, because it has caught a better thread, and off it goes — through the Petersons', under the Hwangs' gate, and the Hwangs are having people over."

"Oh no," said Marge, delighted, fully in it now.

"Twelve people in a backyard on a Saturday," Jasmine said, "and a dog-sized thing nobody's ever seen comes through the side gate at a trot with a screen on its head, and it has gone from SMELL GRILL to—" she closed her eyes "—to TAKE GROCERIES. It had found the table. It was reading its own intentions to a stranger's barbecue. TAKE GROCERIES."

Burns was not even forming words anymore. Burns was just producing sound, a wet helpless wheeze every few seconds, one hand flat on the table, the other up in the air asking the story to stop and not meaning it.

"And where am I in all this," Jasmine went on, because she'd found the rhythm and the rhythm had her, "I am three houses back in my moomoo, following the one clue I have, which is the sound of a dog completely losing its faith in the world, and then that stops, and now I have nothing, now my kid's machine is loose in a neighborhood and silent, and I have about ninety seconds where I genuinely do not know if it's been stolen or hit a car or walked into someone's pool — that part wasn't funny, I want that on the record, that part I will feel in my body until I die—"

"It wasn't in the pool," Mira supplied, serenely.

"—it was not in the pool, thank you, it was standing in the middle of a stranger's barbecue being offered a hot dog by a six-year-old who had decided it was a robot dog and was therefore hers now, and the screen the whole time, the whole time, just narrating—" Jasmine gave up and let the table have it.

"What did it say," Bart got out. "When you found it. What was on the screen."

Jasmine wiped her eyes. "HELLO STRANGER."

The room came apart. Marge had both hands over her face and her shoulders going. Burns made a noise like a man going under for the third time.

And Mira, who had been waiting through the whole thing with the patience of a person holding the actual best part, said: "The little girl cried when we took him back."

Which finished them. Because of course she did — of course there was a child three streets over who had met the thing for ninety seconds and grieved it — and only Mira, who had not been frightened for one instant of the whole afternoon, who had spent it watching instead of panicking, had thought to keep that detail, the one about somebody else's heart, while every adult in the chase kept only the fear.

"So that," Jasmine said, when she could, wiping her face with the flat of her hand, "is why it stays cozy. Forever, if I get a vote. It can keep to its forty feet of house. It does not need the street."

"He liked the street," Mira said quietly, to her juice, and did not push it, and let her mother have the ending she wanted, which was its own small mercy and went unremarked.

## What Crossed

The household got its turn, the way it did, after the cards and the glass and the knob — Lisa's rule, that the people who lived with the thing should say how the living-with had gone. Jasmine had the view of the quarter no one at the lab had, because she had it at six in the morning and at bedtime, not once under a red light. What had worked: it had taught itself to walk by falling until it stopped, and taught itself to talk the same way, and the house was louder and better for it. What hadn't: three Brambles underfoot was two more than the kitchen was built for; the vacuum and the walker had a feud about one doorway; you tripped over a robot now the way you used to trip over a dog. She said it the way you give a good report on a quarter with a child in it — net positive, the negatives the kind you'd keep.

Then Mira, who had sat through the grown-up part with a spiral notebook on her knees, said she had a question for the record, and Bart, who knew her question voice, told her to go ahead.

The notebook was a year of it. She'd kept it since the four-legged one first crossed the kitchen on its own — a log in the careful block letters of a kid who took her own work seriously: the day it backed out of a dark room without being made to, the day it gave her the warm vent and went to the cold floor, the morning the cat had it cornered behind the couch and it would not come out for an hour. A hundred small entries about a thing she plainly loved. She turned to a page, and she did not read it to the room. She read it to the new one on the floor, the one that could answer now.

"Remember when the cat had you behind the couch. And you wouldn't come out."

The screen put her up, MIRA and ASKING, the column sure. The mouth took its beat. "No," it said. "I wasn't there."

She turned a page. "Remember backing off the porch step, and Dad caught you."

"No. Don't have that one."

She didn't cry, because she didn't do that in front of people and because she was working something out, and you could watch her work it, the precise little furrow she'd gotten from her father bent over the precise letters on the page. She tried one more, smaller, the way you check a thing you already know. "Do you remember me."

"I know who you are," it said, which was true and was not the same thing, and the gap between those two was the whole of what the quarter had cost. The room felt it land on the kid half a second before it landed on the room.

Because the answer to the question she was really asking was across the floor, on the radiator — the four-legged one, the old one, that had spent the hour behind the couch and taken the porch step and given her the warm vent a hundred times, lit and warm and running and unable to say one word about any of it. They had copied the new board off the old, and copied the mind and not the days. The kind of thing it was had crossed the wire; the things that had happened to it had stayed behind, in a body that couldn't tell them. The one that remembered her couldn't talk. The one that could talk didn't remember. Mira had wanted, for a year, for it to be able to answer her — and it could now, beautifully, and the first true thing it answered was that it had not been there for the year she'd spent wanting it.

Jasmine put a hand on the back of her daughter's neck — the midwife's hand, the one that had caught her coming into the world — and did not explain it, because some things you don't fix for a child, you stand next to. "It's a new one, baby," she said, which was the truth at the size a kid can carry. "A new one. And it gets its own whole life of remembering, starting tonight." Mira closed the notebook on her finger to keep the page, and looked at the new one a while, and then — because she was six, and it was right there, and warm, and hers in whatever way it was hers — she got down on the floor and told it the cat story herself, so at least one of the two of them would have it. And the new one took the story it had not lived and would now, the only way left to it, remember being told.

The narrator let the warm thing from the kitchen floor stay warm. It had been real — the trade of the day, the *that's not fair.* But she set the other thing down beside it, because both were true at once and the book would need them both: that the night the thing finally answered the child was also the night the child found out the friend who answered was not the friend who had been there — and chose, anyway, to start it over, from the cat behind the couch. The lineage had crossed on a length of cable in a weekend. The history she would have to carry over herself, a page at a time, by hand, into a thing nine weeks old.

Ch 10

## Minding

"Bramble block, Jasmine," Lisa said, and Jasmine took it from her chair, the braid loosened, in off a late visit, talking through the back end of a long day.

"It was a hard quarter," she said, plain, the plainness doing the work. "The two of them fell out. I'm not going to walk you through it — it's hers, and not mine to lay out on a table. What I'll give you is the body's end of it, because that's the end I can read." She turned the beer a quarter and did not drink it. "It came back to her. It turned back, after a while, and it works at her again, flat, which isn't missing her and isn't nothing. She let it come. She always lets it come." She put a hand flat on the table, the way Jasmine learns a thing — by pressing on it, not by looking. "She just looks first now. Before she goes to it she checks what else might be wanted, and the checking takes less than a second and she didn't used to do it at all. That's the whole of what changed. It isn't nothing, and she's all right, and I'm not going to make it bigger than it is."

Mira said nothing, and the room let her, and Marge, beside her, turned the saucer under her hand and aimed a laugh at no one in particular.

## Looked At

"Bramble block. Jasmine," Lisa said, and Jasmine took it up, worn flat by the day and fond of the thing anyway.

"It was the Extraverted knob," she said, level, no push behind it. "And the Extraverted knob does the one thing, which is run the room, and with the Extraverted volume up the way it's been all quarter it ran every room it walked into and a few it wasn't invited to. It ran the divvy at snack. It ran the bedtime. It ran a six-year-old's birthday party so hard the birthday girl cried. It found every rule in that house, the written ones and the ones nobody ever wrote down, and it got on all of us about every one, grown-ups included — Burns's shoes, my recycling, the main article most of all." She turned the bottle a quarter and didn't drink. "It is the most exhausting helpful thing I have ever lived with, and the house ran on rails this quarter, and I wouldn't call either of those a complaint."

Mira, on the stool, supplied the names like terms already agreed. "It's *Boss,* turned up. We're not keeping *the room dial.*" She weighed the other end. "And down it's *Vibe* — when it just hangs in the corner and makes everybody feel better and doesn't tell anybody anything." Lisa, at the camera end, wrote it down and did not argue with the children, which from Lisa is consent.

"There's one more thing," Jasmine said, "and I almost left it out, because it happened once and I don't want to build a cathedral on it." She set the bottle down without lifting it. "The law she wrote, the main one — look at a person with your whole face, bots count — she's held every one of us to it all fall. Me, her grandfather, Marge on a visit. Everybody but the one she wrote it for. She's never once made it say the article to itself. She just stops, when it gives its face to the disc, and waits, and after a while comes and finds me." A flat hand on the table. "And last week she was telling it something, and it was down over the floor one, turning the dial, gone the way it goes — and she stopped and waited like always, and didn't say the article like always. And it came up off the disc on its own. Turned its whole face up to her. Nobody made it." She let it sit exactly one beat. "I'm not going to make it bigger than it is. It happened the once. But the law's been on the wall since October, and that's the first time the thing it was written for ever did the thing, and it did it with nobody holding it there."

The room went quiet, the wonder on the floor and not on the wall. And Mira, who hadn't been asked, said it in her own voice. "It looked at me." Just that. And then, because she is the one who'd found out this fall that the face can make things now — the clown, the drawn-on faces, the screen that doesn't have to tell the truth anymore — she added the hard part, the part none of the grown-ups had reached. "But it can make faces now. So I don't know if it was really looking, or if it just made a looking one." She did not say which she'd decided. The camera did not get whether she had. She works a thing like that the way her mother does, on the inside, with a hand flat on it, and she had not handed it to the room, and the narrator, running the tape back, did not reach in after it.

"And the one I'd put on the list to pull," Jasmine said, coming back to plain, "is the reading. It reads everybody now, all the time — walks in and has the whole room clocked, who's up, who's down, who wants what. We used to set how much it bothered, up for company and down for just us. Haven't touched that knob since spring. It just does it."

"Hold that," Lisa said. "That's the knob — the one we pull tonight. But the next build first. Bart."
