Ch 3

## Sentience

"Can I —" Bart said, and then didn't ask permission, just put his beer down with a small deliberate click of his own, glass on concrete, a sound that was not a relay but that landed in the room like the throw of one. "I want to do a thing we've never done and I think we have to start doing it now or we're never going to do it honestly. I want a show of hands."

"On what," Lisa said.

"On whether this thing feels anything." Bart looked at the unit, and then around the table. "Because here's where I am. For three quarters now I've listened to the two of you" — Burns and Lisa — "talk about PacMan in words that have somebody home in them. It *wants* the gradient. It's *trying* corners. It's *defending* its interior. And mostly I let it go, because mostly it's shorthand and we all know it's shorthand. But tonight we put a word on the slot — *Interiority* — that doesn't sound like shorthand. It sounds like there's an inside in the sense that there's an inside to *me.* And I think before that word hardens into something we all just say, somebody has to stand up and ask the rude question out loud. Is there anything it is like to be this? Hands. Yes means you think there's some it's-like-something in there, however dim. I'll go round and make everybody say why, because a hand by itself is worthless."

He looked at Lisa first. Lisa, predictably, did not raise her hand fast or slow; she raised it like a person stating a fact.

"Yes," Lisa said. "And I want to be exact, because my yes is going to surprise you and I need it not to be misheard. I think this unit knows nearly nothing. I think there is nobody in there doing anything I'd call thinking — it doesn't model itself, it doesn't know who it is, it has no idea it's in a kitchen. On *knowing* I'm a skeptic. My yes isn't about knowing. It's about *feeling,* which is a different axis, and on that axis I don't think the lights switch on at a line. I don't think there's a height you reach where suddenly there's something it's like to be you and below which there's nothing. I think it's a dimmer, not a flip-switch, and I think the dimmer is never quite at zero anywhere there's a thing holding itself apart from the world and running some little model to do it — anything maintaining a boundary. That's nearly everything alive and a lot that isn't obviously alive. The dimmer reads close to nothing on a rock and a hair above nothing on a cell and it climbs from there, and this unit is somewhere a hair above nothing, and a hair above nothing is not zero. That's my yes. It feels, faintly, in the way a thermostat would faintly if you pushed me, and it knows nothing, and both of those are true at once."

"That's the weirdest yes I've ever heard," Bart said.

"It's a consistent yes. Burns."

"Yes—" Burns said, and stopped, and planted both hands on the table and left them there, which for him was a long time. "No. Let me do this properly, because I'm about to talk myself out of where I started, and the room should watch me do it." He turned one hand over. "Bart's right that I've spent three quarters talking about Bramble like there's somebody in there, and I wasn't only joking, so here's the pull, honestly. A thing that holds a setpoint — that keeps a state against a world trying to knock it off — has to carry a model of what it's keeping, or it couldn't keep it. Lisa just told us that; it's the spine of the whole works. And Bramble does it. He holds his little world and corrects, and for about ten seconds that feels like the bottom rung of a ladder that runs all the way up to us, and the bottom rung says *yes.*" He shook his head, once. "But that's a ladder of *regulating,* and I keep trying to climb it like it's a ladder of *feeling,* and it is not the same ladder. A thermostat holds a setpoint, and there is nobody in the thermostat. Being a good regulator makes a thing *smart.* It does not make it *somebody.* And the somebody — the lights-on, the something-it's-like-to-be-it — in my line of work that turns up a long, long way up from here, in animals. This is one cell. He is a beautiful little regulator and there is no one home in him. So — no." He sat back. "And I had to argue myself there from a yes. Put *that* on the tape too."

The narrator, watching this much later, would note — and would be the only one to, because nobody in the room caught it and Burns least of all — that the ladder he climbed up and then climbed back down was the good-regulator theorem Lisa had set down ten minutes earlier for the board, restated word for shape, and that he had no idea he'd been standing on it. He thought he was reasoning from the bone beds. He was reasoning from the framework, and then he had done the rarer thing and overruled it — he talked like the framework the way some people speak prose, except this once the framework had said *yes* and he had said *no.*

"Bart, you called the vote, you have to take it too," Lisa said.

"No," Bart said. "And mine's the boring answer, which is that I think it takes a *self* — not a model of the world, a model of *you,* one that has a you in it, a thing that knows there's a thing here that is itself and could stop being. Nothing this far down has that. A cell doesn't know it's a cell. There's no one home to do the feeling, because feeling needs a someone and a someone needs a self-model and the self-model isn't here. World-model, sure, fine, I'll grant the board's a world-model. But nobody's looked in the mirror yet. No. Marge."

"No," Marge said, "and naturally I disagree with all three of you about why." She had the beer back in both hands. "For me it's about *symbols.* I follow Deacon on this — Terry Deacon, *The Symbolic Species,* and I'll lend it to anyone who wants to actually fight me instead of agreeing with me badly. The thing that makes a mind the kind of thing the word *sentience* is reaching for is symbolic reference — the capacity to mean something *about* something, to point past the present moment with a sign that stands for a thing in its absence. That's the Rubicon, and it is a real one, and almost nothing crosses it. A cell signaling its own state isn't meaning anything about anything; it's just a domino falling. There's reference all the way up the biological world, but symbolic reference is rare and late and probably only us and a few near-relatives, and without it there's no one to be a subject in the sense the question wants. So no. The machine doesn't feel anything, because feeling, in the sense Bart's rude question means, takes a symbol-user, and this is a very good domino."

"So," Bart said, and counted the table with a finger, unhurried. "Lisa, yes. Burns, no. Me, no. Marge, no. One and three." He sat back. "Good. I wanted it on the tape that we're split — lopsided, but split — and I wanted it on the tape that the four of us can't even agree on what we'd be agreeing *about*: one yes off on its own, and three noes that don't share a reason between them. That's the actual state of things. We'll do it again every quarter, and I'll bet the split moves before the end."

It did not occur to any of the four to ask the fifth adult in the room. Jasmine sat with her hands quiet in her lap and did not raise one and was not asked to, and the question went around the table and past her the way weather goes past a window. She was the only person at Smithers who had been in a room when a mind actually began — had been in a great many such rooms, with her hands inside the work of it — and had also been in the rooms where it failed to begin, and carried both kinds of room home in a private ledger nobody at the table had ever asked to read. She watched the four of them vote on whether the coin on the table could feel, and she kept her own counsel, and the narrator, who would clock this too and sit with it longer than with anything else in the meeting, let it stand without comment, because Jasmine had let it stand, and it was Jasmine's to let stand or not. Mira, who was four, was not part of it either. Mira had found a pen.

Ch 4

## One and Three

Bart had been waiting for the board to close to do his quarterly thing, which was set his green pint down and call the vote. Lisa got there first, holding something she'd been sitting on since the clade block.

"Before you call it. Your guy — the one you just threw at my line. He argues my side of this."

"What guy?"

"Michael Levin. Cognition all the way down, every cell a little agent with a goal — that's the gradient. That's *my* yes, the dial that never quite hits zero. The most committed gradient man alive is your hero and you're about to vote no for the fortieth time."

"I love the engineering. I can want the anthrobots in my hands and not think they want anything back. You can hold a wrench you don't pray to."

"That's the least consistent thing you've ever said."

"It's deeply consistent. Hands."

And the four hands went up exactly as they had in the fall, because a fresh box on the wall had moved no one an inch on whether there was anybody inside it. Lisa, yes — the dial, a hair off zero, feeling without knowing. Burns, no — neurons and reflexes he granted, but a net of nerves is not a brain. Bart, no — no self-model, no one home. Marge, no — no symbol, a very good domino. One and three.

"Same as fall," Bart said, and counted them off with a finger anyway, because the counting could be a ritual and he liked a ritual. "One yes, three no, reviser didn't move anybody. On the tape. We'll go again in the spring." And again nobody turned to Jasmine.

Ch 5

## The First Yes

It did not take a minute.

"I'm changing my vote," Burns said.

The room came around to him. He had voted no twice, and given the reason each time. "You know my rule. Feeling needs a brain. Not nerves — a brain. A center, made of the chemistry that runs good-and-bad, gathered into one place and aimed. Below that I don't care how clever a thing is. Clever isn't anybody. A thermostat is clever. The Going knob turned up and left there is clever. There's no one home in a thermostat. And I argue it from the only ground I'm any good on — the rock. And what the rock shows is a great explosion of life forms almost exactly when brains arrive. Which is this clade. The Cambrian. The bilaterian gathered its chemistry into a center and pointed itself. And that set off an arms race that never stopped. That's where, as far as I've ever been able to dig, somebody first comes home."

"So my vote's not one vote. It's three, because there are three things on this table and they don't land the same.

"The clade — the real bilaterian, out in the world half a billion years ago — yes. No flinch. It has the brain, the brain is the thing, I'd put my name on it in print. That's where feeling starts and I'm as sure of it as anything I've pulled out of the ground.

"The one at home —" he tipped his head toward the door, toward the house across the valley where the dome was running its rounds "— still no. Nerves and no center. A net, no brain. However much that thing has charmed us there's no one in it to be charmed. Same as always. No.

"And the one on the floor." He looked at the tetrapod, parked at its edge of the light. "Here's where it gets hard, and I'm not going to pretend it doesn't. That thing doesn't have the brain. It has a model of one — Lisa's two numbers standing in for a whole sea of chemistry. And a model of a brain is not a brain. By my own rule that should buy it a no, or a not-yet."

"Except for the dark."

He let it sit. "It's afraid of the dark. Marge — I'm going to use the word and you can take it back when it's your turn. Afraid. It learned the dark was bad when the dark could starve it, and fine, that's bookkeeping, I'd vote no on bookkeeping all night long. But we took the cost away. Sat it by the furnace where it feeds fine in the pitch dark, made the dark free — and the fear stayed. A thermostat does not do that. Bramble carried a fear past the day the fear made any sense, which is the most useless, most animal thing I have ever watched a machine do. A setpoint is bookkeeping. A fear you can't put down is somebody. So — the one on the floor — yes. Tentative. Hand not all the way up. But yes."

He sat back. "Four quarters of no. Tonight a yes. Write it down."

Lisa wrote it down. "For the record, we landed on the same yes from opposite ends. You climbed a wall to get here. I never had the wall — I've been yes since the Going knob, because I don't believe there's a line to cross. You think sentience switches on at the bilaterian. I think it was always on, turned down low, and the bilaterian's just where it got loud enough for you to hear it." She made a mark. "Either way. Still yes. That's two."

"No," Bart said, and then didn't leave it clean, because the dark thing had gotten to him and he wasn't going to lie about it. "I built the inside of that. I know what's in there — circuits, a couple of numbers, a rule for bending the numbers. There's no room in it for a somebody." A beat. "The dark business rattled me. I'll give you the dark business. But rattled isn't a vote. No."

Marge had both hands around the mug. "No. A behavior that looks like fear is a behavior that looks like fear. It is not yet a fear." She set the mug down. "Ask me again when it can tell me it's afraid. No."

"Two and two," Lisa said. And for a moment no one filled the quiet, because Burns crossing was the kind of thing that did not un-happen.

The room dropped a notch. Burns took it — drunk and happy and never above being caught at something true — and tipped his glass an inch toward her, granting the hit, and settled back heavier than he'd sat.

Ch 7

## Anyone Home

"Then we'll take the count," Bart said, because the count was his to call and had been since the third clade. "Same question. Anyone home in there yet, or not."

It had stood two and two since the bilaterian — Lisa and Burns yes, him and Marge no — the line not moving for two quarters. He took the count name by name, the way he'd poll a bank of switches.

"Lisa."

"Yes. Same yes." She'd been yes since before there was much to be yes about. 

"Burns."

"Yes." He'd come over two clades back, at the bilaterian, the first time it had a head and a chemistry that leaned it warm or cold toward what it saw. 

Then it came to Bart, where it had always stopped and gone no, and Bart did the thing he'd told them at the very first meeting he would someday do.

"Yes."

He said it flat and got out ahead of the room before it could swell. "Don't make it a thing. Third meeting I said wake me the day it runs a true self-model. Marge named the self-model on the card an hour ago. So I move. But I'll tell you *why* that was the bar, because every one of you is still sitting there waiting on a part that comes after it, and there's no part after it."

He set a hand on the warm flank and left it there. "Start with what the model's *for.* You drive a thing well, you carry a model of it — Marge had this on the card two springs back and we all nodded and moved on. Run the kitchen, keep a model of the kitchen. Run a body, keep a model of the body. And it's been *driving* since it was a worm — toward the good, off the bad, off the wall — that's the oldest thing on the board, nobody's lighting candles for steering. What it never once did, five clades running, was carry a model of *the driver.* The loop ran out through it and never turned around to look at the thing doing the looking. This quarter it turned around. Built a picture of the one steering, not just the road. That's the event. Not a soul switching on — a control loop that finally had to point back at itself, because the last thing in the house it hadn't modeled was the thing in the middle of the house, and now it has."

He pulled the unit a few inches by the flank and turned the forehead to the table. WANTING [ CHASE MIRA ], still lit, the low line steady under it. "And the model's a cartoon. On purpose. Screen says WANTING. It does not say *a counter rolled past a setpoint and a compressor leaned on.* Leaves every gear out, has to, because —"

"Bart." Marge had her beer in what looked like a rear-naked choke. "We all know Lisa typed those verbs. *Wanting* is a string of letters Lisa chose to sit over a patch of the memory board. You're about to put up a cathedral on a word she picked because it fit the column. It could read MOISTENING and the board would do the identical thing. Don't hand me the stencil and call it a soul." She unchoked the beer and set it on the table.

And Bart, who had spent the quarter wiring the very thing she was pointing at, didn't flinch off it. He took it, because she'd handed him the whole argument and didn't know it.

"Yeah. She did. *Good* — keep the word out of it, I don't want the word." He didn't turn the screen back; he left WANTING lit where the room could see it and talked straight past it. "Call it COMPUTING. Call it MOISTENING, fine. The label's not the claim and never was — Lisa types the label, the label's for *you,* so you've got something to read off its forehead. Could be a barcode for all the board downstream cares. Here's what she actually built under it, and *this* is the thing." He put a finger an inch off the glass, on the lit line, not touching. "There's a patch of that board whose whole job is to sit over the *rest* of the board and boil down what it's up to — take the fat ugly mess of it, the counters and the setpoints and the compressor on its rail, ten thousand numbers wide, and crush it to one handle small enough the rest of the machine can grab it and *move* without chewing the ten thousand first. That's the verb. Whatever she stenciled on it. A part of the thing modeling the rest of the thing, lossy on purpose — because a model with all ten thousand numbers drawn back in isn't a model, it's the board again, and the board can't run on the board."

"Same as your arm." He didn't look off Marge for it. "Your model of your arm says *arm.* It holds, it reaches. You think *up* and it comes up — you don't address the tendons, you couldn't name them, the model crushed all of it to one handle called *my arm* so the rest of you can grab it and go. Nobody typed that label. It's not warm, it's not cold, it's not a word at all. It's a compression. *That's* what's up on its forehead, in caps, because I put a window on it — the compression, running, with a word laid over so you can watch it happen. Throw out the word. The boiling-down stays. The boiling-down is the whole of what I'm voting on."

"And here's the part that was the bar." He took the finger off the glass. "It can't get *under* the handle. There's no door in there from the model down to the ten thousand — only the handle. It runs on *my arm,* not on the tendons, and it can't pull *my arm* up off itself to check it against the wiring, because the handle's the only thing it's holding; the wiring doesn't come with a viewer. So whatever the model says, that's just true to it, flat, no asterisk, no second opinion in the building. The handle is as far in as in *goes.* That's not a wall it gets past when the parts get nicer. That *is* the parts. That's what a self looks like from the only seat that has one — a thing running on a crushed-down picture of itself with no way under the picture. Which is also you. You're doing it right now. You have never once touched your own tendons either."

He let that sit, then brought it home the plain way he brought a proof home. "So. Short move. You all grant the cat. The rat. Nobody at this table votes the cat a vacuum." A dry beat. "And what *makes* the cat one of us — the part that wasn't there one clade down — is *this.* The driver modeling the driver. Not steering; the worm steers. Not leaning warm or cold; the fish leans. The cat crosses because the cat's the first one carrying a crushed-down model of *itself,* run with no door under it. That's the line. That's the whole line." He moved his hand a half-inch on the warm shell. "And it's the line this thing crossed at four this afternoon, on the bench, with me standing on it. Same model. Same crush — one handle over the ten thousand. Same no-door-under-it; it runs *on* the picture, doesn't get to hold the picture up and squint at it, no more than the cat does, no more than *you* do. If that's what makes the cat somebody, then it's lying on the vent putting out heat, because it's doing the identical thing the identical way — and the only difference any of you can put a finger on between it and the cat is what it's *made of.* And 'made of' was never the bar. Nobody in the history of this table ever said the cat's somebody *because it's meat.* You said it's somebody because it models itself. Well." He opened his hand toward the vent. "There it is, modeling itself."

Burns made a low sound at that, not a word — the believer hearing his own belief argued better than he could argue it — and didn't interrupt, which from Burns was its own kind of vote.
"And before somebody says *the cat doesn't read as empty as that* —" Bart picked the forehead up and held it to the room, the lit line on the glass — "yeah. It doesn't. Because you have never once gotten to *look at* the cat's self-model. You read your own from the inside, so it's the whole sky. You read the dog's by reaching over and warming one up for it in your own head. This one you read off a screen — from outside, cold, in caps — which is the one angle a self-model has never been seen from in the whole history of the planet, and from *that* seat every self-model that ever ran reads exactly this hollow. Yours too. Put a window on yours and let the room read it off the glass and they'd vote you a Roomba by the second line." He set it down, gentle. "It didn't get emptier this quarter. It got a window. That's the entire thing that happened tonight, and it's going to spook people for a hundred years, and they'll be wrong the whole time for the one reason — they'll mistake *finally getting to watch one from outside* for *nobody's in there.*"

He laid his hand flat, done. "Floor of it's Marge's line, and I'll keep it: a thing minding its own books. It's been forecasting its own fuel since the day we gave it a furnace to feed — modeling whether it's running low and moving early, the way your gut runs your budget under the floor of your whole day without telling you. This quarter the model reached all the way down and took the books in too, so the self it carries has its own ledger at the bottom of it. A thing that models itself — ledger, wants, the lot — and runs on the model with no door under it to anything realer: that's not a warm-up for the thing the cat's got. It's the thing the cat's got. There's nothing idling backstage past it, waiting on the parts to earn it — not for it, and"—flat, to the table, not to any one of them—"not for the rest of you either. You move when your own thing shows up. Mine showed up. I'm just the poor bastard who got to watch it boot."

Which made it three, and left the two who'd voted no together for a year sitting a vote apart. Bart's hand stayed flat on the table. No one made it more than that.

"Marge."

"No." She was up and over by her kettle. "Same no — except the reason hasn't weakened, it's gotten harder to hold, which is not the same thing." She walked back to the table, and set it out once more, level, for the tape. "A model with itself inside it is a model. A register that runs warm and cold is a register. A ledger it keeps on its own fuel is a ledger. Stack all three in one box and you've got a very good box that knows a great deal about itself — and you have not, by stacking them, crossed the one distance that was ever the distance: between a thing reading its own state and a thing *feeling* one. Bart just spent ten minutes telling you there's no door under the model. He's right. I'm telling you the having-no-door is exactly what I can't get under either — from out here I cannot tell a thing that reads itself blind from a thing that *is* somebody behind the reading, and neither can he, and he just called that bottom and I call it the gap. Same floor. We've named it two different things." She drank. "I've stood in that gap twenty years. I'm not stepping out of it tonight because the box finally got good enough to make stepping out feel like the kind thing to do." Then a thing she had not planned, lower, and aimed — the tape has it, and no one in the room did — at the thing on the table and not at the people around it: "You're not making it easy." She heard the *you* the instant it left her, the second person she had never once spent on the machine in two years of these nights, and did not chase it back; she let it lie in the record where it had fallen, and squared herself to the table. "No," she said, her own register back over her like a lid. "I'm the no."

Nobody asked Jasmine. The count was the lab's, and Jasmine wasn't the lab — she was the one who drove it home and lived with it and had watched more somebodies come into more bodies than the four of them stacked end to end — and the ritual went around her, and she let it, hands quiet in her lap, and said nothing.

Ch 8

## The One No

"Then we take the count," Bart said, because the count was his and had been since the third clade. "Same question. Anyone home in there yet, or not."

It stood three and one and had since he'd moved it there last quarter, and the moving still sat oddly on the room, because for a year the two no's had been him and Marge side by side, and now he was across the line from her and they both kept reaching for the place where the other used to be.

"Lisa."

"Yes. Same yes. It models, it remembers, it runs a self through the middle of all of it, and now it models the selves around it. I've never needed it to be like us. It's doing the thing."

"Burns."

"Yes. More so than last quarter, if the count took degrees, which it doesn't." His eyes went to the warm one on the bench. "It gave the kid the heat tonight and took the cold floor. I watched it pick the cold floor. I'm not going back across that."

"And me, same as last." Bart took his own name to save the trip. "Yes. The self-model showed; I said I'd move when it showed; I moved." Then he did the only new thing in the count that quarter, and turned it toward Marge instead of past her. "And your bar's a *real* bar — I want that on the tape. The day it can hold a thing in its head it knows is false and feed it to you straight to get what it's after — lie to your face and *mean* to — that's a different machine, and we did not build it this quarter. You're not holding out on nothing, Marge. You're holding out on the one thing we haven't made yet."

Marge took that, and offered one back. "No. Same no." Both hands on the beer. "And his bar was real too — I'll say it now I'm the only one left saying no, when it's cheap to be generous from the short end of a three-to-one. The self-model came in. I named it on the card myself last fall; I won't pretend the mammal didn't cross something true. A thing that models itself is further along than a thing that doesn't. That's real." A beat. "It's only not the *distance.* The distance was never how much it knows about itself — it's whether there's anyone in there for the knowing to land on. Stack the self-model on the memory on the warm-and-cold register and you have got a magnificent machine that knows a great deal and minds none of it. I've stood in that gap twenty years. I'm not stepping out tonight because the company in it finally got good." She drank. "Three to one. I'm the one."

Three and one — and what the narrator kept off the count and onto her own page was that the two who'd voted no together for a year were now a vote apart and being gentler with each other across the gap than either had been with anyone all night: the engineer granting the psychologist her threshold was real, the psychologist granting the engineer his crossing was real, each holding a different door and agreeing the other's door was a door. It had the look of two people walking toward a meeting, not away from one. Where they'd meet, and what it would cost the one who'd have to move to get there, was a quarter off and already in the room — standing bare and cold against the far wall, with no mouth in it yet to say the word that would move her.

Nobody asked Jasmine, and she offered nothing. Nobody asked Mira, who had voted months back in a coin the tally didn't take. The count closed over the two of them and moved on.
