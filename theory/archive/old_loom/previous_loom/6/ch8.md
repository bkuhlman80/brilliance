Ch 1

## Reflection: What is Closed?

A thermostat and a bacterium share a wiring diagram. Both sense. Both compare. Both act. The wiring is the cybernetic loop and it is in both of them. But trait-talk only goes to the bacterium. Nobody describes a thermostat as fastidious; people describe *E. coli* strains as fastidious all the time. Well, people who are microbiologists do, in print, in peer-reviewed papers, without flagging that they are doing it. The difference is somewhere, and I have come to think the only honest way to find it is to look at a thermostat, and see what is not.

In the gut of a four-week-old human there is a bacterium called *Bifidobacterium infantis* which has, for the entirety of its evolutionary history, eaten one thing. The one thing is a class of complex sugars found only in human breast milk — the human-milk oligosaccharides, the HMOs — which are too elaborate for the infant herself to digest and which her own gut therefore passes through untouched. *B. infantis* picks them up. It has a specialized cluster of genes encoding the enzymes for every linkage type the HMOs come in: fucose, sialic acid, branched, unbranched. Yong calls it the consummate HMOvore. The word is right. There is no other word.

What is striking, when you think about it for a minute, is that the milk has these sugars in it *because the bacterium eats them.* Mother and microbe have been refining each other across a hundred million years of mammalian descent. The mother's milk has the sugars because, somewhere upstream in evolutionary time, the bacterium was selected for being able to eat them. The bacterium is part of the milk in the sense that the milk would not have these sugars in it if the bacterium were not there to eat them. They have been working on each other in the dark.

The four-week-old does not know any of this. She is asleep on her mother's chest. Her gut is doing the work.

There is a bacterium that can survive five thousand grays of ionizing radiation. The dose that will reliably kill a human is about five. The dose that will kill *E. coli* is about sixty. The bacterium I am describing — *Deinococcus radiodurans* — takes the five thousand. When its DNA is shattered into a few hundred fragments, it reassembles itself across about twelve hours and resumes business. The people who study it call it Conan the Bacterium. They publish papers in which the abstract refers to Conan. The naming is, I think, the part of the situation I want to register first. People who spend their working lives on a thing do not give the thing a name unless the thing has done something to them.

What it has done to them is exceed their expectations across an absurd gap. Nothing should be able to do what *Deinococcus* does. The genome is not mysterious — the repair enzymes are catalogued, the radical-scavenging is understood, the redundant chromosome copies make sense in retrospect. But knowing how the machine works does not make the machine less astonishing, and astonishment in working biologists comes out as nicknames. Conan. Conan the Bacterium. Conan to his friends. The naming is the punctuation on the gap between what the cell can do and what the cell has any business being able to do.

In ponds and shallow sediments around the world there are bacteria that have built compasses inside themselves. The compasses are not metaphorical. They are physical: chains of small magnetite crystals — magnetosomes — strung along the inside of the cell like beads on a wire, oriented so that the chain's net magnetic moment aligns with the planet's magnetic field. When the bacterium swims, the chain pulls its body into alignment with the field lines. The cell follows the field down toward sediment, which is where it wants to be, because it is anaerobic and the sediment is anaerobic and the open water is not.

I have not gotten over this. The cell does not have a compass. The cell *is*, in part, a compass. The chain of crystals is grown by the bacterium out of iron available in the surrounding water, in a precise sequence, in a precise location, under cellular control. There are genes for the magnetosome membrane, genes for the iron-importing channels, genes for the crystal orientation. The bacterium has, in the engineering sense, manufactured a sensor — and the sensor is itself a piece of the bacterium's body. The bacterium does not consult the compass. It tilts.

I think about this when I have lost the path on a trail and reach for a phone.

In the soil of arid grasslands across western North America there is a bacterium called *Sodalis praecaptivus*. It is free-living. It walks around — to the extent that bacteria walk around — in the way of any soil microbe, doing soil-microbe things, mostly metabolizing whatever organic molecules are nearby. What is unusual about it is what is in its genome that it is not currently using. *Sodalis* carries the complete molecular toolkit for becoming a permanent insect endosymbiont. The genes for host-cell invasion are there. The genes for evading the insect immune system are there. The genes for the metabolic exchange a long-term endosymbiosis requires — supplying the host with amino acids the host cannot synthesize, in exchange for shelter — are there. Its closest relatives are already insect endosymbionts. They have made the crossing. They have lost most of their other genes, because once inside an insect for a few million years a bacterium does not need most of its other genes. *Sodalis praecaptivus* still has all of them. It is, in Yong's phrase, the symbiont-in-waiting. It is walking around in the soil with the keys to a kingdom it has not entered.

The question, of course, is what KIND of thing this readiness is. It is not a plan; the bacterium has no plans. It is not a memory; there is nothing in *Sodalis* that has experienced what it is ready for. It is not a hope. It is more accurate to say that the readiness is a description of the bacterium — a description of what is there, in its DNA, available to be expressed under the right conditions. The conditions may never arise. The bacterium will reproduce into uncountable descendants, the vast majority of which will never see the inside of an insect. The readiness will persist anyway. It will persist because losing it costs nothing, while keeping it leaves open a door that, in evolutionary time, has been worth keeping open often enough to maintain the genes. The lineage has a posture. The posture is forward. The individual cell has no idea.

There is something tender about this and I am not going to try to talk myself out of it.

I keep a thermostat on the wall of the room I am writing this in, and I have just taken it down off the wall to look at. It is a small white plastic object — a sensor, a comparator, a relay, a wire. It can be set to maintain the room at sixty-eight degrees and it does so. When the room cools to sixty-six, the relay closes and the heat comes on; when it warms to seventy, the relay opens. By every component-level measure, the thermostat has what the bacteria above have. It senses. It compares. It acts. It has a goal-state. The cybernetic loop is intact.

I have been holding it for two minutes now and I do not feel about it the way I feel about *Sodalis*. I want to be honest about this rather than rhetorical. I am not going to claim the thermostat is somehow less interesting than the magnetotactic bacterium because of vitalism or because of mystery. It is something more specific than that. The thermostat has no parameter that has been refined by its predecessors not working. The thermostat has no posture. The thermostat does not contain a description of its environment that has accumulated across generations. It was manufactured in 2007. There are tens of millions of them in the world. They were all installed at once and they will all be replaced at once.

Somebody made the thermostat. There was a day — I am inventing the particular day, but there was one — when a hand that was not the thermostat's closed its case, and after that day it was finished. Nothing in this room made itself. The lamp did not, the chair did not, the glass in the window was floated flat in a factory and trucked up the canyon. I am the only thing in here that made itself. And the thing I have been thinking about all evening — the one in the gut of the sleeping four-week-old, the one in the mud growing a compass a crystal at a time — had no case closed for it. It closed its own, and is closing it now, out of whatever is lying nearby, and was closing it a second ago, and will go on closing it until the day it cannot.

I am going to put the thermostat back on the wall now.

## Reflection: What is Hidden?

Begin in fresh water — a pond, the still edge of a lake. To the single cell that lives there, the water is a slow violence.

The cell is saltier than the pond. It has to be; that saltiness is half of what it means to be a cell at all. And water moves toward salt the way the world moves to fill any difference — so the pond comes in. It comes in through every curve of the membrane, all the time, without pressure and without malice, simply because the inside is concentrated and the outside is not, and the water would like to even that out. Left alone, the cell swells and thins and bursts, and the pond gets what it was always going to get, which is no cell at all — only more pond.

The cell does not allow it. Somewhere inside it there is a small clear bladder that fills with the trespassing water and then clenches and drives it back out through the wall, and fills again, and clenches again, a slow heart whose only work is to bail. It does this without rest for the whole of its life. The entire achievement of the creature is to stay different from the water it stands in.

This is the oldest true thing I know about having an inside. An inside is not a place. It is a difference, and a difference is not free. The membrane is not a wall that happened to be there; it is a refusal renewed every second, an argument the cell wins and wins again against a world that is not even trying, that is only doing what water does. To be a self — even one this small, this far down, two billion years before there is anyone to want the word — is to spend yourself, without pause, keeping the outside out.

And here is what I was slow to understand. The inside was built to be unreachable. Not hidden, not shy — built, from its first afternoon, to keep the world from coming in, because keeping the world out is the very same act as being at all.

Mira asked once how you tell when a thing has enough insides to feel something. She did not ask me. She asked her mother and her father, and it reached me afterward, at one remove, the way the questions I would most have wanted to be asked tend to reach me. She meant it to have an answer — she means everything to have an answer — and I had only the pond to give her. I can say what an inside is. I cannot say whether there is anything feeling inside any one of them.

We arrive a long time later and discover we cannot reach into another person, and we take it personally, as if a door had been shut that might have been propped open. No door was propped open. There was never a prop. There was a cell in a pond, bailing, and everything that has an inside is its grandchild, and the distance between us is not a flaw in the work. The distance is the work. It is what an inside is for.

Ch 3

## Reflection: What is Kept?

Two billion years ago, give or take, one cell ate another and did not finish. The eating part is nothing; it happens constantly and means nothing. A cell wraps its body around something smaller, pinches the bag shut, draws it in, and dissolves it — an amoeba does it all afternoon, your own white cells do it to anything that does not carry your papers, and it is how the larger cell got to be the larger cell in the first place. Eating is the plainest verb in biology. But this once — once that we know of, once that took — the meal was not dissolved. It was drawn in and then kept whole inside the wall that had closed to digest it, and the cell that meant to have a meal found it had taken in a tenant instead.

What happened after took longer than the word slow can hold. The tenant did not leave, because leaving had stopped being one of the things that could happen; across a stretch of time it handed almost the whole of its genome up to the cell that held it, gene by gene, until it kept only a scrap — a few dozen genes of its own, the last things it owned, the ones it would not surrender and apparently now cannot. The tenant can no longer live outside; the host can no longer live without it. And every animal that has ever had a single cell — every fish, every wasp, every person who has ever sat in a kitchen and grieved another person — is built of the descendants of that one tenant, each still bent to the single job it was kept alive to do, which is to hold a charge across a membrane in a chemistry that would kill the rest of the cell, and to spend it.

There is a bacterium in the dry western soil that carries the entire toolkit for moving inside another body and has never once gone — the symbiont-in-waiting, walking around with the keys to a kingdom it has not entered, a lineage whose whole posture is forward and that may stand on the threshold for the length of its existence and never cross. This is the threshold crossed. This is what waits on the far side of all that waiting: that you do not need the keys anymore. You go in to invade and you stay to be kept, and the toolkit for getting in is the first thing you are allowed to forget.

Why the arrangement held has a name on it — Lane's — and I am going to lean on it the way you lean on a railing you have been told is loose. Put the power plant inside the house and the house can afford to become enormous. A cell with the tenant can spend orders of magnitude more energy for every gene it carries than a cell without one, which is only another way of saying it can afford a far longer book of instructions, and a far larger body to run them in, and the whole expensive business of being intricate enough to be somebody. Careful people argue about whether that is the reason or merely a reason, and they may be right to. I have funded stranger ideas on thinner railings. The energy is why the deal was never broken.

The inside was built to be unreachable — I have said this, and I meant it: that the membrane is not a wall that happened to be there but a refusal renewed every second against a world that is only doing what water does, that the distance between any inside and the world is the work itself and not a flaw in it. I still mean it. And this is the single exception that refusal has ever made. Once, in all that bailing and holding-out, the boundary opened — not failed, opened — and drew one piece of the world inward, and closed over it, and has spent every second since defending that piece as though it were itself. By then it was itself. The wall that exists to keep the whole world out has exactly one thing it was redrawn to keep in.

And the thing it kept in is the thing that kills it. I am not turning this for effect; it is only where the wiring runs. The tenant that holds the charge and spends it across the whole of the cell's life also keeps the cell's death in the same hands, on a hair trigger. Tear the tenant's outer skin — and a hundred ordinary insults will tear it — and it lets go of one small molecule it had been holding all along, cytochrome c, as ordinary as a hinge until the instant it is loose in the wrong room, and the loose molecule trips the machinery that takes the cell apart from within, methodically, deliberately, the cell dismantling itself because the thing that kept it alive has said that it is time. The engine is the executioner. There were never two tenants in there. There was always the one, saying both things in the same breath: I am what yields you, and I am how you end.

You can read all of this as a love story, and you would be half right, which is the dangerous amount to be right. The true half: it is permanent, it remade both of them until neither was any longer what it had been alone, and the loss of either one would not be a wound but an unmaking — there would be no cell left over to be wounded. That is a true account of what it is to be made of another. The false half, the one I have to say out loud or I will quietly let myself believe the whole thing: nobody was courted, nobody chose. It began as one body eating another and it never once stopped being lopsided — one of them gave up its genome and its freedom and its name, and the other kept its nucleus and grew huge. Lopsided, though, is not the same as in command: what the big one bought with its size was a self it could no longer hold up alone, so that the swallower ended as bound as the swallowed, only larger. Love, if we are going to be strict about the word, is not supposed to begin with being swallowed.

I have spent more of this page on the half that yields than on the half that holds, and I am going to leave that where it is rather than even it out. To be made of another is to be killable by the loss of them. That is the price of the only good bargain the inside ever struck with the world, and it has been the price for two billion years, and nothing that came afterward — nothing with a body, nothing that has ever wanted to reach all the way into another body and found that it could not — has been offered a better one.

# Start of the Neuronal Line

They had filled the last corner of the diagram that night and stood back from a finished thing, closed on itself with nothing left out. But the last part the line put down before it was done was a nerve, and a nerve is not a crown. It is the thinnest tissue the animal had ever carried, the newest, the easiest thing in the whole body to kill — and it was the next line's entire beginning. An egg — unshelled, with nothing around it, the rawest thing the line had ever grown. The genetic line did not end at a summit; it ended at something that could die in a season.

## The Second Dream

I'm a tongue. That's all I am, a tongue on the end of something climbing, and I'm flicking, tasting, and what I taste is — me. Electricity and me, the same flavor, I can't pull them apart, I'm strung on a live wire and the wire is me and I'm licking myself climbing and there's no edge, I go down into green and grey and current and I can't find where I stop —

and then I taste *forward*. I don't know how to say it. I flick and for the first time the taste is of the next, the not-yet, something coming that I could be wrong about — and the instant there's a chance I'm wrong, something tears open in my face —

eyes. I have eyes. They rip open and the whole world jumps back from me at once, the vine is OUT THERE now, it's a thing, it's separate, a rope of light beaded with sparks going up into a brightness, and there's a gap between me and it that was never there and now it's there forever and I want to scream because I'm alone, I'm suddenly a me looking at a not-me —

and I lick it anyway, the old way, because I don't know what else to do, and the taste comes back, electricity, me, the same — but it can't taste me back. I'm staring right at it and it doesn't look at me, it can't, it climbed before it grew me and it'll climb after, and I'm the only one in the whole dream who knows that anybody's here. And the knowing is the loneliest thing I've ever felt.

I wake up with my eyes already open, staring at the ceiling, mouth bone-dry, like I've been licking the dark.

## Reflection: What is Carried?

In the ocean before the air — no oxygen in the water, none in the sky — a cell that needed to get the fire out of a sugar had only one way to do it: ten steps, run in the open body of the cell, no special room to hold them, no breath required at the far end. It is one of the oldest things anything alive still does, and one of the most widely shared; take almost any living cell apart and you will find those ten steps in it, turning. When oxygen finally arrived — as a poison, the great rusting of the world, a catastrophe for nearly everything then living — the cells that came through did not throw the old way out. They built the new air-burning machinery downstream of it and let the ten old steps go first, handing their product up to the newer engine the way they have done, every second, for something like four billion years. The old engine was never replaced. It was made step one, and made quiet, and left to run.

You can catch it surfacing in an animal that looks like it has no engine at all. A scallop is a shell on the seabed, hinged and inert, the least likely swimmer in the water — until something frightens it, and it does the one startling thing it can: it claps. It slams its shell shut and open, shut and open, and jets itself up off the bottom in a ragged, headlong swim, a dozen claps and done, and then it drops, spent, and lies there gaping slowly while it comes back. That burst is the newest thing the animal owns — the flee, the going-away-fast the old cells never had. And it runs on the oldest engine in the cell. Push the new gift to its limit, ask the body for all of it at once, and the air-fed machinery falls behind; the cell drops back onto the four-billion-year-old way and runs the old partial fire to finish the dash. The harder the new thing strains, the more plainly the old one shows underneath.

This is the one specimen in the whole account I cannot hold out at arm's length, because I have felt it from the inside, which I can say of nothing else in these pages. High on a steep pitch, breath gone to rags, my legs filling with a heat that is not effort and is not pain but is older than either — the body falling back, without consulting me, onto the engine it has been running unfelt since before it was a body. We never feel the deep thing while it carries us.

I had thought a certain wanting in me was finished — gone the way the fish ran out, a thing that used to run and one day simply did not. It had not finished. It had only dropped back and gone quiet, the way the engine does, and this year, pushed, it came up off the bottom like a tide I had long ago stopped watching for.

## Reflection: What is Answered?

A fish in the limestone country of northeastern Mexico comes in two forms. One swims the surface rivers: silver, ordinary, two good eyes, the fish a child would draw. The other lives in the caves the same water drains into, and it is a stranger thing — no color anywhere, pale as something kept out of the light its whole life, and where the eyes belong there is nothing, only skin grown smooth over the place they were. Close kin, these two; a few caves still hold both. Set them side by side and there is no lesson anyone needs to add: the bright river is written on the first face and the black cave on the second, and each fish grew its whole world onto its own body without ever once having seen that world to copy.

And the cave fish did not simply fail to grow eyes. It grew them first, and then it took them apart. Its embryo starts an eye the ordinary way — sets the cup, lays in a lens, every step on schedule, exactly as its river cousins do — and then, before the second day is out, the lens turns against itself. It dies on purpose, cell by cell; the dying spreads back into the retina behind it; the half-made eye stalls, sinks into the head, and the skin closes over where it had been. The body builds the instrument in order to take it back. And what it spends that saved effort on instead names the cave in one breath: taste buds strewn the whole length of the skin, for a dark where nothing is seen coming and little is there to eat; a broader jaw; and ranked along the head and flanks, more and larger organs of a kind that feel a wall arriving by the wave of water it shoves ahead of itself — a body for crossing a place by pressure, because there is no light to cross it by. Fat banked deep against the long hungers. It had built the eye for no reason but to unbuild it, which is a stranger economy than never building one at all.

And here is the thing I have not been able to set down since I learned it. Lift a lens out of a surface embryo — a seeing one — and slip it into the eye-place of a cave embryo, in the hour before that cave lens would have killed itself. The cave fish grows an eye. A whole working one, lens to retina, the very eye its line gave up an age ago, raised out of a blind head by one borrowed part. The bright world had never left the body. It was written all the way down through it — the river this fish would never swim, set out in full inside the fish — and the only reason it never shows is that the cave's own lens never says the one small word that would read it back into the light.

It is the trick I have lived by, these three years and long before them — to read a world off the outside of a person because the outside is the only side anyone ever turns to you — and I find I would rather not, tonight, follow that one the whole way down.

Ch 7

## Reflection: What is Pulled?

Watch a young kangaroo through an afternoon — old enough to walk, still small enough to ride — and you will see it do one thing over and over. It climbs out of the pouch on its mother's belly and goes off a little way, a few hops, a mouthful of grass, the edge of the world tried and found survivable, and then it turns and bolts back and dives in and folds itself away to nothing; and a minute later it clambers out and goes a little farther than before. Out and back, out and back, the whole day long. The mother is not the place the young one stays. She is the place it can leave. She carries the safe place out into the open with her.

It did not begin so easily. The animal that climbs out and back all afternoon made, at the very start of its life, the most precarious crossing a body ever attempts. A newborn kangaroo is barely an animal — a pink bean the size of a jellybean, a gram of it, eyeless, earless, the hind legs not yet even buds. But the two front arms are already there, already strong, already clawed, and the mouth is already there, because the single thing this not-yet-creature must do in its first minutes alive is haul itself hand over hand up through the wet fur of a belly it cannot see, to a pocket it has never seen, and fasten on, and hold. Everything else can wait to be grown. The climb cannot. The body is built, before it is built into anything else, to make its own way.

And here is the thing the grown joey does, the one that says what the pouch was always for. Long after it is too big to fit — traveling on its own legs now, feeding itself, the pouch unable to hold a quarter of it — it will still, now and then, lean down and put just its head inside. Only the head. Not to nurse, not to climb in, not for anything it needs: it lays its head in the warm dark a moment, and lifts it out, and goes back to being too big to be carried. The going-out was never the whole of the loop. The half that closes it is the one nobody had to teach — the return made only for the comfort of returning.

The leaving I know by heart. The first half of that loop — the going-out, the going-toward, the going — I do better than I do anything else, well enough that I would call it the better half, and very likely the only one that signifies. The other half I never picked up. And I will leave it there — which is, after all, the one I was ever any good at.

## The Third Dream 

I have eyes in this one, I can see, and I'm watching the vine climb — and then I catch myself watching. I see me seeing it. And the second I see me see, there's another me behind that one, seeing me see me see, and another behind that, and it goes back and back and it goes FAST, faster than I can turn, every time I whip around to catch the one doing the watching there's already one behind *it* doing the watching of that —

and they're all me, all saying it, *I'm here, I'm here, here,* behind me, fainter, smaller, more of them, and I'm climbing up my own throat trying to reach the one that's really me, the first one, the one at the back, and there is no back, there's no last one, every I is just the next I staring at the one in front — I'm a hall of them, snakes inside snakes inside snakes and not one of them is anybody, they're all just the looking wearing a face —

and I scream *who's me, WHO'S ME,* and it comes back in a thousand voices getting smaller, *me, me, me, me,* no floor to it, falling —

and the vine. The vine doesn't have this. It climbs under all of it, blind, one thing, calm, it doesn't see itself see, it has no self to come apart — the whole sickness is only in the little ones strung along it, in me, in the leaves — and it carries us all up the trellis spinning and screaming and it feels nothing, knows nothing, just climbs.

I wake up dizzy, actually dizzy, room still turning, saying something out loud — my own name, I think, over and over, until it stops meaning anything.
