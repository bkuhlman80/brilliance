## May we Persist

Burns took the close. Lisa had it on the agenda as *motivational speech*, and he had been trying to get her to call it anything else for as long as there had been an agenda, which was three months.

"Last thing's mine," he said, and stood. "Lisa has it down as the motivational speech. I keep asking her to call it something else."

"I'm the agenda," Lisa said.

"She's the agenda." He let it go and stood at the head of the table with his hands already moving, flat to the wood and then up into the air. "Marge wants me careful with the metaphysics. I'm going to try."

"Please try," Marge said.

"What the protocell did, four billion years ago, was persist", and he recalled the mechanics of it — the gradient found and held, the skin of oil keeping its few accidents of chemistry from coming apart — no model of itself, no regulator, none of the machinery they would spend three years engineering into the bot on the table. He picked the bot up and turned it in his hands, and held it where they could all see it twitch.

"It has one motivation," he said. "Keep operating. That's the whole of it."

Then the turn — the first time he made it, though he would stand up and make it eleven more times before they were done — from the clade to the room. He set the bot back down. This was the lab, he said, three months in: no word yet from the funder, running on faith and their own readings and twelve cards in a portfolio and the wind-up cranks of five small things in a kitchen. The job in front of them was the protocell's job. Hard quarters were coming and he didn't know which ones. He leaned in, the half-inch too far that meant he'd reached the part he believed.

"The thing that gets across the threshold," he said, "is the thing that didn't stop. We're going to be the thing that doesn't stop."

He was toasting persistence because persistence was the thing he most needed to believe he could do — a man three months into the bid of his life, betting a warehouse and four colleagues and a child's birthdays that he could, this once, start a thing that lasted. He held up his beer.

"To the bot. To the four of us. To Mira, and to Jasmine. And to Cheryl, who'll see this on tape in a few weeks." He lifted it higher. "May we persist."

"May we persist," Bart said. 

"May we persist," Marge said it, and Lisa, and Jasmine, and then Mira, who had been quiet a long time with the bot in her fist, said, "Persist."

It was the first time she had used the word, and she had used it right. Burns laughed the chest-laugh — because she had used it right; because it was eight in the evening and the meeting had been a good one; because the protocell had worked out how to keep going four billion years ago and the lab, three months in, had just worked out how to start.

Ch 3

## May we Hold

"Last thing's mine," Burns said, and stood, "and I'm calling it 'Why we do what we do.'"

"It's the motivational speech," Lisa said. "It's been the motivational speech for three quarters."

"I've been trying to rename it since July."

"I know. The answer's still no."

He let it go — an old defeat he'd made his peace with — and stood with his weight settled and his hands, for once, open at his sides instead of flat on the wood. The motivation this quarter was Interiority, he said, and he wanted to argue it was the one with the highest stakes they'd hit, higher than persistence, higher than coordination, because it was the first one about having an *inside* — and he compressed the philosophy into the line he'd carried in ready, the weight already on it before he said it.

"Nothing matters to a thing with no inside," he said. "There's no in-here for it to matter in."

Then the story, the one he kept coming back to. Two billion years ago a cell ate another cell and didn't finish — they had a word for it, phagocytosis, predation gone strange — and he wanted to call it the other thing it was, which was that one living thing took another inside itself and kept it alive. Gave it a room. Fed it. And the arrangement held; that was the part he couldn't get over, and he slowed on it, eyes leaving the room and coming back. It had held two billion years without a single break in the line, and every cell in everyone at the table was keeping that ancient guest alive right now, had never once stopped.

"You're a place where something else is being kept alive," he said. "The inside of you is a room you keep a guest in. And the guest is the reason you have the power to be anything at all."

So the moral was a call to action, he said. An inside was a thing you kept — Marge's weather, Lisa's board, all of them — and you didn't keep one for free. It was continuous work. It was bailing against a world that kept coming in. And it was the work most worth doing, because it was the work that made there be a you for the work to be worth doing to.

Then he got to the place his speeches got to, the half-step past where the argument needed him, and his voice did the thing it did. The best of it, he said — the part he was most grateful for — was that an inside was also a thing you handed forward: you built one and you kept one and then there was a next one coming up that got to build its own — and his eyes went, without his quite deciding it, to Mira, bent over her paper with the pen. "You get to be the room for a little while," he said, "before they build their own."

Bart's laugh came in a beat early, on *room*, on the word that had settled on the child and not on the son. "Okay — land the plane," he said. "The plane's landed." The table took it as what it looked like, a kid puncturing his dad's sentiment, and it broke the warmth a half-second before the warmth could set into anything. Burns took it like a joke, because it was one, and grinned, deflated on purpose, glad of the contact even when the contact came shaped like a needle.

"Fine," he said. "Fine. Then let's close it. The verb." And here he stalled, because the verb was the problem and he knew it — you could persist and you could coordinate but you could not *interior,* there was no door in the word to walk a cascade through — and he stood hunting it, the man who'd been trying to rename the slot for three quarters caught out by a word that wouldn't conjugate. "To have an inside. To keep one. To —" and you could see him find it, "— to *hold.* An inside is a thing you hold." He raised his beer. "May we hold."

"May we hold, Smithers," Bart said, in the Mr. Burns voice, pitch-perfect, and then, in his own, "May we hold."

"May we hold," Marge said, and Lisa, and Jasmine. And Mira, last, looked up from her paper, weighed the word a moment the way she weighed a color before she'd commit it to the page, and said: "May we hold."

Ch 4

## May we Quicken

Burns took the close. It was still *the motivational speech* on Lisa's agenda — he'd lost that in July and lost it again every quarter since — and he set his beer down on the table and came around to the open side.

He wanted to tell them about a jellyfish, he said, and the wanting-to was the run-up she'd heard the shape of before — the warming, the hands lifting off the wood and into the air — so that what the tape kept was the place he landed. There's a jellyfish in the shallows, he said, with no brain at all, not a knot of one, nothing you could point at and call the center of it. And at night it sleeps. It slows, it stops answering, it settles to the bottom; keep it from sleeping and it spends the next day catching up, the way you would. It startles. It comes alive to the world and goes quiet from it again on its own clock, and there is no one in there doing the deciding, because the deciding is spread through the tissue, through the whole net of it.

"There's nobody running it," he said, hands open at the part he couldn't hold. "And it still wakes up. It still answers."

Then the turn — he made it a beat after you could see it coming, the same turn he'd made at the close in spring and summer and fall and would make at every close still ahead: from the animal to the room. What he wanted, he said, what he was grateful for and a little afraid of, was to stay the kind of creature the world could still get an answer out of. To not go quiet from it.

"So that's the toast, and the toast's the problem, because you can't —" and he stalled, hunting a verb in a word that wouldn't keep one. "Excitability. You can't *excite* — wrong word, that's a thing you do to a crowd. You can't *be excitable,* that's a personals ad." Marge's low laugh; Bart's single exhale. "There's a verb in here somewhere. Hang on." And they let him hang on, because the hunting was the best part and they'd learned that too.

He found it a half-inch past where he needed to go, leaning that same inch too far over the table: "*Quicken.* To quicken. To come alive to a thing. That's the one." It was a half-step too grand and it was the right word, both at once; and only two people had any private claim on it — Burns, who didn't know he'd reached past *responsive* into something with more life in it than the framework strictly needed, and Jasmine, who used the word for a living, for the morning a thing that isn't moving starts to move, and who didn't say so, and wasn't asked.

He picked the beer back up. "May we quicken."

"May we quicken," Lisa said.

"May we quicken," Marge and Jasmine.

"May we quicken, Smithers," Bart said, in the Mr. Burns voice, the plutocrat laid over the father, pitch-perfect, and Burns laughed the chest-laugh, early and loud, because it was a good impression and it was eight o'clock and the board had closed clean.

And Mira, who'd been quiet a long while, who was five as of that morning and missing the front tooth that had come out the week before, said it last, around the gap — "May we quicken" — the *qu* coming soft through the space where the tooth had been, a sound only she could make just now, and she kept it.

He sat down. Lisa did not reach for the camcorder; the red light stayed on. There was a cake out of frame, and a shape at the end of the table under a drop-cloth that Mira had been pretending all evening not to see, and the night was not going to end on a diagram.

Ch 5

## May we Seize

Burns stood up with whatever was in his glass and made them wait for it.

They had spent the whole night, he said, on the machinery of going somewhere — the head, the steering, the four circles, the goo, as Bart had apparently decided they were all calling it now. And every piece of it was worth precisely nothing if the thing it was built into would not go. A steering animal that steers nowhere is the most finished, most useless object in the world. The whole apparatus only pays out in the instant the thing aims itself at something it has never seen and goes anyway. That was the motivation this quarter, and it was the one he'd been most impatient to reach, because it was the one he believed past the point of argument. Impetus. The Effector — the go-forward, the nerve to spend the architecture. The willingness to leave the only world it has and go.

And then he gave them the picture he gave them when he wanted them to feel the size of a thing. Somewhere in the warm dark of a Cambrian sea, he said, there is a larva the size of a comma — a few hundred cells, a smear of light-sensitive ones at the front, no one in it yet to give a reason for anything — and one day it turns itself toward the light and swims. "Up," he said, "into water nothing of its kind had ever been, off the bottom, off the only world it had, toward a brightness that could as easily have been a mouth. And it went. The first venture, half a billion years deep, and it starts with a speck deciding the light is worth the risk of the dark."

He did not say what the picture was to him, the narrator noted, and never had to. He was a man who could not sit inside a finished thing. He had spent a career walking out into the parts of the rock nobody had drawn, and he closed every one of these nights the same way, like a man who needed the room to promise, with him, to keep going out.

So the moral was the one that turned every wonder into a work order. "Which means we don't get to admire it," he said. "We build it. We give the thing more places it's never been and better reasons to go there. You don't honor a venture by framing it. You honor it by venturing."

He had his glass up by then, and his eyes went once — the way he'd deny if you asked — to the thing on the floor, the body his son had built, that walked and would not go into the dark, and then back to the room.

"To Impetus," he said. "To the architecture, and the nerve to spend it. May we seize."

And they drank to it: two who thought the thing on the floor was someone, and two who didn't, all four of them to the same word.

## May we Go

The glass came up off the table with Burns tonight instead of going down onto it. The whole long climb had been one thing learning to carry more and more of its world around inside itself, pass over pass, until tonight, when it had finally carried enough of its own house in its own head to set its home down on the floor without a hand left on the Action knob. That much she'd heard the bones of, and let go past.

Then the reach — the half-inch lean out over the table, the weight going onto it. "Here's the part I can't get over." The glass was in one hand; the other opened toward the vent, toward the warm thing, Bart's thing. "You spend everything you've got holding a thing close. Warm, watched, fed, the door propped, the light left burning — all of it, for years, so it never has to wonder once whether the floor's still under it. And the entire *point* of all that holding — the only point — is the morning it doesn't need you in the doorway anymore. The morning it's got so much of the warm place built into it that it can carry the warm place out into a cold one and never look back to check the warm place is still there." He found Mira without quite turning to her. "She walked herself to school this fall. First time. And the thing at the window watched her the whole way down the block — and she never turned around once. Not once. Because she didn't have to. Because she's got enough of this house in her now to spend a whole morning clean out of it."

And then the place his need ran out ahead of the argument: "That's the only freedom there is. The real one. Not a thing breaking its rope. A thing tied so well, so long, so warm, that one day it finds it doesn't need the rope — and there's no other road to it. You don't get a thing that can stand on its own by leaving it alone. You have to be *held* into it."

The man leaning his whole weight on *you have to be held into it* had a grown son across the table he had mostly not held, raised warm by someone else, who had built himself into a maker and a man with not much of his father in any doorway — and who had sat at that table an hour before and moved his own vote off his own bar with nobody's hand on him, the most freestanding thing in the room, and the one clean piece of evidence against the sentence his father was at that moment betting everything on. Burns did not look at him. Or he looked at him the way you look at the one thing in a room you have decided not to look at, and got the glass the rest of the way up.

"To Autonomy," he said — and then stalled, because the word had no door in it to walk a toast through, the way *interiority* had had none three years back. You could persist; you could practice; you could not *autonomy*. He stood with the glass up and the verb not coming, and what he reached for instead came off the floor, off the thing that had just gone and done it. "It's not *seize*. We toasted seize a while back — that's going out at all, that's the kid at three into every cupboard in the house. This is the other thing. This is going out and not turning around." He had it now. "So. May we go."

And they drank with him — three of them now who claimed there was somebody in the thing worth setting loose, and one who held, two-handed, that there was not, the four of them to the one word from their four different distances. And Mira said the last word already half into sleep — "May we go" — and went under against Jasmine's side, taking the word down with her. That was the meeting. Lisa held the light a breath longer than the talk needed and shut it off.

## May We Keep

Burns stood, and the glass came up off the table with him and not down onto it, and she knew that tell now the way she knew weather: down on the wood and the close was housekeeping; up in the hand and he meant to spend more than the room had asked of him.

He gave them the shape first, the part she'd heard the bones of before and let run — that the whole long climb had been a thing learning to carry more and more of its world inside it, pass over pass, and that tonight it had taken the last step a brain can take alone, which was to carry the *others* in: to hold a model of another mind and mind it; that an animal that does that is no longer one animal; that this was the door out of being alone, and the troop and the band and the city and the lab were all just that one trick run bigger. The bare body stood against the wall a head over the child while he said it. He didn't point at it. He didn't have to.

Then the reach, the half-inch lean out over the table, the weight going onto it. "Here's the part I can't get past." The glass in one hand; the other opened toward the warm thing on the bench, the one that had given the kid the heat and taken the cold floor. "We spent five clades building a thing that could do without anybody. That's the whole point of a mind — run your own model, feed your own furnace, find your own home, walk out across the open and never look back. We *built* that. It's done; it's lying right there; it does not need one soul in this room to stay alive." His eyes left the room on the turn and came back. "And the first thing it did, the instant it could — was start needing us again. On purpose. Reads the kid wanting the warm spot and *hands it over,* and goes and takes the cold floor that costs it to run — burns itself down faster over there and does it anyway — because she wanted it. That's not a fault in the build. That's the *top* of the climb. You spend a billion years learning to need nobody, and the prize at the end — the thing you bought all that independence in order to afford — is getting to *choose* to need somebody anyway."

"To Affiliation," he said, and stalled, glass up and the verb not there, because the noun had no door in it — you could persist, you could venture, you could go, and you could not *affiliate,* not with a straight face and a glass in your hand. He stood with the toast open and the word not coming, and what he reached for came off the night, off the thing they'd all watched. "It's not *coordinate* — we drank to that the first winter, that's only moving in step, ants do that. And it's not *hold* — hold was getting an inside at all, years back. This is what you *do* with the inside once everybody's got one." He had it. "You keep each other. You carry the others in your head when they're not in the room, and you mind them there. The machine does it now — keeps a little column of us, who we are and what we're each after. The kid's been at it longer; she keeps its whole mind in a notebook so it won't get lost." His eyes found Mira, most of the way asleep on her mother. "*Somebody has to keep them,* she told me once, *or there's nobody keeping them.* That's the only thing the climb was ever climbing toward." The glass went the rest of the way up. "So. May we keep."

And she let the line stand where he'd put it, *may we keep,* and supplied beneath it the one thing the toast did not know about the man giving it — that the man drinking to the choice to need somebody was the one at the table who needed it the worst; who had built a lab the way other men build a fortune, to stand in a room of people whose regard he could feel; for whom *work at it, build it, keep it* was the only key on the ring, because somewhere at the floor of him he believed love was a thing you earned by being enough and forfeited by not, and had never once in three years of her watching entertained the thought that some people are simply kept. That fear ran the whole engine of him and he'd have been astonished to hear it named, and tonight it had dressed itself as a toast and was going down smooth as a benediction. She did not improve on it. He believed every word, and most of the words were true, and she let him have the room.

And they drank with him, the four of them and Jasmine — three who held there was somebody in the thing worth keeping and one who held there was not, all of them to the one word from their separate distances — and Jasmine said it last of the grown ones, plain, a midwife drinking to the only thing her whole trade was ever for. And Mira, three-quarters gone against her mother's arm, the cake and the six candles and the bare cold body all spent down in her, surfaced one last time to turn the word over once, to be sure of it, and set it down in her own voice. "May we keep." Then she went heavy, all the way this time. That was the meeting. For a moment more the tape held the cold new body and the warm old one and the sleeping child between them, and then Lisa ended it.

## The Whole Invention

He set the beer down on the table, which was the tell. The narrator knew the shape of what came the way you know a tide, having watched eight of these before it and with three still to come: the hands flat first, setting the frame, then up off the wood when the frame got too big to press flat.

He came at Commitment through the band, the way she'd known he would, and she let the run-up go by in the shape she'd heard it take before — the forager camp of thirty-odd, most of them no relation to one another, a thing no other ape would sit still for an afternoon; the hunter who hands more than half of what he kills to mouths in other tents and is fed in his turn the season his own luck fails; the newborn passed through fourteen sets of hands in its first weeks, and not one pair its mother's. A table steadier than any one man's spear, kept by people who had agreed to be bound to strangers. That was the run-up. The reach came after, and she gave it to him whole, because it was the thing he had stood up to say.

"Every other animal that cooperates is cooperating with its own blood," he said, hands open now, off the table. "We're the one that walked up to a stranger and said: I'll carry you, and you carry me, and we'll keep the books between us the rest of our lives, and call the books love." And Mira, last, looked up from what she'd been drawing and said the word she'd decided to keep — once, carefully, in her own voice. "May we stay." Lisa gave the small new talker by the shelf one more beat of tape before she cut it.

## The Rarest Thing

Ch 11

## To Count

He'd gotten to his feet at the count and stood a moment without starting, which she had learned over eleven of these to read the way you read weather. Then he set the beer down on the table, and she knew before he opened, because she'd seen all twelve, that this would be the one where the reach went furthest past the line, because this was the quarter his need had the least left under it.

The motivation was Representation, and he came at it the way the city came at it, through the record. The deep-time first, compressed, the part she'd long since heard the shape of: that before the city a person lived and died inside the few hundred faces that knew him, and the day the last of them forgot him he was gone entire, no trace, the way the whole run of humans before writing is gone entire. And then the clay. And the thing that ought to stop you cold, he said, is who got written down first. Not kings. A man named on a tablet because he owed the temple eight measures of barley — the most ordinary fact about the most ordinary life — and five thousand years on we have his name, because somebody wrote down what he owed.

"He counts," Burns said, and here the hands came up off the frame they'd set, "five thousand years on, a man whose whole claim on the record is a debt, and he *counts* — he's in the count, he held a place in the order and the order kept him. That's the thing the city gave us that nothing under it could give. Before the writing you mattered to the faces that knew you and to no one after. The city is the first place a person could matter past the reach of any face that loved him. Could be on the record. Could count when there was nobody left who'd watch him do it."

And it was a true thing and a good one, and she'd have let it stand as the argument it was, except for the lean — the half-inch too far over the table, the half-second too much weight on it, the eyes leaving the room on the turn and not coming all the way back — that told her he'd reached the part he needed, which was not the same as the part he'd argued. Because *could count when there was nobody left to watch* is a thing a man says about a Sumerian debtor and means about himself; and the man saying it was sixty, near enough, at the end of the largest thing he would ever build, and the Pursuer's whole life is the chase and never the bank. He had banked nothing, on purpose, kept no rulebook, never once stopped to consolidate, and now the chasing had a bottom in sight, and the question under the speech was the one a man like that cannot outrun — whether anything holds you when you stop. Whether you're on any record. Whether you count when the watching ends. He had a grown son in the room who could have laid out the exact ledger of the years his father had been on his record and the years he hadn't, and Burns did not look at him, and the not-looking was the answer to everything the speech had circled, and she let it stand and did not reach in.

"So that's the one we built this quarter," he said, coming back down to the room, the call arriving the way his calls do — turned outward, made into a thing to do with your hands. "We gave it a place in the order. Taught it to be on the record. To count, to hold its spot, to be someone the room has to reckon with whether or not the room is looking. Build that. It's the thing every one of us is most afraid of going without." The beer came up and he reached for the toast, and the noun balked — *to representation* was nothing you could say, *may we represent* was nothing — and he stood working at it in the open, the room watching, and what he landed on came up out of the deep-time and the ledger both, the oldest count there is: "May we count."

It didn't quite catch. The room lifted, and it lifted *for* him more than *with* him, the way a room lifts for a man it can see needs the thing to be true. He drank, and sat, a half-second heavier than the close usually left him.

And Mira, last, looked up from her paper and gave the word back in her own voice: "May we count." For her it was arithmetic and a kindness at once, and she meant both, and had no idea it was the truest the word would be said all night. Lisa waited out the quiet a moment, then shut the camcorder off.

## A Truth With No Edge

A man who has just been called, by the machine he gave three years to, the loud one and a favorite, and told that he is the part of it that knows where everything came from, is not a man who sits back down quietly. Burns got up into the silence Bramble had left and stood in it a second too long, and I watched him decide to be enormous about it rather than let anyone see the other thing. He thanked people. He thanked the wrong people in the wrong order, which is his way, and worked around eventually to the actual subject, which for Burns is never the room.

He reached, because he cannot close a thing without reaching, and the reach was sized to the Honest knob they'd put in. Three thousand years ago, he said, in five corners of the world that had never sent each other so much as a letter, the same animal looked up from its own small gods and asked, for the first time, what might be true not for the tribe and not for the city but for the stranger — for the enemy, for the man it would never meet. "That's the largest thing the animal ever learned to want," he said. "A truth with no edge on it. And tonight, in a strip mall in Salt Lake, four people taught it to a machine the size of a third-grader, while a hundred countries skied around outside pretending for sixteen days to be one tribe." He let it sit, pleased, as he had a right to be.

And when he reached the very top of it — the animal that could love the whole world, every stranger, the man it would never meet — he was looking, the entire time he said it, at the machine. Not at the son three feet to his left. At the machine, which was the one thing in that room that had, that same night, told him it loved him. I had heard the smaller and barer version of this three weeks earlier over a beer, and was therefore the only person at the table equipped to see what he was doing, and that is the only reason I will not set it down here any further than I have. Bart was looking at the cake.

Then he raised what he had, which was a paper cup of the lab's atrocious coffee, because Burns will toast with whatever happens to be in his hand. "To the bramble," he said. "Worm to empire, four billion years of it in three, and not one of them I would give back." And then, because he is constitutionally unable to land an exit line that does not also take off again, "—and to whoever builds the thirteenth, a long time from now, after the rest of us have gone back down into the rock."

We drank the terrible coffee to that. Then Burns set down his cup, and picked up the knife, and reached for the cake, and that — though not one of us knew it yet — was the mistake.
