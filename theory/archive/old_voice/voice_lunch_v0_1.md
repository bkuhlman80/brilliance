## Close-present lunch register

The register for the twelve interlude lunches, in the first person — Cheryl is in scene and narrates as herself. Her interior is open. The other person is rendered from outside but with the warmth of presence in the room. Cheryl uses the nicknames throughout, including at lunches — she joined at the Pitch and never knew the real names socially. Real names surface one per lunch, each at that character's own (see book_inventions). The lunches render faces and clothing closer than the rest of the book, in daylit light — Cheryl's implicit correction of the reader's mental image; bodies elsewhere stay at lab-anchor resolution.

## Close-retrospective register

Cheryl thinking aloud, looking back, in the first person. Used in the Pitch (Ch 0's closing scene), the twelve interlude lunches, and the Ch 12 retrospective when she narrates her own interior. The Helen passage:

> The relationship had lasted seven years and it had been wrong from the second one. I could see that now. I could not see it then, because at thirty-four I still believed in talking things through, which is one of the great optimisms of the post-therapy generation — that the right number of words, said in the right order, in the right register, could move a person from one shape to another. I had been raised on that optimism. I thought I was good at it.

> My partner's name was Helen. Helen was wiry and small, with the working forearms of a sculptor and a face that did not advertise itself, and she lived inside her hands, which was a kind of life I did not understand but loved the look of. Helen made small bronze things — mostly birds, sometimes hands themselves, once an apple that weighed eleven pounds and that I carried back from a gallery in San Francisco in 1997 wrapped in two flannel shirts. Helen liked one cup of coffee in the morning and another at three, did not want to travel, did not want to talk on the phone, did not want to be seen at parties, did not want to host parties, did not want to host anyone, did not want a dog, did not want a child, did not want, in the end, much of anything that I wanted. We had agreed about this in the second year. We had re-agreed in the third. By the fifth year I was writing it down on note cards, because writing things down is what you do when you can feel a thing slipping. The note cards did not work. None of it worked. We split in 2002 in a kitchen in Sausalito, calmly, with a bottle of wine half-finished between us, and I drove east the next morning and did not stop driving for a long time.

> I thought, for years afterward, that the failure had been mine. Then I thought it had been Helen's. Then I thought it had been the era's, or San Francisco's, or the particular weather of a relationship between an introvert and an extrovert in their thirties. I tried all the available frames. None of them held. What I could not stop noticing was that Helen and I had not gotten worse at being ourselves over those seven years. We had gotten *better* at it. Helen had grown more Helen. I had grown more myself. The talking had not softened either shape; it had clarified both. We had ended because we were finishing becoming what we were, and what we were did not fit, and no amount of words was going to retrofit two adult organisms into a compatible pair.

> This was not, I would later say, a discovery available to me at thirty-four. At forty-one I could see it. At fifty-two I could see further: that the shapes Helen and I had been finishing were not invented in our childhoods or our relationships or our cities, but assembled across a stack of evolutionary deep time so old that human personalities are barely the topsoil on it. You could not talk a person into a different topsoil. You could maybe pull a weed.

> I bought books. I read them out of order. I read them on planes. I read Friston without understanding him and Watson and Szathmáry without understanding them and Mackintosh once all the way through. I got obsessed with the question of what *layer* a person was operating from at any given moment — whether a partner saying *I do not want to host anyone* was speaking from a learned preference, a temperament, something older. I did not have the vocabulary then. I had a hunch that the vocabulary was somewhere out in the literature, scattered across fields that did not talk to each other, and that if anyone was ever going to put it together it would not be me — I was a synthesizer, not an originator — but that I might be the person who handed the pieces to four people in a warehouse in Salt Lake City and asked them to build a robot out of them.

> That was the line from Helen to Bramble. I was aware that this was a strange line. I was aware that it sounded, when I said it aloud, like the kind of thing a wealthy woman tells herself to explain the money she has spent. I was probably wrong about parts of it. I was sure I was wrong about parts of it. But I had funded stranger ideas with thinner reasons, and at least this one started with a real loss.
