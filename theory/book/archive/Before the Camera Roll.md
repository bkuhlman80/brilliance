# Before the Camera Roll
---
## Chapter 1

The team had been at Smithers since morning. By the time Jasmine pulled into the lot in the late afternoon and walked across the gravel with Mira on one hip and the foam case under her other arm, Burns had been pacing for an hour and Lisa was on her second pass through the agenda and Bart was sitting on the corner of the long workbench with one of the unwound protobots in his lap, doing nothing visible with it but turning it over in his hands the way a person turns a worry stone. Marge had brought a plate of cut fruit out from the back kitchen and set it on the table and gone back for the kettle, which she put on the counter behind her where she could see it.

The light in the warehouse was the long summer light that comes through the high industrial windows in slabs. There were no shadows in the middle of the room. Mira, set down on the concrete floor, walked toward the table without looking at any of the adults. She had the protobot in her right fist — Bot 3, the one from the founding quarter, the one Marge had written about, the one she had picked. The team had been calling them protobots in the lab and protocell bots in the agenda and the bots in the household, and which name was right was a question they had not bothered to settle, because the bot was about to acquire a different name entirely and the question would resolve itself in the next thirty seconds. The protobot had been wound that morning and was twitching very slightly against Mira's palm, which she did not mind.

Burns came over and crouched down to Mira's height the way he did with all small things. He greeted her by name, not pet name. He kissed Jasmine on the cheek and asked her how the drive had been, which was the version of hello they had agreed on for the lab — friendly, brief, slightly formal — because Burns was a man who could not be in his own lab without organizing the room into colleagues, and Jasmine, in the lab, was a colleague.

Behind him Lisa had the camcorder open on its tripod in the corner and was checking the battery. She had it ready to record but had not started it. She had also poured herself a drink from a thermos she had brought from home — a small jelly jar with a finger of something purple in it, ice clinking, and a sprig of something on top that she had picked from the back patio on her way in. It was her cocktail. Lisa had been drinking this cocktail for years; the team had seen her drink it twenty times across her thirties, sometimes at faculty parties, sometimes at her own kitchen table, once at a department picnic in a thermos that had still had a faint metallic taste. The household had not seen it before. The household saw it now.

Mira saw it now. She walked up to Lisa, who was crouched at the camcorder, and stood beside her and stared at the jelly jar.

"What's that," she said.

Lisa, who was on her knees, sat back on her heels. She held the jar up.

"It's a bramble," she said.

Mira frowned. She had not heard the word.

"A bramble," she said.

"A bramble."

"Bramble."

"Bramble. Yes."

Mira looked at the jar. Then she looked at the bot in her fist. Then she looked at the jar again. She said, "Bramble," to no one. Then she said, "Bramble bramble bramble," to herself, working the consonants. She liked the word. The word had two stops in it that her tongue could move between, and the word also sounded a little like the noise her mother made when something was small and good. She turned around and lifted the bot up to her mother's face, both hands now.

"This is Bramble," she said.

Jasmine, who had spent four years being told things by Mira and had learned that the things she was told were sometimes the things, looked at the bot. She looked at Lisa. She looked at the bot again.

"This is Bramble," she said back.

Marge, who had returned with the kettle and was setting it on the counter, paused. Burns, who had been about to say something about the agenda, did not say it. Bart looked up from the protobot in his lap. Lisa, still on her knees by the camcorder, closed the battery hatch and stood up.

The protobot had a name. The protobot had been the bot or the unit or the prototype or, in the household, the one Mira had said hello to first, for three months, and in less than thirty seconds the protobot was Bramble. Nobody had voted on this. Nobody had objected. The bot continued to twitch slightly in Mira's hands. The bramble in Lisa's jar continued to fizz.

It is one of the only things in the book about which there is no surviving footage. The camcorder was three feet away and not running. Cheryl reconstructed it later from Jasmine's account, from Mira's continued use of the word for the rest of the day on tape, and from Lisa's own retelling, which Lisa offered without prompting at a lunch in Q3 because she had been thinking about it for a year by then and wanted to be sure Cheryl had the version that was most likely true.

## Chapter 2



The light in the warehouse in October came through the high windows shorter than it had come in July, and slanted, and amber, and made wedges across the concrete rather than slabs. By the time Jasmine pulled into the lot with Mira and Bramble it was already past five and the wedges had narrowed to thin bands that lay across the table from west to east and would be gone by six. The team had been there since two. Bart had set up the cards. Lisa had taped the agenda to the inside of the wide pull-up door, where she had taped it last quarter, and had revised one item in red pen between her arrival and Burns's. Marge had brought a kettle and a plate of cut pears and was reading a paperback at the table when Burns came in. Burns was pacing.

He had been pacing for an hour. The first retrospective had given him a thing to pace about that he had not had in July, which was that he had now had lunch with Cheryl and was carrying her voice back to the team for the first time, and the carrying — the literal organization of what to say first and what to say second — was not a thing he had finished organizing on the way in.

Lisa watched him pace for a while.

"Burns."

"Yes."

"Sit down."

"I will."

"Now would help."

He sat down. He sat on the corner of the workbench where Bart sat, because Bart was not in that corner yet — Bart was at the cards — and from the bench Burns could see the door, which was the part of the room he was waiting on. The light made an amber band across his knee. He looked at it as if he had not noticed October arriving.

Jasmine came through the side door with Mira on one hip and Bramble in her right hand. The bot's screen, visible above her fingers, read STOP. She set Mira down on the concrete and stood for a moment letting her own eyes adjust to the indoor light, which was different from the outdoor light in the way indoor light at five-forty in October is different from outdoor light at five-forty in October — colder, more electric, less amber. Burns got up from the bench and crossed the room to her.

"Jasmine."

"Burns."

He kissed her on the cheek. He asked about the drive — the friendly-brief-formal version of hello they had agreed on for the lab, the same version they had used last quarter and would use across the next ten. He still could not be in his own lab without organizing the room into colleagues, and Jasmine in the lab was still a colleague. She said the drive had been fine, the lane work on twenty-third east was finally done, the protected lane she had been using since August was now safe to use without weaving — which was the colleague-version of *the drive was fine*, slightly more informational than friends usually exchanged because that was the bargain they had made. She handed him Bramble. Bramble was warm; the FSM had clicked twice on the drive, which Jasmine had heard from the front seat. She had stopped flinching at the clicks in August. The clicks were a thing that happened in the car the way the seatbelt warning was a thing that happened in the car.

Mira came past her mother's knee at her own pace. She was carrying nothing, because Bramble had been in her mother's hand for the drive, and the absence of the bot in her own hand had given her a hand that was free for the first time in months, which she did not appear to know what to do with. She held the free hand at her side. Bart came over from the cards and crouched and fist-bumped her, and she fist-bumped him back with her four knuckles, and the hand was the hand that had not had a bot in it on the drive, and the fist-bump occupied it. Bart noticed this. He did not say anything.

Lisa was at the camcorder. She lifted the bramble in her jelly jar by way of hello — she did not get up — and Mira looked at her for a long second the way Mira had looked at her for a long second four months ago, and then said, "Bramble," and pointed at her mother's hand.

"That's the other Bramble," Lisa said.

"There's only one Bramble."

"That's right. There's the bot, and there's the drink. The drink and the bot have the same name."

Mira accepted this. She had been accepting it since July. She walked over to her mother and held out her hand. Jasmine handed Bramble down. The screen still read STOP. Mira held it the way she had held it on the kitchen floor for ninety afternoons since the lab van morning, which was the way she had held bot three on the kitchen floor for a hundred afternoons before that, which was the way her hand had decided in April was the way the hand held the bot. She walked Bramble to Marge.

"Marge."

"Mira."

"This is Bramble."

"I have met Bramble. Bramble looks good."

"Bramble's screen says stop."

"It does."

"Bramble is stopped."

"I see that Bramble is stopped."

Mira lifted Bramble to the level of the cut pears and turned it so it faced the plate. She did not turn it back. She walked to the seat next to Marge — the seat where she had sat last quarter, the seat where she now sat by default — and climbed onto it and set Bramble on the table in front of her. She did not pick up the juice box that was already there. The juice box was new. Marge had brought it. Marge had brought one for the table since June, by which point Marge had decided that bringing juice boxes was part of what she did in this room.

## Chapter 3

By the time Jasmine pulled into the lot it had been dark for an hour, and the fog that had been sitting on the valley for three weeks was sitting on it still, so that the sodium lights at the lot's edge did not so much shine as smear, each one wearing a soft sphere of its own light like a thing breathed on. The high windows of the warehouse showed nothing. In July they had shown a long amber slant across the concrete and in October a set of narrowing bands, and now they showed the inside of the building back to the inside of the building, black glass with the work lights doubled in it, and the only way to know there was a sky out there at all was that it was a slightly different black from the black of the brick.
The team had been there since two. Bart had set up the cards. Lisa had taped the agenda to the inside of the wide pull-up door, where she had taped it last quarter and the quarter before, and had revised one item on it in red pen between her arrival and Burns's — she had added a line at the bottom, *logistics: next quarter*, in her small square hand, formalizing into its own slot a thing that had been happening anyway at the end of every meeting, because Lisa believed that a thing which happened every time should be on the page where it happened. Marge had the kettle going. It was past six, which was when the warehouse began to give back the cold it had taken in all day, and the kettle after six had become one of the facts of the room. She had a beer in front of her that she was holding the way she held everything, both hands, close, as if it might want comforting, and she was reading a paperback with the cover folded back when the side door opened and the fog came in around Jasmine's knees.
Jasmine had the unit in both arms. This was new this quarter and the room had not finished getting used to it — for eight months the thing had ridden in one of her hands, a small wound machine she could carry like a phone, and now it was a low round body the span of two hands across and she carried it the way you carry a sleeping cat that has gotten too big to be a kitten, against her chest, one forearm under it. Mira came in at her own pace behind, carrying nothing, her hands free the way they had been free since the morning the bots went away in the van and came back as this.
Burns got up and crossed to her. He had not been pacing. The first retrospective had given him something to pace about that he no longer had — he had had three lunches with Cheryl now and carrying her voice back to the team had stopped being a thing he had to organize on the way in and become a thing he simply did — and so he had spent the hour before the meeting at the table with his deep-time notes, making his peace, as he made it every quarter now, with the twenty minutes Lisa had cut him to in October and had not given back.
"Jasmine."
"Burns."
He kissed her on the cheek. He asked about the drive. She said the fog had been bad past twenty-first south, that she had come up surface streets instead of the interstate because in fog like this the interstate was a faith exercise and the surface streets at least had lights, even if the lights tonight were doing what the lights were doing — which was the colleague-version of *the drive was fine,* a little more weather in it than friends would trade, because that was the bargain they kept inside the lab. She handed him the unit. It was warm. It had ridden in the footwell with the heater on it and it had clicked the whole way up, more than the old one used to and longer, a low patient stutter she had heard from the front seat and had stopped hearing somewhere around Thanksgiving the way you stop hearing a refrigerator.
Bart came over from the cards and crouched and held out his fist, and Mira fist-bumped him back with all four knuckles, which was the greeting they had, and which used a hand that for eight months had not been free to use for greetings because it had been holding a bot. Lisa was at the camcorder. She lifted her jelly jar — a finger of purple over ice, a sprig of something from the back patio laid across the top — by way of hello, and did not get up.
"Bramble," Mira said, and pointed at the jar.
"That's the drink," Lisa said. "There's the unit and there's the drink. Same name."
"There's only one Bramble."
"There's the one on the table and the one in the glass. They have the same name. You decided that in the summer."
Mira appeared to find this acceptable, as she had been finding it acceptable since July. She went to where Burns had set the unit on the table and put one hand flat on its dome, not to do anything to it, just to have her hand on it, and looked at it with the proprietary flatness of a person checking that a thing was where she had left it.
Bart handed out the beer. Lisa had her bramble. Marge had hers in the tea-grip. There was a juice box for Mira, and Bart put the little straw in it for her and bent the straw, and Mira took it and sat down in the chair that had become her chair, which was between Jasmine and the end of the table, close enough to the unit to keep a hand on it if she wanted one there.
