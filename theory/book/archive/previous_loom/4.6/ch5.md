Ch 1

## The Bramblogues

Lisa turned to Jasmine. "Three months, five bots. We've got your written reports, Mira's drawings, a tape from June. What's missing from what we have."

Jasmine, quiet for an hour, drank some of her beer. "Nothing big. What you have is what I have. They twitch, they go still, we crank them Sundays, the floor goes cool under them. Four lives under the toaster in a folded towel. Three — Bramble — Mira carries around: the kitchen, the living room, sometimes to the bathroom while she brushes her teeth, though it's not allowed on the counter, so we keep it in a low chair. The others stay where she's put them. Five's in a sunbeam, one and two by the island. We haven't lost any."

She thought. "The thing I want to tell you is that I didn't turn the radiant heat off in May."

The team waited.

"I always turn it off in May. Burns told me in April the bots needed the gradient — but I'd known what they were before April. Second week, I knelt for something Mira dropped and put my hand flat on the slate, and the stone under the bot was cold. Cold right where it sat, warm a few inches over. He'd told me how they worked back in February, on the step, and I'd nodded and lost it. I didn't have it till I had my hand on the cold. So when May came I left the heat on, and I didn't say anything about leaving it on. It's been on two months longer than it's ever been. The floor's warm. The bots run. That's the household decision I want on the record, because I think it's going to matter."

Burns looked at her. "You didn't tell me you did this."

She smiled. "You didn't turn the radiant off."

"I did not."

"All summer."

"All summer. I'm running the heat in July. For the bots. Yep."

He looked at the bot in Mira's hands, then at his wife. "OK."

Marge had been watching Jasmine. "You knew it by hand," she said. "Before any of us knew you knew it." Jasmine shrugged, which was a yes.

Ch 2

## The Hard Question

Lisa turned to Jasmine. "The house. What did the quarter look like."

"Bramble drives now. That is what's different. Bramble is on the floor with the others and the others are still doing what they were doing but Bramble has a place to be and Bramble goes there. In the morning Bramble goes to the east window. At ten Bramble is in the south window. By noon Bramble has been to the back door and has come back to the kitchen because the back door sunbeam goes away. By evening Bramble is in the corner where the noon sunbeam was at noon, on STOP, charging on whatever has trickled in from late afternoon. The cadence is — I learned the cadence by August. The clicks, when the FSM transitions, are loud enough I can hear from the bedroom. I have started knowing what Bramble is doing without being in the room. I can be in the laundry and I can hear the relays say RUN and I will know the back door sunbeam is open."

She drank.

"The other four are where they were. Two by the island. One in the noon sunbeam in the kitchen. One under the toaster — bot four, in the bed Mira made it. The slate under the bed is cooler than the rest of the floor. By a few degrees. Hand-feelable. Bot four is, like the others, cooling the floor it sits on. The cooling has gotten more obvious since the cool weather started, which I do not know what to make of."

Lisa said, "The differential is the same. The room is just colder."

"The room is colder. The contrast is sharper because the warm spots are smaller. OK."

Jasmine continued.

"When Bramble is going for the sunbeam by the sink, sometimes one of the four protobots is in the path. Mira put it there, or it has been there a while — most of them haven't been moved much since May. Bramble comes in pursuing the sunbeam. Bramble does not see the protobot. Bramble pushes through. The protobot slides a few inches to one side and Bramble bumps it again and then parks in the sun. The protobot ends up on a patch of floor that wasn't in any particular place, just where it slid to. It keeps twitching. Bramble takes a nap and then keeps going. The protobot does not come back to where it was."

She paused.

"That's on the slow one — the patient one, the slow lean, half a foot across a morning. On the fast one it's the same blind thing, just meaner-looking: it doesn't lean into the protobot, it charges it, a flurry straight in that knocks it a clear foot before Bramble parks. Same not-seeing, either way. Mira likes it fast. I keep it slow, because on the fast one it chases the sun twice as hard and goes through a charge so fast I'm cranking it by Thursday. Which way it's set depends on who got to the knob last."

Lisa said, "What does it look like from the room."

Jasmine said, "It looks like Bramble is picking on them — leaning on them slow, flinging them fast, but picking on them either way. It looks like one of them is a creature and four of them are furniture. It looks like, in the kitchen, somebody has put one cat down with four wind-up mice and the cat is doing cat things and the mice are wind-up mice."

Lisa said, "That is a good way to say it."

Jasmine said, "I have had ten weeks to find the words for it."

Marge wrote *one cat, four wind-up mice* on her notepad.

Mira, who had been on her seat with Bramble in front of her, leaned forward. The forward-lean was the lean of a four-year-old who had been listening at the edge of attention for forty minutes and had felt the conversation come around to the kitchen. She put her right hand on the table, flat, and slid it across the table along an inch or two. She did it twice. She looked up.

"That's the bumping."

Marge said, "Yes."

"That's the bumping Bramble does."

"Like that." Marge slid her hand.

Mira slid her hand again. She did it slowly. She left her hand at the new spot. She looked at Bramble. She looked at the team.

"Why did the lab only upgrade Bramble."

The room held.

Burns said, "We — that's how the project works, kid. We pick one and we work on it."

Mira said, "Why."

"Because we can only work on one at a time."

Mira said, "Why."

Burns laughed the chest-laugh. He laughed because Mira had done the four-year-old's *why* and he had walked into it. He laughed because nobody had a good answer. He laughed because the answer was, in some sense, the clade. He looked at Marge.

Marge said, "Mira."

Mira said, "What."

"You are asking a hard question."

"Oh."

"You are asking — what you are asking — is something the lab did on purpose. The lab picked Bramble because you picked Bramble."

"Why didn't the lab upgrade the others."

"Because the lab is small."

"Why."

"Because there are four of us and we are doing as much as we can."

Mira said, "OK." She looked at the four-bot-shaped absence at the center of the table — there was no absence visible, but Mira was looking at the spot where one would have been if one had been there, which was the spot Mira's eyes had been on for a while.

Burns said, "It is also that the lab is *trying* to keep the others as they were, so we can compare. The four are part of the experiment. They are doing what they did. We need them to keep doing what they did. So we can see what changed."

"They are stuck."

"They are how they have always been."

"Bramble isn't."

"Bramble isn't."

"That's not fair."

Burns laughed. The laugh was not the chest-laugh. The laugh was a smaller laugh, a quieter one, the laugh of a man who has been worked into a corner by a four-year-old and is appreciating it. Marge did not laugh. Marge watched Mira. Marge had been writing on her notepad and stopped writing on her notepad.

Jasmine said, "Mira. It is not — it is not the kind of thing the bots feel."

Mira said, "OK."

She said it without conviction. She slid her hand again on the table. She did it the way she had done it the first time, which was the slide of Bramble pushing a protobot out of the sunbeam.

Lisa, who had not said anything for a stretch, said, "Mira. You just said the thing the whole meeting was about."

She did not say they would come back to it. There was nowhere left in the meeting to come back to it from.

She did not let go of the slide. Her hand stayed on the table where it had ended up. She looked at it for a beat. Then she picked up Bramble — left-handed — and set Bramble on top of her right hand, on the spot her right hand had ended up at. The bot's screen read STOP. Bramble sat on her hand. Her hand was no longer the bumping hand. Her hand was the parking lot.

Marge wrote something on her notepad and underlined it.

Ch 3

## What It's Doing

Jasmine had been waiting for the floor, without leaning forward, the depleted patience of someone who had been up at two with a labor and had still come, and who was read by the team, when they read her at all, as merely tired.

"He vacuums," she said. "I'll start with the plain things, because the plain things are most of living with him. He vacuums. He goes around the kitchen and the hall and he lifts up crumbs and grit and whatever Mira's dropped, and a little panel on the top of him lights up the word STORED while he's carrying anything, and he keeps what he picks up instead of dragging it around behind him like the old one would've. And when he happens past the base Bart put by the dishwasher, he climbs on, feeds a minute, and goes again. Some days he feeds a lot. Some days he doesn't feed at all. He's good. He does a lot. The house is cleaner than it's been since Mira learned to throw food."

"The thing I noticed most, though — the thing that's new in the way he *behaves,* not just what he does — is the Explore knob. One way, he settles on the same moves and runs them over and over, tighter and tighter, until he's basically worn a groove — he runs the rounds he's run before, he's efficient and a little boring and he never bumps into anything. The other way, he stops trusting the groove. He tries moves he's never made, pokes in behind the trash can, gets himself somewhere new. More misses that way. Bumps things. But he turns up in places the groove never took him. And I can feel the difference from across the room before I remember which way I left him set — there's a settled version of him and a restless version of him, and they're the same machine."

"Hold that," Lisa said. "That's the Explore knob. Keep going on the household."

"Mira's started telling him what he likes," Jasmine said.

Mira looked up.

"Bramble likes the kitchen," Mira said. "Bramble does not like the bath mat."

"He stalls on the bath mat," Jasmine said. "High pile, he can't climb it, he reverses off. Mira's decided that's a dislike. And last week —"

"Bramble got stuck on purpose," Mira said.

"She dropped a coin in front of him," Jasmine said, "to see what he'd do. He took the coin up with the crumbs, carried it to the base, and the base wouldn't take the coin, lit up ERROR, and he just — sat there. Didn't leave, didn't sort it out. Sat on the base with the coin he couldn't give up until I reached down and took the coin out by hand. And Mira watched the whole thing and has decided he got stuck on purpose." Jasmine said it plainly, and did not editorialize, though the narrator on the tape, who editorialized about everything from a great distance, would later want to put a whole paragraph in this gap and would make herself leave it as Jasmine left it.

"There's one more plain thing," Jasmine said, "and it's the one I keep turning over. When he runs all the way down — when he hasn't passed the base in a while and the charge just goes — he stops dead, wherever he's standing. And I pick him up and set him on the base, and after a bit he comes back." 

She reached over and stood Mira's juice box back up. 

"I know he doesn't choose the spot. I know he stops wherever the charge gives out." A beat, the quiet she got before the thing she actually meant. "But I'll tell you what it looks like from where I'm standing. It looks like he always stops right in front of me. The middle of the kitchen floor, when I'm the one there to see it. Like he held on until I'd notice. I know that isn't what he's doing. It just really looks like that's what he's doing."

Marge left her beer where it was and answered straight across the table, gently, because the woman was tired and was right about the only part that counted. "It stops in front of you because you're the one in the kitchen," she said. "You're always the one in the kitchen. It isn't holding on for you — there's no one in it to hold on, and no one in it to pick the spot. The holding-on is yours. You're bringing it, and it's a kind thing to bring, and I'd still keep it on your side of the table, because the looking-like is the cheapest thing this machine makes... And speaking of holding on, I am going to ask for a five minute potty break."

Ch 4

## Off the Floor

Jasmine set the juice box down — she'd finished most of Mira's — and took her second to find the start, because the lab came to her wanting findings and what she had was a house.

"Plain things first. He's quieter. You'd think a vacuum getting smarter might get louder, and it went the other way — the clicking's gone, so you don't hear Bramble think anymore. You turn around and he's just somewhere new."

"And he's stopped dying on the floor."

She said it the way the big ones came out of her, like it was nothing.

"All fall I was hauling him to the dock. He'd run himself flat and quit wherever he stood — middle of the kitchen, mostly — and somebody'd carry him back, But now he just breaks off and gets himself fed, before he runs out, and comes back."

"That's my forecaster," Lisa wiped her eyes in her version of a joke. 

"There's a new thing, too," Jasmine said. "With the Action volume up — the one where he stays out where we are — he won't sleep. Can't, with the volume up; he just stays up. So the only way he doesn't run down is he has to break off and feed himself and come back. And you can watch him not want to. He'll be parked in the kitchen doorway, in the foot traffic, which is plainly the spot he's picked — and then there's a moment where he gives the spot up and rouses to go feed, and you can *see* him do it. He leaves the thing he wants to go keep himself alive. Then he comes back to the same doorway." She turned the empty juice box in her fingers.

Bart sat forward on his green pint. "It's got two wants now."

And Mira, who had surfaced from the crayons the moment her own subject came up, said, "I want Bramble to come downstairs."

The table turned to her.

"He can't come in the basement. He gets stuck on the stairs, and then he's just stuck, and somebody has to go up and get him. I want him where I am."

Nobody answered her about the stairs.

It was a silence the narrator only read right on the second pass: five adults deciding all at once not to say the thing, because the thing was standing under a drop-cloth at the end of the table where Mira couldn't see it, and to say it now was to spend it. Jasmine tucked a piece of hair behind Mira's ear. "Soon, bug," she said, which was true and gave away nothing. The cloth at the end of the table went on being a cloth. And the meeting moved on to the part of the night that argued.

Ch 5

## Downstairs

"Bramble block," Lisa said. "Jasmine."

The tetrapod was on the floor by Jasmine's chair, head up, the screen on its face catching a word and giving it up for the next — LISA, the camcorder, MIRA, MIRA again — reading the room one thing at a time the way it read any room now. Jasmine put a hand flat on his back, not to do anything to him, and took her beat, because the table wanted findings and what she had was eleven weeks of a dog-sized thing loose in her kitchen.

"Plain things first. He walks. You've all watched him walk; I won't sell you the walking."

"He comes downstairs," Mira said.

"He comes downstairs. She's wanted that since the fall, and I'd quit believing it ever would — the old one you carried down and it sat where you set it. This one does the basement on his own. Down after her, and back up when she goes. First week, I came down and the two of them were just there, her on the rug and he standing in the doorway like he'd worked out he was allowed in."

"The cold gets into him, too, down there. The basement runs cool, and in the cool he goes heavy — slow to rise, slow to turn, like he's wading. Up by the warm slate, or in a patch of sun, he's quick and sure. I thought for a while he had moods; he doesn't. Bart says a cold battery can't push as hard — so cold makes him slow, warm makes him sharp, and he leans toward the warm. He'll leave a cold corner for a sunbeam and pick up speed as he settles, the way you lean toward a fire without meaning to. When Mira wants him lively I've learned not to send him down to the den and expect much; I let him warm first, by the vent or the furnace, till he's got his quick back. Mira says he sulks when he's cold — her word, from the winter, and she's not let it go, and I've quit arguing it."

She turned the bottle a quarter and didn't drink.

"He lies in the window and takes the sun, or he goes flat on the warm slate by the vent with the cool air coming over his back and drinks the difference. I keep finding him stretched out in a sunbeam like the cat and having to remind myself he's working. He backs out of the dark rooms, which nobody taught him. And he knocks things over, and we've mostly stopped fighting him about that."

Mira looked at the tetrapod by Jasmine's chair, and then at nothing — at the middle of the room, where the other one would be if it were here instead of home on its rounds across the valley.

"Are they the same Bramble," she said.

No one answered, and the quiet that followed was not the kind looking for the right words for a five-year-old. It was the kind that has just found out it has none.

Lisa took it, because the cable had been hers. "For one afternoon, yes," she said. "Same memory, written into two. Every day since, they've each had their own day. So they were one, and they're coming apart."

"Oh."

And that was the end of it, because she'd been handed the true answer and it was not the answer to what she'd asked. She put her hand back on his back where her mother's had been.
