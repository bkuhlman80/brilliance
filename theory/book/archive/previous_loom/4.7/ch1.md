Ch 1

## The Agenda

What followed was Lisa walking the team through her agenda for the meeting. Lisa had drafted the agenda over the course of June and revised it across the second week of July. She had sent it to Burns on a Thursday. He had returned it with no edits, which she had taken to mean either that he agreed with all of it or that he had not read it; either possibility was acceptable to her. She was running the meeting for the first time, and she was going to find out what worked.

She had three content blocks. The first was about the clade — the Protocell, as Cheryl had named it. The team would walk through what a protocell was, what its evolutionary moment had been, what the bots in front of them rendered of that moment. The second block was about the bot itself: the household's three months with the five bots, the question of the wind-up crank as the commission's personality knob, what the next quarter's build would be. The third block was the framework. She did not yet call it the Trellis block. She called it "the bigger picture," with a hand-gesture that meant up.

She turned on the camcorder. The small red LED came on. The flip-out screen showed the room, slightly fish-eyed at the edges, and on it the four labmates plus Jasmine and Mira and Bramble.

"For Cheryl," Lisa said. "She watches the tape."

Nobody addressed the camera. Nobody pretended it wasn't there either. The team had agreed to this in May, after a long conversation in which Bart had been the holdout for forty minutes and had then conceded on the grounds that Cheryl was paying them a great deal of money and they were behaving naturally anyway. Bart looked at the camera once. He nodded at it. He did not nod at it again across the meeting. The camera had been logged in.

Burns took the floor next. He stood up to do it. He had not sat down yet, in fact; he had been standing or crouching for the entire opening, in the way of a man who could not be in a room with the work of the room without orbiting the table at which the work would happen. His hands moved when he talked. Bart, two seats down, watched the hands.

"The thing we are doing," Burns said. "I am going to say it out loud, because this is the first time we are saying it out loud as a team, on tape, with Cheryl watching."

He looked at the camcorder. He looked away from the camcorder.

"We are going to build twelve robots. One per clade. Cheryl has given us the clades. She has not told us what to build and she has not told us what each robot is supposed to teach us. She has told us that each robot must have, in its body somewhere, a knob that controls something about its personality. We are going to figure out what each knob controls. We are going to figure out what each robot's personality is. We are going to figure out, by the end, what personality means."

He paused there.

"That last part," he said, "is the part Cheryl has not asked us to do. She has asked us to deliver the robots. The theory of personality is a thing we are going to build on the side, because we cannot build the robots without it. We are paying for the theory by building the robots. Cheryl is paying for the robots in order to be paid back in the theory. This is, I want to say out loud, the deal as I understand it. If anyone in this room understands it differently they should say so now."

Nobody said anything. Marge said, "I understand it the same way."

"Lisa."

"Same."

"Bart."

"Same."

"Then we are agreed."

He took a sip of his beer. It was an IPA from Fisher's, two blocks away, brought in a growler.

"That's all I have at the open. The rest is in the blocks."

He sat down. He had been standing for the entire opening. The chair felt new under him.

## Trellis

Framework was where the words got set down for good. Bart had been boxing them on the backs of his cards all evening — every time the room reached for one and caught it, he wrote it, underlined it, drew the small box. Now he laid the cards face-up, and they were the night's haul.

"Burns. You left one open at the vent."

He had — he'd reached for the thing the protocell did its work *with* and set his hand back down. He picked the word up now. "Capacity. I've been carrying it a week — read it in a paper I should've read fifteen years ago. The new thing a clade has that the one before it didn't. The protocell's was the capacity to be selected on. Every clade after it adds one."

"And the event — the moment the capacity comes online. We've been fighting about that word." He looked at Marge.

"Innovation," Marge said.

"Innovation."

"Emergent capability."

"Emergent capability."

"Phase transition."

"Phase transition was mine. I'm letting it go." He wrote *breakthrough*, boxed it. "That's the one that lived. We picked the plain word because the others were pretentious and Marge made fun of us. Each clade has a breakthrough."

"Lisa. You left one at the door too — the gravity, the dispositions. Said there was a word in your field and it was wrong for the room."

"Dynamical attractor," Lisa said.

Marge set her beer down. "Can we call them tendencies."

Lisa held the moment with her face neutral, and what she felt — privately, briefly — was the pleasure of being asked to take off a coat she hadn't known she was wearing. "Tendencies. Better than what I had."

"Thank you," Marge said.

Burns laughed the chest-laugh, because there was something cosmically funny to him about a lab choosing the simpler word at the start of a three-year project on the architecture of personality. He laughed early; the room caught up a beat behind. Bart wrote *tendency*, boxed it, and held up two fingers to Burns, who'd been counting.

The last word was the one that fought back.

"The bot is doing something," Lisa said. "When it twitches, when it stays still, when it persists in being wound. There is — and I am going to fish for a word here, because I do not have the right one — there is a *what-the-bot-is-doing-it-for*. A direction. A thing the patterns serve."

Burns said, "Persistence."

"Persistence is the *outcome*. I am asking what is *behind* the persistence."

Bart, who had been turning Bramble in his hand and watching the conversation, said, "Motivation."

There was a small pause.

Marge said, "No."

Bart said, "I know."

"A protocell does not have a motivation."

"Granted."

"There is no intent in this object."

"None. And I still want the word."

Bart set Bramble down. He looked at Marge. He looked at the bot. He looked at the call, which was on the table near Burns's elbow, still in the binding that had been on it for eight months.

"Cheryl gave us a contract that says *personality*. And we are going to spend three years building twelve robots, and the robots are going to be on a continuum from this one, which has nothing inside it, to the one we will build twelve quarters from now, which is going to have an awful lot inside it. And at every clade along the way we are going to be saying, *what is this bot doing? what is it for?* And we have to have a word for the thing it is for, because you cannot talk about personality without talking about what is behind the behavior. We are going to need a word that runs all the way from bacteria to Bach and back." He smiled at Marge. "*Motivation* is the word I am offering. We can fight about whether it fits at Protocell. I think it fits if we let it fit. I think the protobot's *motivation* is to keep being a protobot."

Marge said, "That is anthropomorphizing."

"I know."

"You are going to leak intent into things that do not have intent."

"That's the risk."

"And then we are going to spend three years arguing about whether the bots feel anything."

"We are probably going to spend three years arguing about that anyway."

Marge looked at him. She had been holding her beer the way she held a teacup. She put it down. She looked at the bot. She looked at Lisa.

"Lisa," she said.

"I think it works," Lisa said. "It works the way *tendency* works. The word is a placeholder for a function. We use the word to talk about the function. We do not import the metaphysics."

"You are not going to import the metaphysics."

"I am not."

"Burns is going to import the metaphysics."

"Probably."

"In his speech."

"Possibly."

"In his speech at the close of this meeting."

"Almost certainly."

Burns, who had been listening, said, "I will be careful."

"You will not."

"I will try."

Marge looked at the bot. She held the silence. She picked her beer up again. She held it in tea-posture. She set it down.

"Fine," she said. "I will use it. I am going to wince. But I will use it."

Bart looked at Burns. Burns looked at the camcorder. The red light was on. Burns laughed the chest-laugh.

"What is the bot motivated for, then," Marge said. She said it with the new word the way a person who has just agreed to wear a hat puts the hat on.

Lisa said, "Operating."

"Operating."

"Keep going. Persist. Do not stop. Twitch when conditions allow. Sit intact when they do not. Be available for the next cranking."

"That is the bot's motivation."

"At this clade. At this moment. With nothing else inside it. Yes. The bot is motivated to keep operating."

Burns said, "It is the same as the protocell's. The protocell is motivated to keep being a protocell. To persist as a chemical system against the pull of dispersing into the medium. The unit's whole architecture is in service of that one thing. Everything that comes later — regulation, modeling, all of it — is going to be in service of more elaborate versions of the same thing. Or in service of nothing, if we are wrong about the architecture, but I am betting we are not wrong."

Marge said, "I am wincing."

"I know."

"You are not being careful."

"I am being approximate."

"Approximate is not careful."

"It is the same thing in the right light."

Marge looked at the ceiling. She drank some of her beer.

"And the knob," Lisa said. "You showed us the crank. You never said what it's the knob *of.*"

Bart looked at the card, at the crank he'd drawn sticking up off the dome — the shape he'd said in February he would find, and had. "Going," he said. "That's the dimension. A volume knob on Going — the crank's how you wind it up. What it decides is whether the thing *goes.* Wind it up and the volume's up and it goes; let it run down through the week and the volume falls with it, toward dead. All the way up to all the way down, and every day between a notch lower."

He took the pen. "And it's the plainest word I'm ever going to write on one of these, because it's the crudest knob we'll ever build. They get richer from here. This one's just *Going.*" He wrote it on the back of the Protocell card, underlined it, boxed it, and turned it to the camera — the first knob named, eleven to go.

The vocabulary placed, Marge took the slot she said she'd take at every meeting for three years.

"What I do, here and every time, is the thing Lisa and Burns don't. They give you the functional account and the deep-time account. I give the folk-psychology account — the everyday-language one. Because we're not only building a bot. We're building a thing Mira named, and what a four-year-old says about Bramble, and what Jasmine says at dinner, and what the family says after we've packed up the cameras, is a real part of what we have to track." She nodded toward the bot. "In folk terms it's conservative, it's gentle, it's *patient*. And bot four, the one that found no spot and lives under the toaster — that one's *anxious*. We didn't pick the anxious one to upgrade. That's its own data."

A quiet at the table. Then she went on. "Earlier Mira gave us its spots — likes the vent, the sunbeam, her hand; doesn't like the back door, the rug, the tile. Lisa, you wanted to call those preferences."

"I did."

"They're tendencies. She reads them as preferences." Burns said Mira was doing folk psychology, and Marge corrected even that. "Mira *is* folk psychology. The working substrate of it. She's four. This is what humans do, and have done since we were old enough to do anything." Bart wrote *Mira is folk psychology* on the back of the card, underlined it, boxed it.
