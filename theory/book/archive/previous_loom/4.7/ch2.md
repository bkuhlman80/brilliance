Ch 1

## The Agenda

What followed was Lisa walking the team through her agenda for the meeting. Lisa had drafted the agenda over the course of June and revised it across the second week of July. She had sent it to Burns on a Thursday. He had returned it with no edits, which she had taken to mean either that he agreed with all of it or that he had not read it; either possibility was acceptable to her. She was running the meeting for the first time, and she was going to find out what worked.

She had three content blocks. The first was about the clade — the Protocell, as Cheryl had named it. The team would walk through what a protocell was, what its evolutionary moment had been, what the bots in front of them rendered of that moment. The second block was about the bot itself: the household's three months with the five bots, the question of the wind-up crank as the commission's personality knob, what the next quarter's build would be. The third block was the framework. She did not yet call it the Trellis block. She called it "the bigger picture," with a hand-gesture that meant up.

She turned on the camcorder. The small red LED came on. The flip-out screen showed the room, slightly fish-eyed at the edges, and on it the four labmates plus Jasmine and Mira and Bramble.

"For Cheryl," Lisa said. "She watches the tape."

Nobody addressed the camera. Nobody pretended it wasn't there either. The team had agreed to this in May, after a long conversation in which Bart had been the holdout for forty minutes and had then conceded on the grounds that Cheryl was paying them a great deal of money and they were behaving naturally anyway. Bart looked at the camera once. He nodded at it. He did not nod at it again across the meeting. The camera had been logged in.

Burns took the floor next. He stood up to do it. He had not sat down yet, in fact; he had been standing or crouching for the entire opening, in the way of a man who could not be in a room with the work of the room without orbiting the table at which the work would happen. His hands moved when he talked. Bart, two seats down, watched the hands.

"The thing we are doing," Burns said. "I am going to say it out loud, because this is the first time we are saying it out loud as a team, on tape, with Cheryl watching."

He looked at the camcorder. He looked away from the camcorder.

"We are going to build twelve robots. One per clade. Cheryl has given us the clades. She has not told us what to build and she has not told us what each robot is supposed to teach us. She has told us that each robot must have, in its body somewhere, a knob that controls something about its personality. We are going to figure out what each knob controls. We are going to figure out what each robot's personality is. We are going to figure out, by the end, what personality means."

He paused there.

"That last part," he said, "is the part Cheryl has not asked us to do. She has asked us to deliver the robots. The theory of personality is a thing we are going to build on the side, because we cannot build the robots without it. We are paying for the theory by building the robots. Cheryl is paying for the robots in order to be paid back in the theory. This is, I want to say out loud, the deal as I understand it. If anyone in this room understands it differently they should say so now."

Nobody said anything. Marge said, "I understand it the same way."

"Lisa."

"Same."

"Bart."

"Same."

"Then we are agreed."

He took a sip of his beer. It was an IPA from Fisher's, two blocks away, brought in a growler.

"That's all I have at the open. The rest is in the blocks."

He sat down. He had been standing for the entire opening. The chair felt new under him.

## Trellis

Framework was where the words got set down for good. Bart had been boxing them on the backs of his cards all evening — every time the room reached for one and caught it, he wrote it, underlined it, drew the small box. Now he laid the cards face-up, and they were the night's haul.

"Burns. You left one open at the vent."

He had — he'd reached for the thing the protocell did its work *with* and set his hand back down. He picked the word up now. "Capacity. I've been carrying it a week — read it in a paper I should've read fifteen years ago. The new thing a clade has that the one before it didn't. The protocell's was the capacity to be selected on. Every clade after it adds one."

"And the event — the moment the capacity comes online. We've been fighting about that word." He looked at Marge.

"Innovation," Marge said.

"Innovation."

"Emergent capability."

"Emergent capability."

"Phase transition."

"Phase transition was mine. I'm letting it go." He wrote *breakthrough*, boxed it. "That's the one that lived. We picked the plain word because the others were pretentious and Marge made fun of us. Each clade has a breakthrough."

"Lisa. You left one at the door too — the gravity, the dispositions. Said there was a word in your field and it was wrong for the room."

"Dynamical attractor," Lisa said.

Marge set her beer down. "Can we call them tendencies."

Lisa held the moment with her face neutral, and what she felt — privately, briefly — was the pleasure of being asked to take off a coat she hadn't known she was wearing. "Tendencies. Better than what I had."

"Thank you," Marge said.

Burns laughed the chest-laugh, because there was something cosmically funny to him about a lab choosing the simpler word at the start of a three-year project on the architecture of personality. He laughed early; the room caught up a beat behind. Bart wrote *tendency*, boxed it, and held up two fingers to Burns, who'd been counting.

The last word was the one that fought back.

"The bot is doing something," Lisa said. "When it twitches, when it stays still, when it persists in being wound. There is — and I am going to fish for a word here, because I do not have the right one — there is a *what-the-bot-is-doing-it-for*. A direction. A thing the patterns serve."

Burns said, "Persistence."

"Persistence is the *outcome*. I am asking what is *behind* the persistence."

Bart, who had been turning Bramble in his hand and watching the conversation, said, "Motivation."

There was a small pause.

Marge said, "No."

Bart said, "I know."

"A protocell does not have a motivation."

"Granted."

"There is no intent in this object."

"None. And I still want the word."

Bart set Bramble down. He looked at Marge. He looked at the bot. He looked at the call, which was on the table near Burns's elbow, still in the binding that had been on it for eight months.

"Cheryl gave us a contract that says *personality*. And we are going to spend three years building twelve robots, and the robots are going to be on a continuum from this one, which has nothing inside it, to the one we will build twelve quarters from now, which is going to have an awful lot inside it. And at every clade along the way we are going to be saying, *what is this bot doing? what is it for?* And we have to have a word for the thing it is for, because you cannot talk about personality without talking about what is behind the behavior. We are going to need a word that runs all the way from bacteria to Bach and back." He smiled at Marge. "*Motivation* is the word I am offering. We can fight about whether it fits at Protocell. I think it fits if we let it fit. I think the protobot's *motivation* is to keep being a protobot."

Marge said, "That is anthropomorphizing."

"I know."

"You are going to leak intent into things that do not have intent."

"That's the risk."

"And then we are going to spend three years arguing about whether the bots feel anything."

"We are probably going to spend three years arguing about that anyway."

Marge looked at him. She had been holding her beer the way she held a teacup. She put it down. She looked at the bot. She looked at Lisa.

"Lisa," she said.

"I think it works," Lisa said. "It works the way *tendency* works. The word is a placeholder for a function. We use the word to talk about the function. We do not import the metaphysics."

"You are not going to import the metaphysics."

"I am not."

"Burns is going to import the metaphysics."

"Probably."

"In his speech."

"Possibly."

"In his speech at the close of this meeting."

"Almost certainly."

Burns, who had been listening, said, "I will be careful."

"You will not."

"I will try."

Marge looked at the bot. She held the silence. She picked her beer up again. She held it in tea-posture. She set it down.

"Fine," she said. "I will use it. I am going to wince. But I will use it."

Bart looked at Burns. Burns looked at the camcorder. The red light was on. Burns laughed the chest-laugh.

"What is the bot motivated for, then," Marge said. She said it with the new word the way a person who has just agreed to wear a hat puts the hat on.

Lisa said, "Operating."

"Operating."

"Keep going. Persist. Do not stop. Twitch when conditions allow. Sit intact when they do not. Be available for the next cranking."

"That is the bot's motivation."

"At this clade. At this moment. With nothing else inside it. Yes. The bot is motivated to keep operating."

Burns said, "It is the same as the protocell's. The protocell is motivated to keep being a protocell. To persist as a chemical system against the pull of dispersing into the medium. The unit's whole architecture is in service of that one thing. Everything that comes later — regulation, modeling, all of it — is going to be in service of more elaborate versions of the same thing. Or in service of nothing, if we are wrong about the architecture, but I am betting we are not wrong."

Marge said, "I am wincing."

"I know."

"You are not being careful."

"I am being approximate."

"Approximate is not careful."

"It is the same thing in the right light."

Marge looked at the ceiling. She drank some of her beer.

"And the knob," Lisa said. "You showed us the crank. You never said what it's the knob *of.*"

Bart looked at the card, at the crank he'd drawn sticking up off the dome — the shape he'd said in February he would find, and had. "Going," he said. "That's the dimension. A volume knob on Going — the crank's how you wind it up. What it decides is whether the thing *goes.* Wind it up and the volume's up and it goes; let it run down through the week and the volume falls with it, toward dead. All the way up to all the way down, and every day between a notch lower."

He took the pen. "And it's the plainest word I'm ever going to write on one of these, because it's the crudest knob we'll ever build. They get richer from here. This one's just *Going.*" He wrote it on the back of the Protocell card, underlined it, boxed it, and turned it to the camera — the first knob named, eleven to go.

The vocabulary placed, Marge took the slot she said she'd take at every meeting for three years.

"What I do, here and every time, is the thing Lisa and Burns don't. They give you the functional account and the deep-time account. I give the folk-psychology account — the everyday-language one. Because we're not only building a bot. We're building a thing Mira named, and what a four-year-old says about Bramble, and what Jasmine says at dinner, and what the family says after we've packed up the cameras, is a real part of what we have to track." She nodded toward the bot. "In folk terms it's conservative, it's gentle, it's *patient*. And bot four, the one that found no spot and lives under the toaster — that one's *anxious*. We didn't pick the anxious one to upgrade. That's its own data."

A quiet at the table. Then she went on. "Earlier Mira gave us its spots — likes the vent, the sunbeam, her hand; doesn't like the back door, the rug, the tile. Lisa, you wanted to call those preferences."

"I did."

"They're tendencies. She reads them as preferences." Burns said Mira was doing folk psychology, and Marge corrected even that. "Mira *is* folk psychology. The working substrate of it. She's four. This is what humans do, and have done since we were old enough to do anything." Bart wrote *Mira is folk psychology* on the back of the card, underlined it, boxed it.

Ch 2

## The Effort Knob

Lisa took it.

"Bot side. I'll be quick."

She picked up Bramble — Mira had had Bramble in her lap; she handed it to Lisa without being asked, and Lisa held it in her left palm. She did not pick it up by the dome; she picked it up by the underside, the way Bart had taught her to pick up a protobot back in April. The screen read STOP.

"What this is. The dome is the same dome from April. Inside, now, are two stores where he'd had just one in the spring — a slow high-capacity battery and, beside it, a fast low-capacity supercapacitor. The pairing is new. Also new, there is a microcontroller, an STM32 — Bart picked it — running an FSM with three states. STOP, RUN, TUMBLE. There are four light sensors arrayed around the perimeter, ten degrees off the floor plane each, and they are what Bramble steers by. There are the original heat-transducer-and-actuator pairs from the protocell. There are two thin solar panels along the long sides of the dome, and a small DC-DC converter that takes whatever is coming in — light off the panels, warmth off the transducers, both at once — and charges both stores. There is the wind-up crank, still on, doing what it did at protocell. And there is the new knob, on the opposite side from the crank — the Effort knob."

Jasmine said, "What is an FSM?"

Lisa nodded, "Finite State Machine. Yeah, so — think of the last time you walked down into a subway station and had to go through one of those turnstile things. When you walk up, the turnstile is locked. You push it, it won't let you through."

Bart said, "And then you jump over it."

Mira snickered.

Lisa said, "And then you put your coin in, and the turnstile unlocks. Then you push the bar and pass through. And the bar locks again. Yeah? The turnstile is running a simple algorithm. Coin unlocks. Turning locks. That is an FSM. A small number of states it can be in, and a small set of rules about how to get from one state to another. Bramble's FSM has three states. The turnstile's has two."

Jasmine said, "Got it."

Lisa turned Bramble in her palm.

"The behavior. The seeking first, because it runs the same whatever the knob is set to. The light sensors are the input. If the array reads a coherent gradient — light stronger from one direction than the others — and the stores aren't filling, the FSM transitions to RUN, and the actuators move Bramble toward the light. If the array reads incoherent input — no clear direction — and the stores aren't filling, it transitions to TUMBLE, reorients randomly, re-samples. If the stores are filling — taking in more than he's spending — it transitions to STOP, because he doesn't need to move and shouldn't waste charge. That is the whole seek, and it points at light, always. The warmth the transducers pull off the floor is charge; it is never a destination.

"The Effort knob is a different axis. It does not set where Bramble goes but how he spends getting there — which store discharges. Turned to Effort volume down it draws the slow battery: a low sustained rate, an even crawl it can hold for a long time — aerobic. Turned to Effort volume up it spends the fast supercapacitor in one burst, and then has to sit while the battery refills it before it can spend that way again — anaerobic. Volume down is endurance; volume up is sprint-and-recover. Harvest feeds both stores no matter where the knob sits. The knob governs discharge, not intake."

She paused.

"That's the architecture. There is no plan inside this unit. There is no memory. The FSM has three states; the transition table is short enough to fit on an index card; Bart and I wrote it in May. The behavior the household has been watching for ten weeks is a little table of rules running against the kitchen floor."

She set Bramble down on the table.

"The thing I want to land. Bramble is coupled to the environment now in a way he wasn't last quarter. At protocell he was passive against the gradient — wound, on the warm floor, twitched. Now he *seeks*. He moves toward the light he needs, stops when he has enough, tumbles when the world is not enough. Bramble has — and I am using a piece of common-English vocabulary that I will sharpen later — *directionality*. Last quarter he didn't. Now he does. That is the seek, and it is the headline. The knob rides alongside it, a separate knob: not where Bramble goes, but how he spends getting there."

Burns said, "And the other four bots."

Lisa said, "And the other four bots, they twitch. They cool the floor under them. They run out of ... crank? tension? whatever. They go slack before the next wind-up Sunday. Your household has, at this moment, one unit that navigates and four units that do not."

She looked at the room.

"The contrast is — I want to say this carefully because it is the next thing — the contrast is what makes Bramble legible as a bounded organism. The reason we can tell that Bramble is a *thing* in a way the protobots are not is that Bramble is doing something the protobots are not doing. The protobots are the backdrop against which Bramble stands out; their twitching is what helps us see Bramble's movements as behavior."

## Cybernetics

Lisa pulled her chair closer to the table — she didn't stand for her own segments; her register was the table, not the front of the room — and did the small two-palm rotating thing she did when she was about to think with her hands.

"Motivations. Last quarter we settled on *motivation* for what's behind the tendencies. The protocell's was persistence — keep being — and it's still there; Bramble's still motivated to keep being. The new layer doesn't compete with that, it serves it: Bramble moves toward warmth because moving toward warmth is how he persists. And the new motivation is *coordination* — what arrives when one part of him starts acting on another. The FSM acting on the metabolic layer underneath. One layer of the system acting on the layer below it."

Marge turned her beer a quarter. "Coordination means a lot of things. What does it mean *here.*"

"A layer acting on the layer below. That's all it means here. New at Prokaryote — the protocell didn't have it."

Marge wrote *coordination = layer acting on layer below* in her notepad and did not look up.

"And here's the question I've been carrying about it. I know what that layer *does* — I built it, Bart and I picked the states, he's running it. What I don't know yet is what it *is*, structurally, in the framework we're building." She set both palms flat. "A partial answer — a phrase I've been trying across the quarter. *A stored controller.* A thing whose rules, its behavior given input, are written down somewhere and looked up when the input arrives. The rules don't change while it runs; it doesn't write new ones; it reads them from storage and executes. The protobots don't have one — only their physics, heat in and motion out, nothing in between. Bramble has rules in between, and the rules are stored. Bart and I stored them. He's executing them."

Burns, who had been listening, said, "Lisa. That is what biology is."

Lisa said, "Say more."

Burns said, "Every cell on the planet is running a stored controller. The genome is the storage. The cell is the runtime. The controller is whatever expression program the cell is running. The transition rules — when to make this protein, when to divide, when to gate the sporulation pathway — are stored in the DNA and looked up by the molecular machinery that does the looking up. Bart talked about sporulation an hour ago. Sporulation is a stored controller. The rules for when to sporulate are written in the DNA. The cell reads them when conditions trigger the read. The cell does not invent the rules. The cell executes the rules."

He paused.

"Every cell on the planet is doing this. It is what biology is. It is the substrate that biology runs on. The lineage writes the rules across generations; the cell reads them and runs. The FSM is the same kind of thing. It is just that we, the lab, are the lineage in this case."

Burns said, "Here is the most concrete version of it. One of the things the rules decide is who gets in. The prokaryote's controller builds doors. Real ones — proteins set into the membrane that recognize one specific molecule and haul it in against the crowd, and burn energy to do it. The lac operon Bart walked you through is the controller deciding to build the lactose door when there is lactose worth bringing in."

Jasmine said, "But the protocell already had a membrane. You said so. So what is the new thing — didn't it always keep some things out and let some things in?"

Burns said, "It kept things out by being made of fat. It did not choose. The protocell's selectivity is the shape of the hole. The prokaryote's selectivity is a doorman with a guest list — and the muscle to drag in a guest who would rather drift the other way."

He paused.

"And none of it is free. The world outside is always trying to flatten the cell into it, to make the inside the same as the outside. The cell spends, the whole time it is alive, just to stay different from what surrounds it. The doors are the half you would call cheerful — choosing what to bring in. The rest is paying, every second, to be a separate thing at all."

Bart said, "Protocell's not picky. It's a screen door — whatever fits walks in, and it calls that a membrane. Prokaryote hired a guy."

He paused before the punchline.

"Protocell never learned the word no. That's the whole upgrade. It learned to say no."

Lisa let that sit for a beat. Then she said, "OK. That is the right way to say it. The thing the FSM is, is the thing biology is. I am going to use that. *The FSM is a stored controller. The kind of thing biology is when it routes behavior.*"

Marge said, "It is the program inside."

Lisa looked at her.

Marge said, "I just used that phrase. Five minutes ago. The Blank Slate photoshops out the program inside. The FSM is the program inside. That is what Bramble has that the protocell did not. The protocell had no program inside. Bramble has one. Bramble has the thing the Blank Slate denies people have. Bramble is the place where the program is visible, because we wrote it down and we know which lines it is."

She paused.

"If you think about it, the protocell is the case where the Blank Slate is correct. There is no program inside the protocell. The protocell's behavior really is — at the clade — just its physics responding to the floor."

She paused.

"I am not yet saying the four fallacies map onto the four clades. I am noting that the Blank Slate stops being right at the place where the FSM starts existing. That is one piece."

Lisa wrote *the program inside = stored controller* in her notebook. She underlined it.

"Two more things to name and then a problem to flag."

Lisa turned the card with the schematic over and laid it back down with the drawing facing up. Bart had written notes on the back of the card; Lisa was using the front for what was next. If Bart needed the card, then Bart could wait.

"Last quarter we said the protocell sat in a lineage. The lab and the five sibling protobots. This quarter, the lineage is still there. The four protobots are still siblings of Bramble. They didn't get upgraded but they are still part of the lineage. Bramble is the prokaryote-tier member of a lineage with four protocell-tier members still in it. Lineage is carrying forward as a thing the framework is going to keep tracking."

She paused.

"That's one carry-forward. The new one is the thing I want to flag. Bramble at this clade reads as a *bounded organism* in a way the protobots did not. The four protobots were a small population. This quarter we began to see a unit that navigates and four that don't, and we gave the navigating unit a different ontological status. That status is — I am going to use a field-native word — *organism*. The unit represents an organism. *Organism* names the bounded coordinated work-doing thing. Bramble is like that. The four protobots are not."

She paused again. She looked at Bart.

"So that's two. Lineage from last quarter, and now Organism. I don't have a word for what those two things are members of. They are both — *couplings* is not the right word, because we use *coupling* technically. They are both — the essence of the thing — that's vague. I am going to leave the category-noun open for now. I want to flag that the framework is going to need one. We can come back to it."

Burns said, "File it."

Lisa said, "Filing."

Bart said, "Goo?"

Lisa looked at him.

"The category-noun."

"Bart."

"It's the GOO!"

"Bart."

"I'm filing Goo."

"You're not filing. You're throwing words at the file."

"I'm doing both."

Burns laughed the chest-laugh. He laughed because Bart had said *goo*. He laughed because Lisa had not laughed. He laughed because the room had spent the protocell quarter coming up with words — *capacity*, *motivation*, *Bramble*, *tendency* — and now there was another word the room needed and Bart was throwing something at it the way Bart threw first things at problems. 

Marge said *category noun* under her breath.

Lisa said, "We will come back to it."

She turned to Marge.

"Folk side."

Marge took a beat. She did not stand. She set her beer on the table — tea-posture, then flat — and looked at Bramble.

"The new motivation. Coordination, in folk vocabulary — what the unit is now doing that it wasn't — is the unit *getting itself together*. Jasmine has used the phrase to me on the phone twice this quarter without prompting; the household has been using something like it without the word. The unit is, in the household's experience, no longer just a small twitching thing. The unit is doing something on purpose. Or close enough to on purpose that the household's language has started reaching for words it would have used about a small animal — Bramble *wants*, Bramble *tries*, Bramble *gives up*. None of these are right. All of them are honest."

She paused.

"And the organism part. The bounded thing. In folk vocabulary the household is treating Bramble as a *someone* now in a way they did not treat the protobots. Bramble is the *one* that Mira carries. The four protobots are the *ones* — plural — that live in the kitchen. The grammar of the household has, without the household deciding, made Bramble singular and the four plural. *Someone* is not the word the household uses, exactly, but the grammar is the grammar of someones-and-things. The household is treating Bramble as a thing that has, in the language Burns has been using at the dinner table, *insides*. The four protobots are still things. Bramble is starting to be a someone."

She paused.

"The motivation Lisa named — coordination — is, in the household, the unit *getting itself together*. The new, um, category noun Lisa named — organism, the bounded thing — is, in the household, the unit *being a someone*. The protobots are still not someones. Bramble is starting to be one. I will say more about why that matters at my fallacy section. The point right now is that the folk vocabulary is tracking what Lisa is tracking. It is using different words. The words are not wrong."
