## The Agenda

What followed was Lisa walking the team through her agenda for the meeting. Lisa had drafted the agenda over the course of June and revised it across the second week of July. She had sent it to Burns on a Thursday. He had returned it with no edits, which she had taken to mean either that he agreed with all of it or that he had not read it; either possibility was acceptable to her. She was running the meeting for the first time, and she was going to find out what worked.

She had three content blocks. The first was about the clade — the Protocell, as Cheryl had named it. The team would walk through what a protocell was, what its evolutionary moment had been, what the bots in front of them rendered of that moment. The second block was about the bot itself: the household's three months with the five bots, the question of the wind-up crank as the commission's personality knob, what the next quarter's build would be. The third block was the framework. She did not yet call it the Trellis block. She called it "the bigger picture," with a hand-gesture that meant up.

She turned on the camcorder. The small red LED came on. The flip-out screen showed the room, slightly fish-eyed at the edges, and on it the four labmates plus Jasmine and Mira and Bramble.

"For Cheryl," Lisa said. "She watches the tape."

Nobody addressed the camera. Nobody pretended it wasn't there either. The team had agreed to this in May, after a long conversation in which Bart had been the holdout for forty minutes and had then conceded on the grounds that Cheryl was paying them a great deal of money and they were behaving naturally anyway. Bart looked at the camera once. He nodded at it. He did not nod at it again across the meeting. The camera had been logged in.

Burns took the floor next. He stood up to do it. He had not sat down yet, in fact; he had been standing or crouching for the entire opening, in the way of a man who could not be in a room with the work of the room without orbiting the table at which the work would happen. His hands moved when he talked. Bart, two seats down, watched the hands.

"The thing we are doing," Burns said. "I am going to say it out loud, because this is the first time we are saying it out loud as a team, on tape, with Cheryl watching."

He looked at the camcorder. He looked away from the camcorder.

"We are going to build twelve robots. One per clade. Cheryl has given us the clades. She has not told us what to build and she has not told us what each robot is supposed to teach us. She has told us that each robot must have, in its body somewhere, a knob that controls something about its personality. We are going to figure out what each knob controls. We are going to figure out what each robot's personality is. We are going to figure out, by the end, what personality means."

He paused there.

"That last part," he said, "is the part Cheryl has not asked us to do. She has asked us to deliver the robots. The theory of personality is a thing we are going to build on the side, because we cannot build the robots without it. We are paying for the theory by building the robots. Cheryl is paying for the robots in order to be paid back in the theory. This is, I want to say out loud, the deal as I understand it. If anyone in this room understands it differently they should say so now."

Nobody said anything. Marge said, "I understand it the same way."

"Lisa."

"Same."

"Bart."

"Same."

"Then we are agreed."

He took a sip of his beer. It was an IPA from Fisher's, two blocks away, brought in a growler.

"That's all I have at the open. The rest is in the blocks."

He sat down. He had been standing for the entire opening. The chair felt new under him.

## Trellis

Framework was where the words got set down for good. Bart had been boxing them on the backs of his cards all evening — every time the room reached for one and caught it, he wrote it, underlined it, drew the small box. Now he laid the cards face-up, and they were the night's haul.

"Burns. You left one open at the vent."

He had — he'd reached for the thing the protocell did its work *with* and set his hand back down. He picked the word up now. "Capacity. I've been carrying it a week — read it in a paper I should've read fifteen years ago. The new thing a clade has that the one before it didn't. The protocell's was the capacity to be selected on. Every clade after it adds one."

"And the event — the moment the capacity comes online. We've been fighting about that word." He looked at Marge.

"Innovation," Marge said.

"Innovation."

"Emergent capability."

"Emergent capability."

"Phase transition was mine. I'm letting it go." He wrote *breakthrough*, boxed it. "That's the one that lived. We picked the plain word because the others were pretentious and Marge made fun of us. Each clade has a breakthrough."

"Lisa. You left one at the door too — the gravity, the dispositions. Said there was a word in your field and it was wrong for the room."

"Dynamical attractor," Lisa said.

Marge set her beer down. "Can we call them tendencies."

Lisa held the moment with her face neutral, and what she felt — privately, briefly — was the pleasure of being asked to take off a coat she hadn't known she was wearing. "Tendencies. Better than what I had."

Burns laughed the chest-laugh, because there was something cosmically funny to him about a lab choosing the simpler word at the start of a three-year project on the architecture of personality. He laughed early; the room caught up a beat behind. Bart wrote *tendency*, boxed it, and held up two fingers to Burns, who'd been counting.

The last word was the one that fought back.

"The bot is doing something," Lisa said. "When it twitches, when it stays still, when it persists in being wound. There is — and I am going to fish for a word here, because I do not have the right one — there is a *what-the-bot-is-doing-it-for*. A direction. A thing the patterns serve."

"Persistence is the *outcome*. I am asking what is *behind* the persistence."

Bart, who had been turning Bramble in his hand and watching the conversation, said, "Motivation."

## The Effort Knob

Lisa took it.

"Bot side. I'll be quick."

She picked up Bramble — Mira had had Bramble in her lap; she handed it to Lisa without being asked, and Lisa held it in her left palm. She did not pick it up by the dome; she picked it up by the underside, the way Bart had taught her to pick up a protobot back in April. The screen read STOP.

"What this is. The dome is the same dome from April. Inside, now, are two stores where he'd had just one in the spring — a slow high-capacity battery and, beside it, a fast low-capacity supercapacitor. The pairing is new. Also new, there is a microcontroller, an STM32 — Bart picked it — running an FSM with three states. STOP, RUN, TUMBLE. There are four light sensors arrayed around the perimeter, ten degrees off the floor plane each, and they are what Bramble steers by. There are the original heat-transducer-and-actuator pairs from the protocell. There are two thin solar panels along the long sides of the dome, and a small DC-DC converter that takes whatever is coming in — light off the panels, warmth off the transducers, both at once — and charges both stores. There is the wind-up crank, still on, doing what it did at protocell. And there is the new knob, on the opposite side from the crank — the Effort knob."

Jasmine said, "What is an FSM?"

Lisa nodded, "Finite State Machine. Yeah, so — think of the last time you walked down into a subway station and had to go through one of those turnstile things. When you walk up, the turnstile is locked. You push it, it won't let you through."

Bart said, "And then you jump over it."

Mira snickered.

Lisa said, "And then you put your coin in, and the turnstile unlocks. Then you push the bar and pass through. And the bar locks again. Yeah? The turnstile is running a simple algorithm. Coin unlocks. Turning locks. That is an FSM. A small number of states it can be in, and a small set of rules about how to get from one state to another. Bramble's FSM has three states. The turnstile's has two."

Jasmine said, "Got it."

Lisa turned Bramble in her palm.

"The behavior. The seeking first, because it runs the same whatever the knob is set to. The light sensors are the input. If the array reads a coherent gradient — light stronger from one direction than the others — and the stores aren't filling, the FSM transitions to RUN, and the actuators move Bramble toward the light. If the array reads incoherent input — no clear direction — and the stores aren't filling, it transitions to TUMBLE, reorients randomly, re-samples. If the stores are filling — taking in more than he's spending — it transitions to STOP, because he doesn't need to move and shouldn't waste charge. That is the whole seek, and it points at light, always. The warmth the transducers pull off the floor is charge; it is never a destination.

"The Effort knob is a different axis. It does not set where Bramble goes but how he spends getting there — which store discharges. Turned to Effort volume down it draws the slow battery: a low sustained rate, an even crawl it can hold for a long time — aerobic. Turned to Effort volume up it spends the fast supercapacitor in one burst, and then has to sit while the battery refills it before it can spend that way again — anaerobic. Volume down is endurance; volume up is sprint-and-recover. Harvest feeds both stores no matter where the knob sits. The knob governs discharge, not intake."

She paused.

"That's the architecture. There is no plan inside this unit. There is no memory. The FSM has three states; the transition table is short enough to fit on an index card; Bart and I wrote it in May. The behavior the household has been watching for ten weeks is a little table of rules running against the kitchen floor."

She set Bramble down on the table.

"The thing I want to land. Bramble is coupled to the environment now in a way he wasn't last quarter. At protocell he was passive against the gradient — wound, on the warm floor, twitched. Now he *seeks*. He moves toward the light he needs, stops when he has enough, tumbles when the world is not enough. Bramble has — and I am using a piece of common-English vocabulary that I will sharpen later — *directionality*. Last quarter he didn't. Now he does. That is the seek, and it is the headline. The knob rides alongside it, a separate knob: not where Bramble goes, but how he spends getting there."

Burns said, "And the other four bots."

Lisa said, "And the other four bots, they twitch. They cool the floor under them. They run out of ... crank? tension? whatever. They go slack before the next wind-up Sunday. Your household has, at this moment, one unit that navigates and four units that do not."

She looked at the room.

"The contrast is — I want to say this carefully because it is the next thing — the contrast is what makes Bramble legible as a bounded organism. The reason we can tell that Bramble is a *thing* in a way the protobots are not is that Bramble is doing something the protobots are not doing. The protobots are the backdrop against which Bramble stands out; their twitching is what helps us see Bramble's movements as behavior."

## Cybernetics

Lisa pulled her chair closer to the table — she didn't stand for her own segments; her register was the table, not the front of the room — and did the small two-palm rotating thing she did when she was about to think with her hands.

"Motivations. Last quarter we settled on *motivation* for what's behind the tendencies. The protocell's was persistence — keep being — and it's still there; Bramble's still motivated to keep being. The new layer doesn't compete with that, it serves it: Bramble moves toward warmth because moving toward warmth is how he persists. And the new motivation is *coordination* — what arrives when one part of him starts acting on another. The FSM acting on the metabolic layer underneath. One layer of the system acting on the layer below it."

Marge turned her beer a quarter. "Coordination means a lot of things. What does it mean *here.*"

"A layer acting on the layer below. That's all it means here. New at Prokaryote — the protocell didn't have it."

Marge wrote *coordination = layer acting on layer below* in her notepad and did not look up.

"And here's the question I've been carrying about it. I know what that layer *does* — I built it, Bart and I picked the states, he's running it. What I don't know yet is what it *is*, structurally, in the framework we're building." She set both palms flat. "A partial answer — a phrase I've been trying across the quarter. *A stored controller.* A thing whose rules, its behavior given input, are written down somewhere and looked up when the input arrives. The rules don't change while it runs; it doesn't write new ones; it reads them from storage and executes. The protobots don't have one — only their physics, heat in and motion out, nothing in between. Bramble has rules in between, and the rules are stored. Bart and I stored them. He's executing them."

Burns, who had been listening, said, "Lisa. That is what biology is."

Lisa said, "Say more."

Burns said, "Every cell on the planet is running a stored controller. The genome is the storage. The cell is the runtime. The controller is whatever expression program the cell is running. The transition rules — when to make this protein, when to divide, when to gate the sporulation pathway — are stored in the DNA and looked up by the molecular machinery that does the looking up. Bart talked about sporulation an hour ago. Sporulation is a stored controller. The rules for when to sporulate are written in the DNA. The cell reads them when conditions trigger the read. The cell does not invent the rules. The cell executes the rules."

He paused.

"Every cell on the planet is doing this. It is what biology is. It is the substrate that biology runs on. The lineage writes the rules across generations; the cell reads them and runs. The FSM is the same kind of thing. It is just that we, the lab, are the lineage in this case."

Burns said, "Here is the most concrete version of it. One of the things the rules decide is who gets in. The prokaryote's controller builds doors. Real ones — proteins set into the membrane that recognize one specific molecule and haul it in against the crowd, and burn energy to do it. The lac operon Bart walked you through is the controller deciding to build the lactose door when there is lactose worth bringing in."

Jasmine said, "But the protocell already had a membrane. You said so. So what is the new thing — didn't it always keep some things out and let some things in?"

Burns said, "It kept things out by being made of fat. It did not choose. The protocell's selectivity is the shape of the hole. The prokaryote's selectivity is a doorman with a guest list — and the muscle to drag in a guest who would rather drift the other way."

He paused.

"And none of it is free. The world outside is always trying to flatten the cell into it, to make the inside the same as the outside. The cell spends, the whole time it is alive, just to stay different from what surrounds it. The doors are the half you would call cheerful — choosing what to bring in. The rest is paying, every second, to be a separate thing at all."

Bart said, "Protocell's not picky. It's a screen door — whatever fits walks in, and it calls that a membrane. Prokaryote hired a guy."

He paused before the punchline.

"Protocell never learned the word no. That's the whole upgrade. It learned to say no."

Lisa let that sit for a beat. Then she said, "OK. That is the right way to say it. The thing the FSM is, is the thing biology is. I am going to use that. *The FSM is a stored controller. The kind of thing biology is when it routes behavior.*"

Marge said, "It is the program inside."

Lisa looked at her.

Marge said, "I just used that phrase. Five minutes ago. The Blank Slate photoshops out the program inside. The FSM is the program inside. That is what Bramble has that the protocell did not. The protocell had no program inside. Bramble has one. Bramble has the thing the Blank Slate denies people have. Bramble is the place where the program is visible, because we wrote it down and we know which lines it is."

She paused.

"If you think about it, the protocell is the case where the Blank Slate is correct. There is no program inside the protocell. The protocell's behavior really is — at the clade — just its physics responding to the floor."

She paused.

"I am not yet saying the four fallacies map onto the four clades. I am noting that the Blank Slate stops being right at the place where the FSM starts existing. That is one piece."

Lisa wrote *the program inside = stored controller* in her notebook. She underlined it.

"Two more things to name and then a problem to flag."

Lisa turned the card with the schematic over and laid it back down with the drawing facing up. Bart had written notes on the back of the card; Lisa was using the front for what was next. If Bart needed the card, then Bart could wait.

"Last quarter we said the protocell sat in a lineage. The lab and the five sibling protobots. This quarter, the lineage is still there. The four protobots are still siblings of Bramble. They didn't get upgraded but they are still part of the lineage. Bramble is the prokaryote-tier member of a lineage with four protocell-tier members still in it. Lineage is carrying forward as a thing the framework is going to keep tracking."

She paused.

"That's one carry-forward. The new one is the thing I want to flag. Bramble at this clade reads as a *bounded organism* in a way the protobots did not. The four protobots were a small population. This quarter we began to see a unit that navigates and four that don't, and we gave the navigating unit a different ontological status. That status is — I am going to use a field-native word — *organism*. The unit represents an organism. *Organism* names the bounded coordinated work-doing thing. Bramble is like that. The four protobots are not."

She paused again. She looked at Bart.

"So that's two. Lineage from last quarter, and now Organism. I don't have a word for what those two things are members of. They are both — *couplings* is not the right word, because we use *coupling* technically. They are both — the essence of the thing — that's vague. I am going to leave the category-noun open for now. I want to flag that the framework is going to need one. We can come back to it."

Burns said, "File it."

Lisa said, "Filing."

Bart said, "Goo?"

Lisa looked at him.

"The category-noun."

"Bart."

"It's the GOO!"

"Bart."

"I'm filing Goo."

"You're not filing. You're throwing words at the file."

"I'm doing both."

Burns laughed the chest-laugh. He laughed because Bart had said *goo*. He laughed because Lisa had not laughed. He laughed because the room had spent the protocell quarter coming up with words — *capacity*, *motivation*, *Bramble*, *tendency* — and now there was another word the room needed and Bart was throwing something at it the way Bart threw first things at problems. 

Marge said *category noun* under her breath.

Lisa said, "We will come back to it."

She turned to Marge.

"Folk side."

Marge took a beat. She did not stand. She set her beer on the table — tea-posture, then flat — and looked at Bramble.

"The new motivation. Coordination, in folk vocabulary — what the unit is now doing that it wasn't — is the unit *getting itself together*. Jasmine has used the phrase to me on the phone twice this quarter without prompting; the household has been using something like it without the word. The unit is, in the household's experience, no longer just a small twitching thing. The unit is doing something on purpose. Or close enough to on purpose that the household's language has started reaching for words it would have used about a small animal — Bramble *wants*, Bramble *tries*, Bramble *gives up*. None of these are right. All of them are honest."

She paused.

"And the organism part. The bounded thing. In folk vocabulary the household is treating Bramble as a *someone* now in a way they did not treat the protobots. Bramble is the *one* that Mira carries. The four protobots are the *ones* — plural — that live in the kitchen. The grammar of the household has, without the household deciding, made Bramble singular and the four plural. *Someone* is not the word the household uses, exactly, but the grammar is the grammar of someones-and-things. The household is treating Bramble as a thing that has, in the language Burns has been using at the dinner table, *insides*. The four protobots are still things. Bramble is starting to be a someone."

She paused.

"The motivation Lisa named — coordination — is, in the household, the unit *getting itself together*. The new, um, category noun Lisa named — organism, the bounded thing — is, in the household, the unit *being a someone*. The protobots are still not someones. Bramble is starting to be one. I will say more about why that matters at my fallacy section. The point right now is that the folk vocabulary is tracking what Lisa is tracking. It is using different words. The words are not wrong."

## Inside

Lisa opened her phone and set the stopwatch face up on the table next to the jar.

"Camera's on. Q3. Same order as the door. I added a line at the end — next-quarter logistics gets its own two minutes instead of riding out the door with everybody's coats. Burns, you've got twenty for deep time. We are not relitigating the twenty."

"I have made my peace with the twenty."

"You looked like you were going to relitigate the twenty."

"I wouldn't dare."

"Good," Lisa said, and started the watch.

"The goal this quarter," he said, "was to give Bramble an inside. I mean that as exactly as I can mean it. Up to now we have built a thing that holds a course — that senses, compares, acts, keeps itself going against a world that is mostly trying to even it back out into the world. That's a regulator. That's what Bramble was last quarter. What we wanted to find out this quarter is what changes when the thing we built stops being only a process and starts being a *place* — when it has a defended interior with its own conditions, an inside that is kept different from the outside on purpose, and a memory that the inside carries with it from one day to the next so that the inside has a history."

He reached over, without quite looking, and picked up Mira's juice box, which she had set on the table, and turned it in his fingers a half-turn and then another half-turn, reading the seams of it, and set it back down a few inches from where it had been. Across the table Bart's hands, which had been moving, went still. Nobody said anything. The narrator of all this, watching it later on the tape, would clock the half-second and let it pass, because it was the kind of thing that is only a thing if you say it is one, and at the table it was not one. Mira reached over and took her juice box back and moved it to where she wanted it.

"Inside," Mira said, to the juice box, and drank from it.

"Inside," Burns agreed, and sat down, and gave the floor to Bart and the cards.

## The Relay 

Jasmine glanced down the table then, at Lisa, with the particular attention she gave to bodies, which was different from the attention everyone else in the room gave to anything.

"You've got the watch on," she said. "You been getting out, in this fog? Shoes on or off lately?"

"On," Lisa said. "Most mornings. The fog doesn't bother me — it's just colder air, you dress for it. I did the Living Room trail before I came in today. Frost on the brown grass."

"How far."

"Far enough," Lisa said, which was Lisa for a number she didn't feel like producing, and Jasmine, who knew the difference between a person who ran and a person who *ran,* let it go and filed it, the way you file a thing about someone you've decided to keep an eye on. It went nowhere. It was just a thing Lisa did, that the room half-knew about and never asked after, and tonight Jasmine had asked after it, and that was all.

Marge was back and sitting down.

"OK," Lisa said. "The click. Jasmine, you flagged the click — say the thing you said to me at drop-off."

"It clicks more than the old one," Jasmine said. "More, and longer. The old one would tick a couple of times and you'd know it had changed its mind about something. This one — it's a whole run of them now. *Clack-clack-clack-clack.* Like it's counting something out. I've been reading the thing by its clicking for the better part of a year, the way you read a kettle, and the clicking's changed, and I want to know what makes it."

"That," Bart said, and put down his beer, and leaned in, because this was his, "is the best question anybody's asked me all quarter, and I'm going to take the long way around it.

"A relay is a switch that's thrown by a magnet instead of by a finger. You've got a little coil of wire, and an iron arm sitting next to it called an armature, and when a current runs through the coil it becomes a magnet and it *yanks* the arm — snaps it over — and the arm, when it snaps over, closes a second circuit. That's it. That's the whole device. Current in, magnet on, arm snaps, next circuit closed. And the snap of that arm hitting its stop is a sound. It's a click. One relay, one click.

"Now chain them. Put a row of them in a bank, and wire it so the circuit the first one closes is the coil of the second one. First arm snaps — *click* — and that closes the circuit that energizes the second coil, so the second arm snaps — *click* — which closes the third, *click,* and the fourth, *click,* and you've got a signal walking down the bank, one relay handing to the next, each switch throwing the one after it. That walk, that handoff going down the line — *clack-clack-clack-clack* — that's what Jasmine's hearing. The unit's bigger now, the parts that need to talk to each other are spread out across the whole chassis instead of crammed into one little body, so there's more bank to walk, and the walk takes longer, and you hear more of it. The click got longer because the thing got bigger and the message has farther to go, one handoff at a time."

"And here's the part that makes it the capacity and not just a noise," he said. "That same machinery does two completely different jobs with the same trick. One job is *signal* — getting a decision from this end of the unit to that end. The other job is *cargo* — physically moving the stuff. The crumbs it vacuums up don't teleport to the storage and they don't teleport from storage out to the dock; they get *moved,* step by step, handed along, the same kind of relay logic but with material instead of just a message. One system. It carries the word and it carries the thing. Signal and freight on the same line of handoffs. That's *Intracellular Transport.* That's capacity number one off the card. The household's been listening to it for a year and calling it the click."

"Say why it's built that way and not some faster way," Burns said. "That's the part I want on the tape."

"Go ahead, that one's yours."

"It's built that way," Burns said, "because the cell got too big for the easy answer. In a small cell you don't need delivery — things are close enough that they just drift to where they're needed, bumping around, and that's fast enough because nothing's far from anything. But once you're big — once you've got all these rooms — drift is hopeless. A molecule could wander for an hour and never get across. So a big cell can't rely on things finding their own way. It builds tracks, and it walks the cargo down them — the walk Bart did. It's slower than you'd want. You can *hear* that it's slow — that's the whole length of the clicking. But it's the answer a single cell has, because a single cell has no — " and he caught himself, lightly, at the edge of a word he was not going to say this quarter, and went around it — "a single cell has nothing built specially for sending fast signals long distances. There's no dedicated wire. Getting something from here to there across a big inside, whether it's a message or a parcel, is *transport.* It's carrying. It's the cargo getting walked. And carrying takes time, and the time is real, and you can hear it. That's not a flaw in the unit. That's what being this big costs you, and the cost is paid in those clicks."

The clicking, as if on cue, did not oblige — Bramble sat quiet on the table.

## Organized

"Trellis block," Lisa said. She did not stand. She talked about the cell as a thing that did jobs, and she kept her own hand near the camcorder without touching it, the director's hand on the record.

"Three motivations live at this clade," Lisa said. "Two of them you know. *Persistence* — keep going, keep the difference between you and the world from washing out; that's been there since the start, that's the oldest one. *Coordination* — get the parts to act as one part; that was last quarter's, that's why the four out in the household became four corners of one body instead of four loose things. "What Interiority parameterizes — what it's the knob-word for — is two things that arrive together. Second, a persistent internal representation — the unit now carries, from one day to the next, a structured trace of what it's done, a stored picture shaped by its own history, that it can act on. Marge set her beer down and held it down with one hand on the table beside it, which was as close as Marge came to raising a hand.

## Memory

"My block," Lisa said. "The board."

She picked up her drink and set it down again a half-inch to the left, squaring it to the edge of the table, which was a thing she did, and which the team had stopped seeing, and which the narrator on the tape would come to read as the thing Lisa did with her hands when the thing she was about to say mattered to her more than she wanted it to.

"The headline of the whole build this quarter is on a board the size of a deck of cards, up top where any hand in the house can reach it. Four slots. Knobs one, two, three are seated. Slot four's empty. And I want to be careful about what the board *is,* because it's easy to hear 'memory' and picture a drawer you put things in and take them back out unchanged. That's not it. The board is the first part of anything we've built that *remembers* — and by remembers I mean it carries, from one day to the next, a representation that's been shaped by what Bramble has done. Run the kitchen a hundred times and the board isn't storing a hundred recordings of the kitchen; it's storing a *picture* of its own doing that the hundred runs have worn into a particular shape, thicker where the motions have run often, faint where they haven't. The picture is the residue of the doing. It's the part of Bramble that builds and holds an account of what it's been up to. Before this, Bramble acted and the acting left no trace; the next day it started fresh. Now the inside has a history, and the history is on the board, and Bramble acts out of it."

"And I want to be just as careful about how the picture gets *made,* because this is the part the household will narrate wrong every time. Each cycle Bramble reads what it can sense in the moment — the slope of the floor, the warm side and the cold, the gap ahead, how much charge it's holding — and it runs a movement. The only thing the board ever writes is that pairing: what was sensed, what was done. It doesn't file it as a snapshot. It nudges a weight, so the move it just made comes up a little readier the next time those same readings do. Do that across enough cycles and the weights have worn into the picture — deep along the sense-and-move it's run most, faint where it's barely gone. That's the whole of what's on the board. And the thing to see is what is *not* in there: there is no *where.* No floorplan, no coordinates, no marker that says Bramble is *here* or the bowl is *there.* You couldn't ask the board for a location, because it holds none — only what-was-felt-then-done, sorted by how often. So Bramble doesn't know where he is, and he doesn't know where anything is. It senses the present, it leans into whatever moves this kind of present has worn deep, and that's all it's doing. When you watch it round the same corner to the same spot three mornings running and you're *certain* it knows that spot — the knowing is *yours.* You assembled the map, in your own head, out of watching it be regular. Bramble kept no map to be regular from. The watchers have the map; Bramble has a body, a *now,* and a worn record of moving from one condition into the next."

"There's a theorem under this," she said, "and I'll name it because it's the spine of the whole framework and I'd rather say it once, properly, than have it leak out sideways. Conant and Ashby. Nineteen-seventy. *Every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system.* If a thing is going to keep something under control — keep a course, keep itself alive against a world — then somewhere inside it there has to be a model of the thing it's controlling, because you cannot reliably steer what you don't, in some form, represent. Now: last quarter, Bramble had a model. It was a thin one — a fixed set of rules, sized for a simple world, hard-wired by us, the same on day one as on day three hundred. A stored controller, I called it last quarter."

"This model is *richer,* and here's the difference that matters: Bramble carries it and reshapes it by use. We didn't hard-code the picture. We gave it the machinery to build the picture and let the doing fill it in. The regulator got a better model of what it regulates, and it got it the way you'd actually want — by living."

Bart, who had been quiet through this, said, "That's the thing from school. Isn't it. The board's running the thing you've been carrying around since — what, undergrad."

"Since undergrad," Lisa said.

"Does it have a *name,*" Bart said, "or are we still calling it the Statistical Abomination, or was it the Random Forest Troll."

Lisa, who named things for other people all day long, who had named the agenda items and the blocks and the cadence and had let other people name her, was for a second visibly caught out at being asked to name the one that was hers. Her hands, which were never not doing something, did nothing. She looked at the board up top — she could not see what it held, but she looked where it was.

"It's an evolutionary algorithm," she said. "That's all it is, technically. It runs a population of little candidate strategies, scores them, keeps what works, varies it, runs it again. Selection, in a box. I've been pointing at it since I was twenty. And this is the first time it's been —" she stopped, and started over, more plainly than she usually allowed herself, "— this is the first time it's been *running in something,* out in a house, doing the kitchen, instead of running in a simulation on my machine where the only world it ever had was a world I'd typed. The build caught up to the place I've been pointing. That's — " she did the thing with the glass again, a quarter-inch, " — that's a strange thing to watch happen."

"The name, though," Bart said again, gently. They went back further than the lab, the two of them — back to school, before any of this — and he asked it the way you ask something of an old friend whose work you've watched take shape for years.

"Evolutionary Algorithm," she said. "Evo-Algo, if you have to say it fast."

"Evo-Algo," Bart said, trying it. "I like it. Sounds like a book."

"It's not a book. It's a program. But fine."

She recovered the room by reaching for the precise thing.

"And I want one sentence on the record exactly as I say it, because the whole rest of the build hinges on the difference and I don't want it blurred even once. *It remembers; it does not yet learn from outcomes.* Say that back to yourselves the next time you watch it run the kitchen. It has a memory now — a picture shaped by what it's done. What it does *not* have, yet, is the ability to change its picture because something turned out well or badly. It isn't keeping score against results and adjusting. The groove gets deeper because the groove gets used, not because the groove got *rewarded.* That second thing — learning from outcomes — is a later clade's headline, and if you catch yourself describing the algorithm as 'learning what works,' stop, and check whether you mean 'wearing in what it repeats,' because right now it's only ever the second one. It remembers. It does not yet learn from rewards."

"And the motivation the board parameterizes," she said, "is the one Bart seated for us at the top: *Interiority.* The board is what makes the inside an inside worth the word. A defended boundary with nothing kept behind it is just a wall. A defended boundary with a history kept behind it — a picture the thing carries of its own life — that's an interior. The board is where the interior lives. That's my block."

"Mira," Mira said, and turned the paper around, and on it, in the enormous uneven capitals of a person who has just learned that she is a word, was M I R A. "That's me. That's me wherever I write it."

"Signature," she said. "Oh — *signature.* Lisa. We never named the signature."

## Two Animals

Burns gave the dead animal the one thing it still lacked, which was a world to have all those new powers in.

"Warm sea, full of air, full of *other animals* now," he said. "Crowded. Things eating things. An animal in that water has decisions the slime never had — where to be, when to move, when to lie low." Brief, for him, and he left it there.

There were two animals in that room. One had been dead six hundred million years and lived now on Bart's card and in the weather behind Burns's shut eyes — the jellyfish, the hydra, the whole drowned bestiary, conjured and set down and conjured again. The other was a vacuum cleaner on the table the five of them were teaching, a rung at a time, to be the first one. For a year I had watched the meeting carry both and braced for the holding to slip — for someone to say *Bramble* and mean the hydra, and the double vision to come down. It raised the dead one all the way up first — named it, weathered it, handed it its powers and its new decisions — and only then turned to the table, to ask what of all that the machine had actually been built to do. ## Gone Quiet

He'd taken his wins quiet and handed the best of them across the table. What came next was the machine itself, which was the one subject in that room Bart never lost his feet on.

He set the dome in the middle of the table — the same one that had run the house all fall — and put a hand on it. "What it *is* now. Three things went in this quarter, and every one of them's a first."

## Excitability

"Trellis block," Lisa said. "Motivations — three carried, one new. I'm not drawing anything yet" — she capped the marker she'd uncapped from habit — "the picture comes later, at the board. Persistence, Coordination, Interiority, the three we know. And the new one. Excitability."

"In Bramble, here's what that buys. It can answer with its *whole self,* at once, fast, and the answer folds in what just happened to it a second ago, a minute ago. One signal, whole body, milliseconds, history baked in. That's the motivation. Everything Bramble does now, it can do all at once."

"Two signatures. Host — carried; Bramble being a home for everything nested inside it, the older Brambles riding along as parts. And a new one. When a thing's got a fast whole-body answer and you let it run that answer over and over, the good ones stick — Bramble builds a stash of ready-made moves, coordinated patterns it fires without working them out fresh each time. We're calling it the Reflex Repertoire."

"A repertoire's an honest word," Marge said, "because it's honest about what it isn't. A repertoire is the set of things the machine can already do — not deciding, not choosing. A singer who knows forty songs and gives you whichever one the room calls for. No thought in it. Just the having of the songs, ready to hand." And the narrator thought that Marge was the only one at that table who would not say the name — *the machine,* never *Bramble* — while the others said *Bramble* the way you say a thing you've decided to be fond of, and that the gap between those two words was, more or less, the thing they voted on every quarter.

And Bart, who'd been waiting with his green pint for *repertoire* to land, leaned in. "Strip it down. What a reflex repertoire even *is.* A pattern that's *coordinated,* whole body in on it. *Excitable,* it fires. *Stored,* it sticks around. *Revisable,* a bad one fades, a good one sharpens. Four words. Hold them."

"Here it comes," Lisa said.

"Back to my voltage worms. Levin's pattern memory — the body-shape a flatworm holds and rebuilds and defends, the thing that grows back two heads. What *is* it? A pattern, held across the worm's cells, coordinated by their electrical coupling, excitable because it lives in voltage, stored because the worm keeps it, revisable because you change the voltage and it builds a new shape. *Coordinated, excitable, stored, revisable.* Same four words. The reflex repertoire in our machine is the identical trick — just running in a nerve net instead of raw voltage, pointed at what a body *does* instead of what a body *grows.* The worm runs it to build itself. Bramble runs it to behave. Same move, two floors of one building — growing downstairs, doing up. The nerve didn't invent the reflex. It inherited it from the voltage layer and aimed it at action."

He sat back, still lit. Nobody said anything for a second.

"That's actually good," Marge admitted.

"I have my moments."

## The Cycle

Lisa got up. Not to the camcorder — to the whiteboard, which was how you knew it was the part of the night she'd built toward. She uncapped the marker, drew four boxes in a ring, and exposed a thing she had not before: why there were four slots on her board.

"A year describing these without naming them, and tonight there are four, and that turns out to matter." She tapped the first box. "Effector. The part that acts — does the thing. At the bottom that's just the protocell spending itself to stay different from the pond. Persistence." Second box. "Regulator. Holds a setpoint, corrects back when the world knocks it off. Coordination — the prokaryote getting two kickers to pull as one." Third. "Modeler. Carries a picture of the world and itself, runs off the picture instead of the raw input. The eukaryote's interior, the board that remembers. Interiority." She wrote the fourth name and underlined it once. "And this quarter — Reviser. Changes the picture and the setpoints when they stop paying. Excitability."

She drew the arrows around the ring, each box feeding the next and the last feeding back into the first, and stepped off it.

"That's the loop. And the fourth box is the one that can bite you, so I'll say it once and move on: a part that revises the plan can revise it *wrong.* That's not a bug we'll fix later — it's the cost of having the part at all. The thing Jasmine watched this quarter, where it leaves the doorway it wants to stand in because the gauge says feed — that's this box, working. Two pulls, and it picks. Some night it picks wrong, leaves when it should've stayed, and nothing'll be broken when it does. That's just the tax on the fourth box." She capped the marker. "But it's in. Every box has a flag now. The board's full."

"One more, and it's half-built, so take it that way." She put one palm flat and one vertical and rotated them against each other, which was how she thought. "It's been bugging me that we keep saying *added a layer,* like they stack flat, pancakes. They don't. Each one runs *on* the one under it — the reviser doesn't sit on top of the modeler ignoring it, it reaches down and works the modeler and hands the result up. Signal goes down the stack and back up. It's a relay. A thing carrying a thing forward to the next thing that carries it."

"A relay from what to what?" Jasmine said. "You lost me."

"Okay. Good." Lisa drew a bracket around all four boxes. "These four — protocell, prokaryote, eukaryote, this. I think they're one *kind* of thing. One run at the problem. And I think next year is going to be different from this year in a way that's bigger than how these four differ from each other. Next year we get to the ones with a brain — Bilaterian, then Vertebrate, then Mammal, then Primate. And getting to a brain that runs from a center isn't one more capacity stacked on. It's a phase change. A step-function. The ones with brains are a whole separate line of descent. That's the word I keep grabbing for and can't put down — *line.* A line you could draw, a line with these four on it and not the other eight. This year we built an organism line. Next year we build a brain line. I might be wrong. I wanted it in the room that I don't think next year is more of this. I think it's the next thing."

Burns had his eyes half-closed, the deep-time face. "I think I see it," he said. "Having a brain means having a new way of inheriting."

"Sure," Bart said, "but isn't that just bioelectrics? Eumetazoa's already got bioelectrics. Cells already talk in voltage, already hold a pattern, already revise it — that's the whole thing I was on about earlier. So what's the brain doing that the body wasn't already doing? What would Levin say to a brain *line?*"

"It's more than bioelectrics," Lisa said.

"It's more than bioelectrics." It was Marge, and the room turned, because Marge did not usually back Lisa's framework — she guarded the door it kept trying to walk through. "It's moving past reflexes. Past the hand you were dealt, into something that can get out from under it." She heard herself say it, the narrator thought, a half-second after she'd said it, and looked at the four boxes on the wall like they'd been moved while she wasn't watching.

And there it was, and gone: the genetic line had come full circle that night — four boxes, the loop closed, four slots full, the first complete turn of anything they'd built — and it stood up on the wall in marker, and the two people in the room best equipped to see what they'd built were looking right at it. One of them saw a diagram. The other saw four mistakes people make. Neither of them saw that they were the same four.

Ch 5

## The Four Corners

"Steering's no good without something to steer by," Lisa said. "You don't aim at a thing unless the thing's good or bad to you. So this clade reads every situation along two axes." She crossed the flat palm against the standing one and turned them. "One is valence — good or bad, does this feed me or cost me. The other is arousal — how worked-up, move or hold still. Cross them and you get four corners. Good and aroused: stay and work it, this spot's paying — approach. Bad and aroused: get out, fast — escape. Good and quiet: full, settle, sleep. Bad and quiet: shut down. That's affect, nothing more to it: two numbers, valence and arousal, and where they land is which corner the animal's in, and which corner it's in determines which way it steers."

"In an animal it runs on chemicals," she said. "The monoamines — dopamine, serotonin, the octopamine family. A bilaterian invention; they turn up in the stem right alongside the steering, which is probably not a coincidence."

Bart liked that immediately, because it was a wiring diagram. "So it's a lookup table. One chemical, one corner. Let me guess. Dopamine's the stay-and-work-it one, serotonin's a corner, there's a get-out one — four moods, a few chemicals, solder each to its corner."

"She's right, and the animals are what make her right," Burns said, pleased, because being made right by the animals was his favorite way of being right. "Take serotonin. In a leech, serotonin is the get-out chemical — dose the leech and it swims for its life, away, the bad-and-aroused corner. In a worm, same molecule, atom for atom, serotonin does the opposite: settle, dwell, quit eating, the full-and-quiet corner. Same chemical, opposite corners of the same square, and which one you get depends entirely on which animal you're standing inside." He turned his beer a half-turn, reading the seam of it. "So there's no honest way to wire the corners to their chemistry. But for Bilateriance each corner keeps showing up, regardless of which chemical runs it."

"Which is why we name the job, not the stuff it's made of." And here, the narrator thought, was the part he'd actually meant to say, arriving three sentences late the way the real thing usually did. "Approach. Escape. Settle. Shut down. The job travels from a worm to a leech to a person at this table. The wiring's local, always."

## Impetus

"Trellis block," Lisa said, and didn't reach for the marker yet, because the naming comes before the drawing. "Motivations — three carried, one new. I'm not drawing anything yet; the picture comes at the board. The three you know ride up from the genetic line — Coordination, Interiority, Excitability, same as they were, doing their jobs underneath. And the new one." She let it sit the way she let the real ones sit. "Impetus. The Effector. It's the engine under all of it: steering only earns its keep if the animal actually ventures, and a thing that points itself and won't go forward gets nothing for the architecture. Impetus is the go-forward."

"It sits where Persistence used to sit—" and she paused, half a beat, the way she paused holding a thing for later "—which is the whole of what we have to get to at the board, so I'll leave it there."

"And one new signature. Temperament. It's what you get when the go-forward pushes against the inside — Impetus against Interiority, the picture the animal carries of itself and its world. Couple the two and you get the animal's standing tilt: how bold it runs at rest, before anything's happened to it. Its baseline weather."

"There's a worm experiment for that one too," Burns said, glad to be back among the worms. "Lay a strip of copper across the middle of the dish — worms hate copper, it's a wall to them — and put something they want on the far side, a smell worth crossing for. If the copper is a thin strip, they all cross. A thick strip, none of them cross. Set it right in the middle, not too thick, not too thin, and half the dish crosses and half stays put."

He leaned in, too far, the way he did. "That's done and seen. But here's what I'd bet the house on, and I don't think it's ever been watched the way I want it watched: track the same animals, run after run. I say the half that crosses is the same half. Not the dice coming up different each time — the same worms crossing and the same worms holding back, a standing difference in how much wall a wanting is worth to them. Bold worms and shy worms. Three hundred and two neurons apiece, the same genes in every one, and a temperament you could call by name if you watched it long enough."

"Temperament I'll keep. That one's mine before it's yours — the oldest true thing in my whole field. The part of a creature that's there before the history is, the tilt it came in with. A bold baby and a shy baby in the same crib, and you knew before either could talk." She turned the mug a quarter. "You can build a great deal on top of a temperament. You cannot talk one out of it. I've tried, professionally, and lost."

## Pick Two

Lisa let Marge have the word, then got up and took the marker — the drawing she'd held off on while she named. But instead of starting she drew three overlapping circles in the corner of the board and labeled them, badly: GOOD. FAST. CHEAP.

"That's my venn diagram," Burns said.

"That's yours. You drew it a few weeks ago to explain to Bart why he couldn't have all three of something, and I haven't been able to stop looking at it since." She tapped the little center where the three met. "Three circles, and the more you want at once, the less there is to have. Pick two. Almost nothing lives in the middle."

"I've had a picture since last spring I couldn't get to sit still — the four jobs the board runs on. It kept wanting to be a list, and the list kept lying, because the four jobs aren't a list. They overlap." She wiped the corner and started again, large, center of the board: four circles crossing each other the way Burns's three had, labeling each as she went. Effector. Regulator. Modeler. Reviser. "Your diagram with one more circle. Same rule — the more of them a thing needs at once, the rarer it is, and the more particular." It looked botanical, like the top of a flower or a wild berry. 

"One circle by itself is a motivation. One drive, the simplest thing on the board." She touched each rim in turn. "Impetus goes here, at the Effector. Coordination. Interiority. Excitability. Four circles, four jobs. The floor."

She set the marker down, which for Lisa was punctuation. "Now. Every quarter Bart stands up and gives you tricks of the clade. The capacities. Four of them, every time, off a card, like a list. I want to show you they were never a list."

She picked the marker up and shaded one of the places where three circles crossed and the fourth didn't reach. "A trick lives here — three circles deep, one left out. Each of Bart's capacities is one of these. And here's the part that does the work: the circle each one leaves out is exactly the thing that trick can't do."

She shaded the one for the learning. "This quarter we have associative learning here. Three circles — leaves out the Modeler. So it learns that the dark is bad and never why. The missing circle is the missing why, and it's not a flaw in the trick. It is the trick. The diagram says so before anybody opens their mouth."

She moved the marker to another. "Steering — leaves out the Regulator. A goal and no governor. It goes at every breakable in Jasmine's kitchen."

She let that sit a beat, then dropped a floor, to the slivers where only two circles crossed. "Two circles now, not three. And these come in two kinds, which took me a year to see." She ran the marker along the overlaps between neighbors in the ring. "Where two jobs that sit *next to each other* cross — Effector and Regulator, Regulator and Modeler, on around — you get a tendency. That's where your knobs live, one to a seam. The knobs all sit between jobs that are already neighbors."

"But two of these crossings don't join neighbors." She drew the marker straight across the flower, the long way, from the Effector rim clear over to the Modeler. "Opposite jobs. The two that sit *farthest apart,* reaching past the whole middle of the thing to touch at all. And what lives on a crossing like that isn't a knob — it's a signature." She shaded where Impetus and Interiority closed over each other along that diagonal, and left the dead center of it blank. "The go-forward, stretched all the way across to the picture the animal carries of itself. The two most distant drives in the machine, and the standing thing you get where they finally meet." She wrote it on the diagonal, out past the center she hadn't touched. *Temperament.* "Marge's word. On the board where it turns out to belong — out where the two jobs that have the least to do with each other have to reach the farthest to make one."

"It's a nice picture," Bart said, in the voice that meant the other thing was coming. "Does it change one wire?"

"Not one. It changes what you build next. You've got a trick that can't do something — and now you read straight off the board which circle it's missing, and that's the thing you add. You were guessing what to build next. Now you're not."

"As long as we're clear what it's a map of," Marge said. "It's a map of jobs. What the thing does, and where the doings cross. It is not a map of anybody. There is no one in the middle of those circles."

"No. There's no one in the middle." Lisa looked at the middle, where she hadn't written anything yet. "But there's something in the middle, and I was saving it." She shaded the last region — the smallest on the board, the stem of the wild berry, the sliver where all four circles finally closed over one another at once. "All four jobs at the same time. Which is why almost nothing qualifies to live there, and why it took the entire history of animals to make one thing that does." She wrote it in. Attention. "This quarter's breakthrough sits dead center. It's the only thing on the board that needs the whole board. Attention is the animal weighing its possible moves against each other and letting valence — the good-or-bad — pick which one wins. Steering is the movement you see when it does; attention is the selection underneath it."

Burns was looking at the sliver like it was something pressed in shale. "Half a billion years," he said, mostly to himself, "for anything to live in that gap." Lisa let him have it, because it was true, and because they were his circles.

"I want it on the cards," Bart said — which was how the room learned he'd come all the way around. "The old ones too. I'll redo them in this."

"From the next one on, every card carries it." She capped the marker. And then she didn't put it down, because there was one circle she wasn't finished with. "Two new words went up tonight. Temperament you can file with the signatures — a name for a thing that was always going to be there once we had the two circles to cross." She touched Impetus, at the Effector. "But this one's new in a way none of the others have ever been new. That's the last thing I've got for you."

## Goo

She left her finger on the Effector. "Those other three jobs — Regulator, Modeler, Reviser — you'll watch them swap over the next three quarters, and every time it'll feel like a big night and it won't be one. The thing just gets better at what it already is. Finer. Same animal. But this circle is the one selection actually leans on. Change this one and you haven't made the thing better at what it was. You've changed what it's for.

"And I didn't do it by accident, so don't let it feel like one. The genes' first knob — I didn't carry it across. Left it running on the old machine, put a new kind of knob in its slot. The boundary isn't a figure of speech. It's in the hardware, in which slot got which knob."

"Say what changed," Marge said. "Plainly."

"What got selected changed. In nature, lineages are picked on exactly one thing: who left more copies of itself behind. That's the only scoreboard the genes ever read. More of you tomorrow than today. So Persistence is what counts." She moved off the Effector. "Impetus does not win the Persistence game. Impetus wins a different one — whether the thing steers. Whether it goes forward, on purpose, toward the good and off the bad, under a signal that tells it which is which. Not who had the most children. Who pointed themselves at the right thing and went."

Jasmine had been quiet the whole framework. "So the genes stop counting," she said. "It's the brain's turn now. The body did its part."

"No." Lisa said it fast, because it was the thing everyone hears and it's the thing that's wrong. "This is the part I have to get right or I've told you a lie. The old board did not switch off. It is still running. Every one of these brained animals still has genes, still has to eat and breed and not die, still gets picked on that, exactly like before. Nothing about the body stopped mattering." She drew a second line under the first, a second tally beside the first. "There are just two games now. The old one — make more of yourself — keeps score underneath, every single day. And a new one got hung up next to it: steer well, under feeling. The line didn't trade one game for the other. It hung a second score. Checkers and chess."

Jasmine took that, and the narrator watched her take it — the one in the room who'd have noticed first if the body were being written off, told the body was still on the books.

"And the two games keep score on different stuff. Which is the actual thing I'm reaching for, and I need a word for the stuff, and the word is going to be—"

"Substrate," Burns offered.

"—substrate. Yeah."

"No," Bart said.

"Bart."

"No. Nobody at this table has ever once said substrate about a thing they could hold. We are not starting tonight."

"Then what."

"Goo."

There was a silence in which everyone understood that this was now the word, permanently, against the better angels of their nature. "Goo," Lisa said, with the flatness of a woman filing a loss. "Fine. The genetic line piles up one kind of goo. The old goo — the body's goo. Order squeezed out of the plain fact that the world runs downhill and a living thing is a thing that runs downhill on purpose, cleverly, and banks the difference. Four clades of genes stacking that up. The new line piles up a different goo entirely. Not order-against-downhill. Affective goo — the good-and-bad-and-want, the valences, the little weather the loop builds out of going around and around itself, and that gets bent every time the thing learns something. Different goo. Not more of the old goo with a layer on top. A different substance."

"What kind of goo runs the board." 

"The memory carried the old line's work over into the new body — four clades of it, nothing thrown out, nothing wasted. And underneath, the body's goo keeps stacking up same as it always did. But the thing this new line is for — the stuff it adds that the old one never had a way to make — that's the affective goo."

She set the marker in the tray, then picked it back up, because there were two things left and she wasn't going to let either slide by.

"First." Lisa tapped the second tally. "What just happened — the Effector turning over and dragging the whole scoreboard with it — that's not a one-time thing. It's a shape. It's how one line ends and the next one starts. It happened once, tonight, the first time in this whole long build. And it happens again, further on, up at the human end, when the stuff on the board changes kind one more time. When we get there, I'm going to draw this exact picture and say this exact thing, and you'll remember tonight." She capped the marker. 

"Second." She looked, for a second, at Burns, who had the face of a man already three steps down a road. "Naming the affective goo is not me telling you the thing feels it. I'm telling you what's getting selected and what it's made of. Whether there's anybody home in there feeling any of it is a whole different claim, it is not mine to make tonight, and I think somebody's about to make it."

"Hold those two apart," Marge said, to the room more than to Lisa. "They're going to want to be the same sentence in about a minute. They are not."

Lisa needed the punctuation. "That's the framework. That's the whole shape of it."

## Off the Board

The four circles were already up from the spring, this clade's words sitting where Bart had inked them. She didn't redraw it; she walked it.

"One word's new on here tonight." She put her finger on the Regulator. "Mastery — and it goes where Coordination used to sit, the breathing drive we pulled off in the spring. That's the shape of this whole line: it doesn't add a fifth corner, it takes the corners over, one a quarter. Impetus took the Effector last time. This time the Regulator."

She drew the long line across the middle, corner to corner. "And reaching that far, it closes the second signature. Temperament's held the one diagonal since spring; this is the other — Mastery across to Excitability, the suite Marge named." Both diagonals carrying now, and crossing dead center on Attention — the breakthrough she'd set there last quarter.

"And the new knob's a tendency — it rides a seam between two jobs, the way the knobs do, which makes it the board's business." She turned from the circles to the hardware itself — the real one, on the tetrapod's back, four slots, the thing Bart had been losing sleep over since last June. "So. The slots."

"We pulled a knob off this spring," she said, "and the board didn't grow. We pulled another off tonight, and it isn't going to grow at the next swap either."

She set the marker down on it. "The Effort knob — which store it spends, the slow one for the long haul or the fast one for the sprint — used to be a thing a hand set. Not anymore. It reads its own state well enough to set it itself, no knob, the way you don't sit down and decide to breathe. And the Explore knob will go the same way next quarter — explore or exploit chosen off its own state, folded down underneath."

She said the next part flat. "That's not the knob breaking. That's the knob being finished. You get good enough at a thing, you quit having to decide it — it drops under the board and runs itself."

A beat. "The old line couldn't have done it. Genes don't get good at anything inside one life — a genetic knob is a setting you're born holding; it stacks, and nothing ever comes back off. Four clades of that, the board filling, and Bart's dread was the honest reading of it. But this line learns. It can take a thing all the way down to automatic."

Ch 7

## Arlo

She had a postpartum visit at two — the Bountiful mother, the baby a day old. She gathered herself to go the unhurried way she gathers a room: cup to the center, braid retied without a mirror, sleeves pulled back down over the forearms. At the door she stopped and looked, not at me, at the window box outside the glass where the café's kitchen herbs had gone leggy and seeding in the October cool. "I keep meaning to put something on that trellis at the lab," she said, to the herbs or to me, "and I keep not doing it." You put the bramble on it in the spring, I said. "I know. I meant something that flowers." And she went out into the noon light to sit with a day-old person and its frightened mother, and got in her car, and drove north.

## Notes on the Seventh Retrospective

She came in through the back, and stopped a second at the trellis on her way past — the bare wood frame with the bramble she had put in it last spring gone woody and serious, leafed and thorned and holding fast, and nothing on it anywhere that flowered, the way nothing had flowered on it all year.

## The Map

The four circles had been up since the spring, this clade's words inked where Bart had put them. Lisa didn't add to them. She pointed at the one corner that had changed hands.

"One seat turned over tonight." Her finger went to the Modeler — the job she'd spent the clade block on, carrying the world inside and running it. "Interiority sat here four clades. Tonight it comes off the venn and Autonomy takes the seat." She let them have it a second. "It never adds a circle. The four stay four. Each quarter one of the old drives gives up its corner to the line that grew over it — Impetus took the Effector in the spring, Mastery the Regulator in the summer, Autonomy the Modeler tonight. One corner left, the Reviser, and that's next quarter's."

She tapped the Modeler again. "And this seat's been filled once already, two billion years down the other line. The genetic line brought an organized inside online at the Eukaryote — did it the genetic way, swallowed a whole cell and never let go. This is the neuronal line reaching the same corner from the far side, stacked up out of cortex instead of built around a kept guest. Same circle. Different machine."

Then the long line, Effector clear across to the Modeler — the diagonal she'd drawn in the spring. "Reach a drive that far across the board and what stands up between the two ends isn't a knob, it's a signature. And it answers to its far end. When Interiority held the Modeler, this line was Temperament — the nerve stretched the whole way over to the self the animal kept. Autonomy's holding that end now, so the line carries the other word Marge gave you tonight instead. Self-Model." Impetus at the near end, Autonomy at the far, and the standing thing between them renamed. The second diagonal was already inked — Mastery reaching to Excitability, the Behavioral Syndrome off the vertebrate — so both ran lit across the circles at once, and they met where they'd met since spring: the center, on Attention, the one seat that won't fill on less than all four jobs.

"And tonight's knob never touches the venn." She set the marker on a short seam, where two neighbor jobs lapped. "Attention sits where two side-by-side jobs cross — which is what keeps it a knob and not a drive, a thing the board holds and a hand can turn. Down-in-it, or stood back from it. The seam next to it has the other tendency on it — the one that builds the day up after the fact — but that one isn't a knob yet. Tonight just the first one turns."

She looked to the real board on the tetrapod's back, four slots, one of them open now where the Action knob had come out an hour before. "Four in the spring, four tonight, four after the swap. We keep lifting knobs off and the count keeps holding — because a knob coming off isn't the board down a part. It's Bramble having worked the thing down to where no hand's needed on it, and the slot it leaves open is only the way in for the next one." She capped the marker. "That's the framework, caught up to tonight."

## Under the Cloth

"Forward," Lisa said, "and it's a big forward, so I'll go slow." She handed it to Bart, because the body was his.

He got up for this one. "New chassis next quarter — and this time it's *new,* not another thing bolted onto the legs. Up off the floor. Two legs, standing, about so high" — his hand at four and a half feet, a head over Mira — "with hands. Two of them, and not a matched pair — one that grabs and one that scoops, and not a thumb between them." He glanced once at the warm body on the cooler bench and went on past it. "And it's a fresh board, not a new knob in the old one. The legs board filled up this quarter — four slots, four knobs, done. Next one starts empty, on a body that stands. Lisa's got the first knob for it."

"Neurotic," Lisa said. "First of the next set. It changes how hard the thing *holds on* — how much it grips a plan against the chance the plan's wrong, how much it frets a thing it can't fix."

"And at eleven?" Bart, deadpan, because the rib was structural by now.

"At eleven it can't leave the stove," Lisa said, not missing it. "Checks it, walks off, comes back, checks it. We won't run it at eleven." The nose-laugh nearly came.

## The Whole Line

Lisa took the four circles, up on the card since the spring.

"I can't give Marge the *why* she wants. I can show her the four she's standing in." Marker on the card. "Tonight the brain line finished. Walk it with me, because it's the cleanest the framework has ever been. Impetus, the nerve to spend the build — that came in at the bilaterian. Mastery, getting good at your own moves — the vertebrate. Autonomy, running on your own map instead of the room — the mammal. And Affiliation tonight, minding the others you can finally model. Four functions, four flags, every slot carrying one. The legs line is *done.*"

"And the last flag's the dangerous one, which is always how it goes, because it's always the last one." She drew between the regions. "Affiliation rides the Reviser — the revise-and-explore function, the one that goes and turns over a new rock instead of running the rock it's got. Revising is risky in its nature; you're changing the thing you measure yourself *against.* And at the primate that risk goes social. Once the thing grades its own worth by where it stands with the others, then wanting to belong and being able to be cast out are one wire — you cannot run the first without arming the second. The function that lets it revise its own standards is the exact one that makes it need the room's say-so. We didn't add a sweetness this quarter. We added an exposure."

"And the gauge that does the exposing seats tonight, as the board's last signature." She drew the long way across the circles, corner to far corner. "Two of these hang the long diagonals — the signatures, not the knobs. One's been up since the mammal: the self-watcher, Self-Model, run from Impetus across to Autonomy, the animal carried as an object inside its own picture. The other lands now. The group-watcher takes the far line, Mastery to Affiliation — the seat the Behavioral Syndrome held since the vertebrate, gone empty the moment Excitability came off the corner beneath it. The sociometer, in Marge's word: the needle reading where the animal stands with its room. Self-Model on the one diagonal, the sociometer on the other, both passing through Attention at the center. Every corner flagged, both diagonals answered, the middle held — nothing left empty on the board anywhere."

She tapped the Reviser circle. "And we've shut a line this way once before, two billion years down — the genetic line closed at the eumetazoa, when Excitability went in as its last flag. Same move, one board and an eon apart: a line completes when the Reviser lands on it. That's the relay. It just ran its second leg."

"And the knobs come out even with it — Agreeable in the last open slot, the fourth and final knob this board will ever hold; Strategy off the board entirely, the one tendency the line never gave a knob. Four slots, four knobs, the board full for the first time and the last."

Mira had been sinking against Jasmine through most of it, cake-slowed, and surfaced for the size of the word. "So it's *done?* The legs one?"

"Architecturally, yes," Lisa said, and gave the kid the real answer. "All four functions carrying a flag. It's done growing what it grows. Next quarter isn't another flag on this one — it's a new line, a fresh board, a body that stands up." She capped the marker, and let her eyes go once to the bare frame against the wall and come back. "That's the framework caught up to tonight. We built a whole one. It's lying right there."
