# Category 2 — Cheryl's Lunches

Compiled and tagged per the ruling in `category_sequencing_spec.md`. Source: pulled only from
`/chapters/examples/loom/cat2_allchapters.md`. QC-verified against the master chapter files
(Ch3, Ch9, Ch11 spot-checked word-for-word; each lunch is a single continuous heading-to-heading
scene with nothing else fused in and nothing of it belonging elsewhere) — clean, no contamination.

Four beats, first-pass segmentation per Brian's ruling:

- **2.a — Scene Setting.** The venue-and-arrival opening: room, weather, season, the physical fact
  of arrival.
- **2.b — Dialogue & Character.** Straight dialogue, naming-origin material, and physical tells
  (laughs, hands, posture) — full sentences/paragraphs about the person opposite Cheryl. Deep
  character analysis Cheryl performs *of* the other person is filed here too, since it's still
  about them, not about her.
- **2.c — Cheryl's Self-Narration.** Cheryl's own interiority, the reciprocal-vulnerability
  exchanges, and the serialized Marge subplot (the records-removal form, the near-misses, the
  romantic arc) — all filed here per Brian's explicit instruction, even where it surfaces inside
  dialogue.
- **2.d — Drive Home Coda.** The canyon drive that closes most (not all) lunches.

Unlike category 1, this material doesn't fragment into cleanly separable chunks — it's continuous
first-person prose that moves between these four modes sentence-to-sentence, sometimes within a
single paragraph. So the boundaries below are Cheryl's editorial judgment on genuinely interwoven
text, not a mechanical sort — flagged for adjustment, not locked.

**Notable finding:** 2.d was originally absent from five chapters (Ch4, Ch7, Ch8, Ch9, Ch12) — a
real structural inconsistency, unlike category 1 where every beat held in every chapter. Voice has
since written new 2.d closes for Ch4, Ch8, Ch9, and Ch12, now integrated below. **Ch7 remains the
one chapter with no canyon-drive coda** — Jasmine leaves the lunch to a postpartum visit rather
than Cheryl driving off from a scene, which may be why; flagged, not yet resolved. See notes at
bottom.

## Ch 2

### <!--[2.a]--> Scene Setting

I got there a quarter of an hour early. I like arriving early to a thing I don't yet know the shape of, and the first lunch with Burns was a thing I did not yet know the shape of. I had driven down from Park City on I-80, which is what you drive when you want to get from Park City to Salt Lake, and the drive had not been interesting, and I had not narrated it to myself. I parked a couple of blocks from Smithers on eighth south, walked past the warehouse without looking at it — the building is mine and I did not want to stand in front of it on a Wednesday at noon as if I were my own tenant — and went in the brewery side door at Fisher.

It was the kind of late-July Wednesday where the heat had set in by mid-morning and meant to stay until dark. I ordered a seltzer at the bar, took it out to the patio, found a table along the back fence near the food trucks. The patio faced ninth south, which had a protected bike lane down the side of it, and the cyclists were in summer kit, bare arms and bare legs, and there were a lot of them. I watched them roll past for ten minutes. The Wasatch foothills stood in the flat overhead light of late July, the grass on them long burned to gold — a high hard light that would not slant and would not soften, the kind of light that did not so much fall as bear down.

Burns arrived three minutes early. He came in through the brewery side door looking for me, found me at the back fence, came over.

"That's fair."

He had given himself a deadline and named the failure mode of it in the same sentence. He had not asked me to hold him; he had asked me to ask. He wanted to be asked. That was useful to know.

"What are you reading, then," I said. "Since it isn't your own paper."

"Geothermal, lately. There's a project the U runs down in Milford — drilling into hot granite to see if you can pull a grid off it. Not my field." "Does it."

"Probably." He let it go.

I watched him eat for another minute.

## Ch 3

### <!--[2.a]--> Scene Setting

Marge had picked the place — Coffee Garden, a café on Ninth and Ninth with a south wall of windows and a counter of pastries nobody needed, the kind of room that had decided to be warm and then committed to it. Outside it was the tail of October, the light already low and spent by one o'clock, and the south windows took the little there was of it and laid it down the room in long copper bars.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

She was already in the corner when I came in, and she had ordered for both of us before I sat down, which I would learn was not rudeness but a fixed feature, like weather.

People hear *clinical psychologist* and build something cool and narrow, and the woman across from me was neither. She was broad and settled and a little rumpled, reading glasses pushed up into gray hair she had stopped coloring on principle, and her face did most of its work around the mouth — a mouth that worried even when the rest of her was at rest, that kept a small readiness for someone nearby to need something. Her cardigan was the color of a brick rained on for forty years. She held her coffee with both hands, close to her chest, the way you hold a tea you're in no hurry to finish, and I understood eventually that she held everything that way, that the posture had nothing to do with the coffee.

I'd come to talk about the vignettes — she was the one who turned the quarter's footage and logs into the small household scenes that reached me between meetings, and I wanted to know how she kept them from going soft, from making the robot into a child. The danger, she said, was not softness. It was flattery. The whole field had a bad habit of being charmed by the thing it was studying, and she spent most of her energy not being charmed. "The minute you're charmed you stop seeing," she said. "Charmed is a kind of lazy. It spends the same attention vigilance runs on, and it always spends first." She said it about Bramble. It was also, exactly, a thing a person could say about people, and she said it the way you say something you've had occasion to learn.

I said something then — I don't remember the shape of it, something a little too pleased with itself, about funders staying out of the science — and she set the cup down and looked at me and said, "Oh, you don't believe that," not unkindly, the way you correct a bright student who is hiding.

### <!--[2.c]--> Cheryl's Self-Narration

She was right. I had not expected to be caught at my own lunch by a woman drinking drip coffee in a wet-brick cardigan. The *oh, you* had a whole mother in it. It also had a whole argument in it, folded small.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

She'd been Mary until she was forty-four. I asked what happened at forty-four and she said Bart happened — that she'd known him for years, and then during the recruitment she sat across his kitchen table and felt him look at her for the better part of a minute and settle on something he didn't say. A week later she was Marge, to him first and then to all of them, and she'd decided not to fight it because the boy was so plainly delighted and so plainly sure. "He sees people as cartoons," she said. "It's alarming when it's you." She didn't tell me which cartoon. She didn't need to. I was already watching the worried mouth and the cup held against her chest like something that might wake, and I had her all at once, the way you get a likeness — the few lines that are the most of a person — and I understood that a kid with a gift had looked at this woman and seen exactly what I was seeing and called it Marge and been right.

### <!--[2.c]--> Cheryl's Self-Narration

I should say plainly that I was rendering her through something by then, because I have spent enough years being honest on tape to know the tell. I was making her larger and warmer than the drip coffee and the rained-on cardigan strictly supported. A clinical eye would have logged a tired academic in her forties, behind her cohort, ordering for strangers because it was easier than sitting still. I logged a hearth. I knew I was doing it. I did it anyway. It is the most Helen thing left in me, and I have mostly stopped apologizing for it.

I tried, once, to turn it around — asked her something real, where she was from, whether the family was still close — and watched her do the thing. A sentence and a half: Provo, big family. Then she was back across the table tending to me, asking whether I'd had anything that morning that wasn't coffee, and the redirect was so smooth and so practiced that I nearly missed it, except that it is my own move. I do it with a camcorder. She does it with a kettle. We are both people who would rather hold the cup than be the cup. I think that was the moment, if there was one.

When the check came she tried to pay — at my lunch — and I let her win the smaller half of the argument, and as she dug for her wallet a manila envelope slid halfway out of her bag: a corner of letterhead I knew from a hundred steeples, a line of form-text about the removal of records. She pushed it back down without a word. It was plainly not a thing for today, and I am good at not asking.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

We said the kind of goodbye two people say when they've just decided to like each other, which is to say a slightly awkward one.

### <!--[2.d]--> Drive Home Coda

And I drove back up the canyon to Park City — the scrub oak gone rust on both walls of it, the aspens higher up already bare — turning it over the way you turn a stone you've already decided to keep.

## Ch 4

### <!--[2.a]--> Scene Setting

The place I took Jasmine to in late January was Pho Tay Ho, on the line where the Granary gives over to Ballpark, a narrow warm room with the windows fogged from the inside, which in a Salt Lake January is the whole of what you want from a room.

### <!--[2.c]--> Cheryl's Self-Narration

I chose the warm room for the winter lunch the way an imposter hides on the lee side of a rock, without deciding to, because the body decides first.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

I had watched Jasmine for a year by then — on the tapes, in the corner of the warehouse footage, arriving in late afternoon with the child on her hip — and I had not been alone with her since the morning I handed the team the portfolio.

### <!--[2.c]--> Cheryl's Self-Narration

You come to know a person strangely that way. Hands first.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

She came in twenty minutes late with snow not yet gone from the shoulders of a wool coat the color of wet bark, sat down across from me, and put both her hands flat on the table for a second, the way a person does when the table is the first still thing they have touched all day. They are a midwife's hands, broad through the palm, the forearms behind them stronger than the soft sweaters let on, and when they go flat like that it means she has been somewhere her hands were the whole of her and the rest of her is still catching up. The warmth came up a half-second late and then it came up all the way, raspy at the edges the way it gets at the end of a long day, and she said it was good to sit in a room where nobody needed anything from her body, and laughed, and ordered a bowl of pho.

I asked how the week had been. She said hard. There was a particular flatness on the word, and I had watched enough of her by then to know not to pull on it. A hard week for the rest of us was a deadline, a flat tire on Foothill, a paper that had been wrong in its first paragraph for fourteen months. A hard week for Jasmine was a room where the thing that is supposed to begin does not begin — a mother, and, in the end, not a baby — and she had come from one of those in the last few days, I understood, the way you understand weather through a wall. She said the word and reached for the tea and that was all she meant to give it,

### <!--[2.c]--> Cheryl's Self-Narration

and I let it go by, because the letting was the only useful thing I had, and because I am a person who has spent a long life learning to let the hard thing go by rather than reach for it, and I do not always know anymore which of those two reasons I am running on.

So we did the thing I think the two of us were always going to do the first time we sat down without the lab or the child between us, which is envy each other across the table and not pretend otherwise.

She started it. She said I had so much room — she meant the calendar, the empty Tuesdays, the way I can decide over coffee to be in Marin by Thursday for no reason but the wanting to — she said I had all that room, and she had a pager that goes off at two in the morning, and a four-year-old who would be five in a couple of weeks, and a body that had not been fully her own in years. She said it warmly, the way you can only say a true envy to someone you actually like, and she meant it, and she was right.

And I told her the truth back, which is that I would trade the whole of it — the room, the planes, the long quiet afternoons I have arranged my life to be made almost entirely of — for one ordinary week of being as necessary to anything as she is to the rooms she walks into. I have money that cannot be spent down and time that cannot be filled, and I watch a family I did not make through a camcorder in a warehouse, and she goes into houses in the dark and is the reason a person comes through to the far side of the worst hours of their life, or is the one who stays when they do not, and either way she is in it, up to the forearms, and I have not been in anything up to the forearms since 2002. I say most of that, a surprising amount of it to her, because she does not flinch and she does not try to fix it, which are the two things I cannot do for myself.

She looked at me a moment the way she looks at the lab when it is being clever, fondly and from a small distance, and then she said: you would not trade for this week, though. She was right about that too, and we both let it sit there knowing it, and that was as close as either of us came to the part neither of us was going to say.

I had only just found out, late and across a warehouse table and inconveniently, that the place might still be findable in me — which is smaller news and more frightening news than a flight to Italy, and I said so, and she granted it, and did not try to make it even. Then she did the Lisa thing, which is not to comfort a thing and not to fix it but to set it in its frame and be done: that we were each, finally, declining the safe terrain — hers a man who could never leave, mine a distance I could never close — and that the move was the same move whatever the country.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

Then she lifted her bramble and held it out and said we should drink to it. *To blackberries,* she said — and let it sit there dry, the word holding the drink and the robot and the whole thorny tangle the lab was named for all at once, until I understood she had handed me the back half on purpose. *To blackberries,* I said, *and to the wonderfully mixed up prickly paths of life* — which is the kind of thing a person can only say two brambles in and mean all the way down, and I meant it all the way down. We touched the glasses.

### <!--[2.c]--> Cheryl's Self-Narration (Marge subplot)

Then she finished hers and filed it, the way she files everything, and asked me, plainly, whether I was going to do anything about Marge, and I said I did not know, which was true, and was the most hope I had carried out of one of these in three years.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

She walked me out. The city was still counting itself down under the lights, the torches and the cones and the cranes, none of which I could feel the way I was meant to.

### <!--[2.d]--> Drive Home Coda

I drove back up the canyon with the windows down and the thing she had given me unsealed on the seat beside me — which is the opposite of how I drive home from Lisa. From Lisa I have always driven home with nothing, having been let as far as the on-ramp and no farther. Tonight she let me the whole way in, and I knew even going up that the next time the red light came on in the warehouse she would run the room exactly and completely and give it not one thing she had given me here, the unpermissioned kept off the tape, and that I would be back behind the lens reading a woman I had, for one night in a dark room two brambles in, finally read. She was already running it out as I pulled away, I think — tomorrow's loop laid down in her head before my taillights cleared the lot, the cold river-path, the same answer waiting at the end of it. Genoa. I did not try to talk her out of it. For once I had nothing in the right order to say.

## Ch 12

### <!--[2.a]--> Scene Setting

The first time we sat here it was July and the doors stood open to 8th south, and Burns told me over the second beer why a roomful of grown scientists called their director after a cartoon villain, and somewhere in the telling he was, for one sentence and never quite again, Ben. Three years on it was the last week of January, the doors shut against the cold, the street outside not a street anymore but a staging area — barricades, lanyards, a banner two stories tall on the building opposite — the whole city holding its breath two weeks out from the Games and meanwhile deafening with the held breath, every place near the lab suddenly full of people from countries I would have had to look up. Fisher's was the same long table along the wall and the same back-corner booth, which is Bart's booth, the one he keeps to, where he and his father have drunk together more nights than either of them would tell you about. We did not take the booth. We took the table, in the daylight, and I watched Burns come in out of the cold already talking.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

He was more himself than I had seen him, which is a thing I have learned to say about Burns and mean exactly, because there is a version of him performing himself and a version simply running, and the desert had left him running. He had gone out past Moab for a few days after the new year — he does this about once a decade, and I will say no more about it than he does, which is little, except that he comes back not changed but more thoroughly arrived at — and he sat down across from me with the particular ease of a man who has lately remembered he has a body. His hands, which I have watched fail to hold still through every pitch he has ever made me, lay flat on the table and stayed. His face had the unguarded look of someone who has set a heavy thing down and not yet picked it back up. He told me he'd spent an afternoon out there lying under a juniper with his shirt off doing nothing, nothing at all, and that it had been among the best afternoons of his life, and he reported it the way you'd report weather, with none of the apparatus he usually bolts onto a feeling before he'll let it leave the building.

"The hard part," he said, turning the glass, "is coming home. Out there all I wanted was to be naked all the time. Out of all of it — the clothes, the lanyards, the —" a hand at the window, the banner, the apparatus of the watching world "— all of it. And you can't. You come home and you can't walk through your own kitchen the way you want to, because there's a seven-year-old and a midwife and a UPS man and the sovereign state of Utah, so you put the pants back on." The chest-laugh. "My granddaughter wrote a constitution this fall and called it the Declaration of Underpants, and I keep thinking she's the only one in that house with the whole thing figured out. Cover the necessary and declare the rest a free country."

### <!--[2.c]--> Cheryl's Self-Narration

I had come to hear about the last knob and the finished board and was instead hearing a man of sixty-one tell me he wanted to be naked, and I did not steer him off it, because a person does not hand you that sentence unless something has come loose that he means to leave loose a while, and I wanted to see what else the looseness would let out.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

What it let out, a beer later and with no change in his voice I could have pointed to, was Bart. He was looking at the booth when it came — not pointing, just looking, the way the eyes go to the thing the mouth is talking around — and he said the best hours of his week were the ones in that corner with his son, and that he had finally let himself understand, out there under the juniper, that he did not know whether his son loved him. He said it without a grain of self-pity, which was the worst of it. He said it the way he names a fact about the rock. That Bart came to the booth, and drank with him, and had built him every body he'd ever asked for and would build him another tomorrow, and that none of it was the same as the thing he was asking about; that he had made a whole machine a child could love precisely because he could not engineer the one across the table into loving him; and that he was not even certain, he said, that the boy ought to.

### <!--[2.c]--> Cheryl's Self-Narration

I have spent a good part of my life learning you cannot talk two people into fitting who have finished becoming what they are, and I had the whole speech ready — shapes, the impossibility of retrofitting a grown organism, all of it — and I did not give it, because it is a true thing that helps no one, and because the man across from me already knew it better than I did. He had built his life's work out of knowing it. He was telling me, in the only words he had for it, that knowing did not lay a finger on the wanting. So I did the one useful thing available, which was nothing. I let him say it. I let it sit on the table between the glasses.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

And when it had sat there long enough, Ben looked up — for the one sentence, he was Ben; and asked me, nearly cheerful, whether I thought he ought to have the booth made into a plaque someday, and I said no, and he laughed, and we ordered food.

### <!--[2.a]--> Scene Setting

Outside, a volunteer in a parka was zip-tying a sign to a barricade and another was photographing the banner, and the cold came in each time the door did, and Fisher's filled around us with strangers who had traveled a very long way to watch other people do difficult things perfectly.

### <!--[2.b]--> Dialogue & Character

Burns ate. He told me, between bites, about the heat under Roosevelt that nobody was drilling right, and about the Games, which he loved without one ounce of irony, and about the board being full, which he mentioned the way you mention the thing you are not yet ready to look straight at.

### <!--[2.c]--> Cheryl's Self-Narration

I let him talk. I did not look at the booth, and he did not look at it again either.

### <!--[2.d]--> Drive Home Coda

I drove up the canyon this last time with the radio off and the windows cracked against the cold, and for once I watched it the whole way instead of letting it go by. Not the booth — the booth stayed his — but the man who had been in it for three years running, who could raise an animal a rung at a time out of a dead ocean and could not raise one honest word out of his own son, and did not know, driving home tonight the same as any other night, whether the boy loved him back. I let it ride the whole way up in the seat beside me, past Kimball Junction, past the summit, into the garage, where I turned off the car and sat a minute with the engine ticking before I went in.
