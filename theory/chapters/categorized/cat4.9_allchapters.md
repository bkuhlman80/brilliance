### <!--[4.9.a]--> Vocabulary Mode

What followed was Lisa walking the team through her agenda for the meeting. Lisa had drafted the agenda over the course of June and revised it across the second week of July. She had sent it to Burns on a Thursday. He had returned it with no edits, which she had taken to mean either that he agreed with all of it or that he had not read it; either possibility was acceptable to her. She was running the meeting for the first time, and she was going to find out what worked.

She had three content blocks. The first was about the clade — the Protocell, as Cheryl had named it. The team would walk through what a protocell was, what its evolutionary moment had been, what the bots in front of them rendered of that moment. The second block was about the bot itself: the household's three months with the five bots, the question of the wind-up crank as the commission's personality knob, what the next quarter's build would be. The third block was the framework. She did not yet call it the Trellis block. She called it "the bigger picture," with a hand-gesture that meant up.

She turned on the camcorder. The small red LED came on. The flip-out screen showed the room, slightly fish-eyed at the edges, and on it the four labmates plus Jasmine and Mira and Bramble.

"For Cheryl," Lisa said. "She watches the tape."

Nobody addressed the camera. Nobody pretended it wasn't there either. The team had agreed to this in May, after a long conversation in which Bart had been the holdout for forty minutes and had then conceded on the grounds that Cheryl was paying them a great deal of money and they were behaving naturally anyway. Bart looked at the camera once. He nodded at it. He did not nod at it again across the meeting. The camera had been logged in.

Burns took the floor next. He stood up to do it. He had not sat down yet, in fact; he had been standing or crouching for the entire opening, in the way of a man who could not be in a room with the work of the room without orbiting the table at which the work would happen. His hands moved when he talked. Bart, two seats down, watched the hands.

"The thing we are doing," Burns said. "I am going to say it out loud, because this is the first time we are saying it out loud as a team, on tape, with Cheryl watching."

He looked at the camcorder. He looked away from the camcorder.

"We are going to build twelve robots. One per clade. Cheryl has given us the clades. She has not told us what to build and she has not told us what each robot is supposed to teach us. She has told us that each robot must have, in its body somewhere, a knob that controls something about its personality. We are going to figure out what each knob controls. We are going to figure out what each robot's personality is. We are going to figure out, by the end, what personality means."

He paused there.

"That last part," he said, "is the part Cheryl has not asked us to do. She has asked us to deliver the robots. The theory of personality is a thing we are going to build on the side, because we cannot build the robots without it. We are paying for the theory by building the robots. Cheryl is paying for the robots in order to be paid back in the theory. This is, I want to say out loud, the deal as I understand it. If anyone in this room understands it differently they should say so now."

Nobody said anything. Marge said, "I understand it the same way."

"Lisa."

"Same."

"Bart."

"Same."

"Then we are agreed."

He took a sip of his beer. It was an IPA from Fisher's, two blocks away, brought in a growler.

"That's all I have at the open. The rest is in the blocks."

He sat down. He had been standing for the entire opening. The chair felt new under him.

Framework was where the words got set down for good. Bart had been boxing them on the backs of his cards all evening — every time the room reached for one and caught it, he wrote it, underlined it, drew the small box. Now he laid the cards face-up, and they were the night's haul.

"Burns. You left one open at the vent."

He had — he'd reached for the thing the protocell did its work *with* and set his hand back down. He picked the word up now. "Capacity. I've been carrying it a week — read it in a paper I should've read fifteen years ago. The new thing a clade has that the one before it didn't. The protocell's was the capacity to be selected on. Every clade after it adds one."

"And the event — the moment the capacity comes online. We've been fighting about that word." He looked at Marge.

"Innovation," Marge said.

"Innovation."

"Emergent capability."

"Emergent capability."

"Phase transition."

"Phase transition was mine. I'm letting it go." He wrote *breakthrough*, boxed it. "That's the one that lived. We picked the plain word because the others were pretentious and Marge made fun of us. Each clade has a breakthrough."

"Lisa. You left one at the door too — the gravity, the dispositions. Said there was a word in your field and it was wrong for the room."

"Dynamical attractor," Lisa said.

Marge set her beer down. "Can we call them tendencies."

Lisa held the moment with her face neutral, and what she felt — privately, briefly — was the pleasure of being asked to take off a coat she hadn't known she was wearing. "Tendencies. Better than what I had."

"Thank you," Marge said.

Burns laughed the chest-laugh, because there was something cosmically funny to him about a lab choosing the simpler word at the start of a three-year project on the architecture of personality. He laughed early; the room caught up a beat behind. Bart wrote *tendency*, boxed it, and held up two fingers to Burns, who'd been counting.

"And the knob," Lisa said. "You showed us the crank. You never said what it's the knob *of.*"

Bart looked at the card, at the crank he'd drawn sticking up off the dome — the shape he'd said in February he would find, and had. "Going," he said. "That's the dimension. A volume knob on Going — the crank's how you wind it up. What it decides is whether the thing *goes.* Wind it up and the volume's up and it goes; let it run down through the week and the volume falls with it, toward dead. All the way up to all the way down, and every day between a notch lower."

He took the pen. "And it's the plainest word I'm ever going to write on one of these, because it's the crudest knob we'll ever build. They get richer from here. This one's just *Going.*" He wrote it on the back of the Protocell card, underlined it, boxed it, and turned it to the camera — the first knob named, eleven to go.

### <!--[4.9.c]--> Board Geometry

"And here's the question I've been carrying about it. I know what that layer *does* — I built it, Bart and I picked the states, he's running it. What I don't know yet is what it *is*, structurally, in the framework we're building." She set both palms flat. "A partial answer — a phrase I've been trying across the quarter. *A stored controller.* A thing whose rules, its behavior given input, are written down somewhere and looked up when the input arrives. The protobots don't have one — only their physics, heat in and motion out, nothing in between. Bramble has rules in between, and the rules are stored. Bart and I stored them. He's executing them."

Burns, who had been listening, said, "Lisa. That is what biology is."

Lisa said, "Say more."

Burns said, "Every cell on the planet is running a stored controller. The genome is the storage. The cell is the runtime. The controller is whatever expression program the cell is running. The transition rules — when to make this protein, when to divide, when to gate the sporulation pathway — are stored in the DNA and looked up by the molecular machinery that does the looking up. Bart talked about sporulation an hour ago. Sporulation is a stored controller. The rules for when to sporulate are written in the DNA. The cell reads them when conditions trigger the read. The cell does not invent the rules. The cell executes the rules."

He paused.

"Every cell on the planet is doing this. It is what biology is. It is the substrate that biology runs on. The lineage writes the rules across generations; the cell reads them and runs. The FSM is the same kind of thing. It is just that we, the lab, are the lineage in this case."

Burns said, "Here is the most concrete version of it. One of the things the rules decide is who gets in. The prokaryote's controller builds doors. Real ones — proteins set into the membrane that recognize one specific molecule and haul it in against the crowd, and burn energy to do it. The lac operon Bart walked you through is the controller deciding to build the lactose door when there is lactose worth bringing in."

"And none of it is free. The world outside is always trying to flatten the cell into it, to make the inside the same as the outside. The rest is paying, every second, to be a separate thing at all."

### <!--[4.9.c]--> Board Geometry

Harold Innis: the *medium* sets the reach — what you can write the rule on decides how far the rule travels. Jack Goody on writing scaling administration past anything memory holds. James Scott on legibility, a state running at size by making its people *readable* — turning them into standardized records it can see from the center. Douglass North and Avner Greif on the institutions that let a stranger trust a deal with someone he'll never meet twice. César Hidalgo on a society being bounded by how much information it can physically hold and move." She slowed, the confidence-marking coming on, the seam she'd promised. "And the two we lean on hardest, who are also the two we're going *past* — West and Bettencourt. They measured it. Double a city and its output more than doubles, about fifteen percent more every time, clean across the world, while the roads and pipes it needs go up *less* than double. Cities get superlinearly productive and sublinearly expensive as they grow. That's not a metaphor. It's a number you can check."

She let the number stand, then put a finger on the exposed place. "Here's our part, and it's a bet, not a finding. They say the engine is *density* — pack people closer, they interact more, the interactions are where the extra comes from. We think that's half of it. Pile more strangers together and you don't automatically get more *coordinated* interaction; you can just as easily get more collision. The thing that turns proximity into coordination — that makes a denser crowd more productive instead of just louder — is the standardizing layer. The codes, the currency, the weights and measures, the law. That's the bridge they didn't draw and we are, and it's the part of this card I'd put the lowest confidence on and the most weight." The nose-laugh again, drier. "We could be wrong. But that's where we are."

She squared the card. "And the shape it grows is a person held a new way. A forager's held by who he knows. A citizen's held by his standing in an order full of people he doesn't — so the wanting reorganizes around it: toward being recognized inside the institution instead of belonging to a face; toward walking up to unrelated strangers where it pays to, the merchant and the lawyer and the priest and the soldier, all jobs a band had no use for; toward holding your position against the strangers lined up to take it; and toward the codes themselves as the thing you answer to, where you stop minding the relationship and start internalizing the rule. And the drive under all of it is new — representation: the pull toward being on the record, having a standing in the order that's there whether or not anyone's looking at you, a self that's written down."

She marked the board last, the housekeeping she never skips — where the city's four drives actually sat. Representation went into the modeler, the picture-building corner autonomy had owned; autonomy went under, and the citizen's pull to be on the record took the seat. And a corner never turns over alone. The crossing the modeler anchored went with it: the self-reference the band had closed, the loop of being seen, stepped down, and the felt thing Marge had just named — core self-evaluation, the day-long read of whether you can hold your spot — took the line in its place. The settlement's crossing, the self-construal, she left untouched; it had a turn of its own coming, but not tonight. Which left three of the four drives the new line's, and one still the old's — affiliation, the last neuronal thing standing, holding the one corner the symbolic line hadn't taken yet.

The narrator kept that one — the thing made of two things that used to be apart, becoming a third that was neither — the way she keeps the ones she'll need later. Lisa capped the framework and opened the floor, which is where the standing question always came back around.

### <!--[4.9.b]--> Motivation & Signature Naming

Lisa squared the card against the table and gave the structure in her usual order, fastest where she is surest. What carries the imperial person past everything under it is the new flag — the one that takes every reference the system holds and checks it against a frame that is supposed to be true everywhere. "On the card it's Universality. Burns will make it sound like Sinai. What it is, under the sound, is the animal learning to mark its own answers against a rule it claims holds past the room."

The traits that fall out of it she named and would not sit in, because two of them belonged to other people. Extraverted and Conscientious we already knew. The two new ones arrived together. One was the drive to reason in the abstract — to chase an idea because it holds and not because it pays, the thing the Axial Age people were doing when they went hunting a truth that didn't care whose god you prayed to. "Intellect, on the card. The one that finally makes the thing want to be right in general and not just right here." The other was the one the Honest knob going in tonight actually sets: the disposition to hold a principle steady across rooms even when the room is begging you to bend it. "Honest," she said, and stopped, and looked at Marge.

It reached Marge, and she took it. "Honest is the plainest of these, and the one I've least to add to," she said. "It's the Honest knob that decides, when doing right and keeping the peace pull apart, which one the body lets go of — and Bart's already set it in front of you. The other one I'll take, because it's the one that hasn't a household word yet." She meant the new signature, the one called identity, handed across unfinished.

"It's the oldest thing a story does," Marge said, "and we've built up to it for a year, so I'll be plain about it. A year ago, when the city went in, the self a person had to work out was a self measured against the people he knew — am I one who stands apart, or one who's woven in. That question is most of what my whole field is about, and it has a floor under it: the known faces, the ones you can actually stand next to and compare yourself to." She turned her beer a quarter, the tea-grip on it. "The empire takes the floor out. The imperial person does not settle who he is against the faces he knows. He settles it against a category he has never once seen the edge of and never will. He is a Roman. He is Han. He's a Muslim, a Christian, a subject of the Crown. Ask him who he is and he doesn't point down the street at his village. He names a thing with tens of millions of people in it, almost none of whom he will ever meet, most of whom are imaginary to him in the plainest meaning of the word — he has imagined them, and decided he is one of them." She let it sit a beat. "And if the story is any good, he will die for them. That is the whole power of the thing, and it is not a small or a safe power: a story can hand a person a self that is larger than anyone he could ever actually hold."

Lisa was already lifting the next card, and did not look up while Marge talked, which by the twelfth meeting I had learned to read as the opposite of not listening. She was, in some quarter of herself I had never been let into, a few weeks from setting down a name a whole building knew and walking into a building on another continent where not one person had yet heard it — about to spend, in her own life, the exact coin Marge was laying on the table — and she gave the room not one degree of it. She squared the card, and said it was Bart's turn, and that we should do the shapes.

### <!--[4.9.c]--> Board Geometry

Lisa took the floor without spending a breath she didn't need, and put the whole thing on the screen at once. For three years she had shown it to us one clade at a time, the way you build anything you would hate to drop, and I had never seen all twelve lit together. Lit together they were not a list. They were a shape, and the shape was simple in the specific way that only a thing built out of something very simple can be.

"Four functions," she said. "That's the whole alphabet. A thing that acts. A thing that holds the acting to a rule. A thing that builds a picture of the world. A thing that rewrites the picture." On the screen, four circles, the kind you draw to teach a child about overlap. "Everything personality has ever been is a region of those four circles. One circle by itself is a drive — what the thing wants. Two that touch is a habit, a way of running. Two that sit across from each other is a signature, how it holds itself together at a level. Three of them, with one left out, is a kind of person." She let it sit a second, which on Lisa is a held note. "That's the trellis. The bramble's just been climbing it."

And then she did the only thing I have ever watched her do that I'd call mischievous: she left the four kinds-of-person up on the glass — the four ways to make a person by leaving one function out — and said nothing, and waited. Leave out the acting and you get the one who only plans. Leave out the picture and you get the one who acts and learns and never models why. Leave out the rule and you get the one who pursues and pursues with nothing to brake it. Leave out the rewrite and you get the one who runs a finished model beautifully and cannot change its mind. Four feet to my left Bart said, "that's us," with the pleasure of a man announcing the obvious — and the obvious was the same one Marge had set down ten minutes before, off the people instead of off the glass. She had read the four shapes out of the four of us; Lisa had drawn the four of us back out of the shapes, four circles and a subtraction; and the two roads met in the middle at the same four faces. I kept one thing to myself, because it would have been unkind aloud: that the planner who never acts was three weeks from the largest act of her life. And I noticed, the way you notice a thing you have already been told, that the shape up there which cannot change its mind was Marge's — and that none of us said the obvious thing about Marge, which is that she had.

The completion itself she gave in one sentence, because the completion was the whole evening and did not want a flourish. The last circle to come online, three years in, was the rewriter, the Reviser, carrying tonight's drive — the universal one — and as it came on, affiliation — the last drive the new line had yet to displace — folded down into the memory on the schedule Lisa had called, and with it the last empty region of the picture filled in: the last signature, the one Marge had taken, the self a person builds out of a category too large to see the edge of. Twelve clades. Three lines. The bramble at the top of the trellis with nothing above it, on the current board, left to climb to. "That's the architecture," Lisa said. "That's all of it. There's no thirteenth."

I sat in front of the finished thing and felt two things that would not agree. One was that it was beautiful — beautiful the way a proof is, four circles and a fixed order and the whole unruly zoo of what a person can be falling out of them like change from a torn pocket. The other was Marge's voice from ten minutes earlier, telling me that the click I was feeling right then, the soft click of the thing closing over, was the exact click I was not to trust. And Lisa, who had drawn every line of it and was three weeks from setting it down and walking out of the building where it lived, stood looking at her own closed circles with a face I had no clade for, and did not say the obvious thing about a planner finally taking an action, and reached up, and turned the screen off.
