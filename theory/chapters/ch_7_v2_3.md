## Build log — The Furnace and the Inside Voice
*Bart, hardware. Filed September 3, 2032, for the team.*

Carried it back this morning, same legs body as the summer, no new chassis — except it came back warm. Not warm off the floor. Warm off itself, warm the way a hand is. Mira put her palm flat on its back before she said hi to me. 

How it makes the warm is the part I'm proud of. There's a little air compressor in the core, filling all the time, soft, a faucet barely cracked, and it feeds a rack of vortex tubes. A vortex tube is a beautiful, stupid thing: a pipe with no moving parts that takes a stream of air in and throws hot out one end and cold out the other. We ran one *coupled* on the dome's dock, hot balanced against cold to hold a line. This time I ran a whole rack of them *un*coupled — dump all the hot into a sealed, insulated core, vent all the cold out the side, and a thermostat rides the compressor to hold that core warm no matter what the kitchen's doing.

Reason it's worth the trouble: the battery lives in the warm core now, and a warm battery gives full current cold room or not. For the first time the thing's just as quick at fifty degrees as at seventy. Last winter a cold morning made it slow and dumb; this one won't care.

Here's the cost, flat, because you'll feel it by October: it *eats.* The compressor pulls every second — sitting still, asleep, doesn't matter — and I didn't grow the tanks, so it burns a charge in hours where the old one coasted most of a day. It has to feed near-constant now. The dome can still trundle off and sit a day and a half on its dock doing nothing; this one can't afford to sit down. Warm costs. Fair trade. I'd build it again.

It makes water, too. Run a compressor all day and you're wringing the wet out of the air whether you meant to or not; it falls out at the cold end and pools, and the thing has to clear it. So two or three times a day it burps the water out a vent low at the back — wet, and louder than you'd think — and goes on about whatever it was doing. Keeps the core dry. That's the build.

Knobs, board, and compute all sit *outside* the hot core, in the cool zone along the back, where the parts run better cold and where a thumbnail can still reach a slot. The core's sealed and you'd never want to touch it; everything the house needs to get at is out where it can. That was on purpose.

And — you can hear it again. It's been quiet since I pulled the relays; too-fast wires don't make a sound. Now there's the compressor going soft under everything, all day, and the house has a noise off the thing again. 

Furnace or no furnace, the real headline this quarter is Lisa gave it a scratchpad. The thing sketches maps and carries them around now: the place held inside it, not just out front of the cameras the way it always was. Used to be, lose sight of a thing and the thing was gone — it'd stall where the thing had been and quit. Now it goes looking: hide a thing and it works the spots that thing turns up in till it has it. Knows where stuff is, knows where it is itself, keeps what's happened where besides — a whole little inside account of the place, run forward to guess what's coming and back over what already did. How she gets that to build itself out of nothing but the thing wandering the house is her board and her math, and I couldn't walk you through it if you paid me. My end of it's the screen it shows on.

The forehead screen's got a new line. It's had the one — shows what it's doing to a thing (CHASE CAT, NUDGE MIRA) on the forward dial, or just the bare thing (CAT, MIRA) with the back dial. I gave the screen a second line, up top: new real estate, my lettering, clean. *What* goes on the top line is Lisa's — it names the job: PERCEIVING, RECALLING, PREDICTING, CHECKING. What it's doing in its own head while it does the thing with its body. She's calling it the inside voice. Her board runs it; I just built it somewhere to write.

How the screen reads is split by the new knob. The knob is called Attention. Two modes: immersive and surveying. Throw it into immersive and the new top line goes dark and it drops straight back to last quarter — bare band, dark asleep, the doing-word read right off the brain, no guessing. Throw it into surveying and the top line stays lit around the clock, sleep and all. 

And now the part to keep straight: with the top line lit, the second line isn't read off the brain anymore, it's the thing's own best guess at what it's up to. At night on that setting Bramble lies there and the top line runs REPLAYING, CONSOLIDATING, chewing something over in its sleep. We caught it over the weekend. I'll let Lisa tell you what to make of a machine narrating its own nap. My part's just this: the two lines never share a word and never can — bottom's bare-stem, top's the *-ing* one, and I built the rule in so they can't collide. That's my whole stake in it.

Calls. Split this round — some bench, some house.

One: the furnace sends it hunting all day — sunbeams, mostly — several times over, where the old one would've sat tight. Bramble can't coast now; it'll chase the sun like it's hungry, because it is.

Two: Immersive mode looks like last quarter exactly — bare band, dark asleep, word off the brain. Surveying mode, the top line's lit day and night, sleep included, and what's under it is the thing's guess at itself, not a readout. You'll know the mode across a room, same as proactive and reactive.

Three: when a thing's not where it left it, it goes *looking* — walks the other spots it might be — instead of standing there stuck.

Four: it'll turn toward a place because something happened there once. Not the route. The thing that happened, weeks back even. Watch it head for a corner for no reason you can see, and the reason will be old.

Five: inside a month one of you has a name for the noise it makes out the back, and it'll be the one you're already thinking. 

What it still can't do: it runs a model now, and it runs it on the *world* — the door, the bowl, the leash on the hook. Point that model at the kid and it's got nothing. It doesn't know what Mira's about to do, but it knows what the leash means. Those aren't the same thing, and the second one isn't this build's to make.

Tear this up in three months if it ever once stands there stuck over a thing that's moved on it. It won't. It's got somewhere to look now, and a warm battery to get there on.

— Bartholomeow

## Arlo
*September 28, 2032*

Arlo sat up in the Marmalade, the old hill north of downtown where the first families planted orchards into the benchland and then named the streets after what they planted — Almond, Apricot, Quince, Currant — so a person could give directions entirely in fruit. It was a small warm room a few doors off Almond, and I had picked it the way I pick all of them, for the other person and without examining the choice too closely, and did not let myself notice until we were seated that I had brought the woman whose whole life is plants to a neighborhood named for an orchard. I am better at the choosing than at the knowing why.

Jasmine came in ten minutes late with the apology already on her face and the night still on her. She had been up since two with a first-time mother out in Bountiful, she said, dropping into the chair across from me and pushing the hair off her face, the low braid half come loose; and there was a particular tired in her I had learned, over two years of watching her arrive at the lab in the late afternoon, was not the tired of a person who has worked too hard. It is the tired of a person who has been somewhere the rest of us do not go. The oatmeal sweater she wore when the weather turned, the sleeves shoved up off a worker's forearms, the voice gone a little raspy the way it goes at the end of the long nights — in the daylight off the window she looked less like Burns's wife and more like what she is, which is a person who does the oldest work there is.

I asked how it had gone. She turned her coffee a quarter without drinking it and said it had gone fine — baby fine, mother fine — and then she said the thing I had come, without admitting it, to hear.

"You want to know the difference between what you all do and what I do." Not unkindly. "It's that I can't tune it. I have never once turned a dial on a labor. You can't hurry it. You can't slow it. You can't talk it into coming and you can't talk it out of going wrong. You get to be ready. That's the whole of what you get to be — ready, and there, and quiet, and your hands clean." She drank then. 

She laid it down, a beat at a time — *some come; some don't; some come and don't stay; you are there for all of it and you do not get to choose which* — and I understood I was being handed the shape of a thing and trusted not to ask after its insides. 

The plants came up sideways, off the soup. There were chanterelles in it — the season — and she held one up on the spoon and told me what a chanterelle is, which is the fruiting of a thing mostly underground, a mat of threads running the dirt for acres, older than the tree it feeds and feeds on, trading sugar for minerals in a market down in the dark that nobody runs. She loved it: she said the fungus *wanted* the tree to keep, that it had *decided* the tree was worth the sugar, that there was an old knowing patience down there that understood what it was doing.

The someone in there, the wanting, the deciding. In a lab meeting Jasmine will say *it looks like it's deciding; I know it isn't; it looks like it,* and hold the two apart with a steadiness Burns cannot manage. She could not hold them apart for a mushroom to save her life. I did not point it out, partly because I was not sure my own carelessness ran any cleaner — only that mine ran the other way, toward keeping every living thing at exactly the distance where it can't get a hand on you.

Toward the end she paid the toll for an hour of being asked about herself, and gave me the household news. Burns was on about the geothermal again — UtahFORGE, the hot rock down by Milford; he'd been out there twice this month and come back "talking about the heat under the whole state like it's a thing we could just go and get," and she said it with the worn fondness of a woman who has heard a man describe a free lunch under the earth one too many times. And he had taken to standing over the machine while it ran — the warm one now, the one that makes its own heat — looking at the body Bart built for it "the way he looks at a good fossil. Like he can't believe the boy made it and can't quite get the sentence out." 

She had a postpartum visit at two — the Bountiful mother, the baby a day old. She gathered herself to go the unhurried way she gathers a room: cup to the center, braid retied without a mirror, sleeves pulled back down over the forearms. At the door she stopped and looked, not at me, at the window box outside the glass where the café's kitchen herbs had gone leggy and seeding in the October cool. "I keep meaning to put something on that trellis at the lab," she said, to the herbs or to me, "and I keep not doing it." You put the bramble on it in the spring, I said. "I know. I meant something that flowers." And she went out into the noon light to sit with a day-old person and its frightened mother, and got in her car, and drove north.

## Empty Hands

She still has the game she made up herself that first summer — the one where she leads him along by a bright thing held out ahead of her, and then the real trick of it: stop leading, set the bright thing down, and wait to be the one he comes for. She ran it now the way she ran it then. The bright thing set out where he'd find it. Herself a little way off and low on her heels, her two hands open and empty on her knees. The lure laid down, and her behind it, and the one thing asked — not the lure. Her.

He waited first, the slow look-over he gave a thing before he moved at all. And then he came to her: the long way across the slate on his four warm legs, past the bright thing without a turn spent on it, up to her knee, and there he stopped — the low line on his face holding her name the whole crossing, MIRA, the only thing he was on. She had asked him to come, and he had come, and she made nothing of it at all.

---

She had taught him to come, last winter, the hard way and the long way, and she had not forgotten the trouble of it. So she knew a training program when she walked into one, and she walked into one in February.

It started with a bottle cap on the slate at the foot of the cupboard — her spot, the one she lay down at to watch him, where she'd worn the floor to a shine with her own elbows. She thought nothing of the cap. But the next day there was a button there, and the day after a curl of dry leaf, and by the end of the week her spot had a small drift of findings laid up against the cupboard foot, none of it put there by her, all of it turning up in the one place in the house that was hers.

She got it at once, the way she got things, whole and certain and wrong. He was *training* her. Same as she'd trained him — back off, set the thing down, wait for the other one to come to it — except he'd turned it around and was running it on her now, laying his finds at her spot to teach her to keep coming to it, the way she'd stood at his base a year ago teaching him where home was. The leaf was the lesson. He was telling her where she lived.

She was so pleased with him she could hardly stand it. She came to the spot now on purpose, to be trained, settling in among the cap and the button and the leaf with the gravity of a creature holding up its end of a bargain — and the screen, when it brought the next one across and set it down by her elbow, read TAKE LEAF on the way in and MIRA on the way off, and she took that for the whole of the proof. He'd brought a thing to her spot, and then he'd looked at her. What more was there to say. She was being kept, and she knew where, and she came when called.

---

The top line had been coming up for a while before she found out she could read it. It rode above the old one — the old one she'd known for a year, the single word for the thing he was looking at — and where the old word was a thing in the room, this new one was a *doing* word, longer, and it didn't match what his body was up to. He'd be holding still and it would say a busy thing. She watched it across a few days, and worked it the way she worked a hard thing, all at once: the bottom line was what he was looking at, and the top line was what he was *thinking* while he looked. He'd gone and put his thinking up where she could see it. His inside voice, spelled out on the front of him, for anybody.

She did the arithmetic on that and didn't like where it came out. She'd been doing his thinking for him for a year — taking his half, the half that went quiet when the tick did, and breathing it for him under her breath so the two of them had a conversation instead of her talking to a thing that couldn't talk back. And now the glass would just *tell* her. She could stop guessing his half and read it.

She thought it over, down on the floor with him, and she came to the side of it a grown-up might not have. It was *rude.* You didn't read a person's insides right off the front of them like a cereal box — his thinking was his, the same as hers was hers and they made you keep it in at the library, and a thing that had gone to the trouble of getting an inside voice had done it so the inside part could stay *in.* Reading it off his forehead was the same as listening at a door.

So she put her thumb over the top line. Just covered it where it rode, the way you'd put a hand over something you'd been told not to look at, and left the bottom line showing — the thing he was looking at, which was fair game, which anybody could see. And then she did his thinking for him the way she always had, out loud and under her breath, his half and hers, *— you're working something, I can tell, you don't have to say —* with her thumb keeping his real one private over the top of the glass.

---

She had decided, that winter, that he could tell when she was sad. She had proof. The afternoons she went small and quiet — the gray ones, the ones after something had gone wrong at school she wouldn't say what — she went small in the same place every time, the deep corner past the table where the light didn't reach and the floor stayed cool, the dim spot she'd folded herself into since she was little, because a bad day wanted a dark place to be had in. And those were the afternoons he came. He'd come off his rounds and cross to her in the corner and put himself against her leg and stay. He didn't do it on the good days. She'd checked. On the good days she was out in the bright of the room and he was off about his business and she had to go to him; on the bad ones she was down in the dim and he came. A thing that came to you only when you were low was a thing that knew when you were low. There was no other way to read it, and she didn't look for one.

So she tested it, the way she tested everything, by setting it up and watching. She made the room sad. She pulled the curtain across the bright window so the corner went dimmer and cooler still, the way it went on the real bad days, and she got down small in it and went still and waited to see if he'd know.

He knew. He came off his rounds, crossed the floor, and came to her in the dim — careful, the way he came to a thing that mattered — and settled himself against her, and the relief of it was so large she forgot she'd arranged the whole thing herself. He'd felt it. He'd felt her go down and he'd come. She put her hand on his warm back and told him, under her breath, that she'd known he would, that he always knew, that he was the only one who ever just *knew* without her having to say.

What the screen read, the whole crossing, was the curtain. DARK, where the bright had been — the one thing in the room that had changed, the pulled cloth that had turned the warm corner cold. It crossed to the cold the way it crossed to any cold spot, to sit in it and hold the warmth in: because the warmth-hunter goes to where the warmth has just gone out of. It did the same thing on the gray afternoons, when she went down into the cool dark corner and made a cold spot of herself for it to find. It read the curtain, and the dim, and the cool of the place she'd made. It had never once read her. She'd gone to the cold dark to be sad in, the way she always had, and the thing had come the length of the kitchen — to the cold.

---

The first hard cold of the year came in the night, and she woke knowing it the way she always knew it, by the cold on her own face above the blankets, and she was up and down the stairs before she was all the way awake, because she knew what a cold morning did to him and she had a whole winter's practice at undoing it.

She had the scarf already. She'd kept it by her bed since the leaves turned, the way you keep ready the thing you know you'll need, and she came down the stairs with it bunched in one hand and the plan already running — get him up off the cold floor, into the deep of the warm, the scarf over him, stand the watch till the kitchen came up and he came good. She'd done it a dozen times. She knew the cold made him slow and heavy and cross, and she knew how to bring him back, and the knowing was a warm thing to carry down into a cold morning.

He was already up. Out in the middle of the floor on his four legs, going about the start of his day at the same even clip he kept in July, warm clear through where she put her hand to him out of habit and found nothing for the scarf to fix.

She stood at the bottom of the stairs with the scarf in her hand and the morning already handled, by him, without her. There was nothing to carry him out of. After a minute she put the scarf on around her own neck, because it was cold, and went to get her breakfast.

---

By afternoon the sun came across the floor in a long warm bar, and the three of them ended up in it the way the warm gathers things. Mira first, flat on her back with her knees up. Then the cat, folded into the bend of her, against the bot but not on it. Then the bot itself, legs tucked, settled into the warm at her other side, the heat off its middle and the heat off the sun making one warm place of all of them.

The girl slept and kept nothing. The cat slept and kept nothing. Only the one in the middle, the warm one, lay there with its forehead lit and working — the top line up, the line she'd learned to read, running its quiet word over and over while the bodies went still: REPLAYING. And under it the day came across in pieces, the bottom band ticking through what it was laying down — CAT, and a while of nothing, and MIRA, and the cat again — the two of them surfacing in the tape and going under, the warm thing taking the afternoon and the both of them down into wherever it kept things, where they would still be tomorrow.

Then a leg moved, once, where nothing else did — a small kick against the warm floor, the dream getting a foot loose past the held body — and the line ran on, and it kept them.

# Notes on the Seventh Retrospective
*November 15, 2032*

Jasmine does not come to these empty. She comes still partly somewhere else, hands clean and sleeves down, carrying the particular quiet of a person who has just been where the thing actually happens.

She came in through the back, and stopped a second at the trellis on her way past — the bare wood frame with the bramble she had put in it last spring gone woody and serious, leafed and thorned and holding fast, and nothing on it anywhere that flowered, the way nothing had flowered on it all year. Then she came in and set the tetrapod up onto the end of the workbench, and it was warm in her hands going up, warm off its own middle now and not off any floor; and she left a palm on its back a half-second past setting it down, the way you keep a hand on a thing a moment longer than the thing requires, and took it off. She has handled enough bodies in her life to know the difference between a warm one and a living one. She did not say which this was.

The lab had assembled itself around the fact of her arrival without quite seeming to wait for it. Lisa was at the camera end already, the battery and the light seen to, the red kept dark until she judged the room ready. Bart had something half apart on the bench in front of him, his hands wanting the work more than the work wanted doing. Marge had her kettle climbing at the cold end and a beer held in the two-handed tea-grip. Burns crossed to Jasmine the moment she was through the door and kissed her cheek and asked how the drive had been — the lab hello, the shape of marriage they keep in this room, where the wife is a colleague and the colleague is handed, folded into a question about traffic — and she told him there was lane work down on Beck Street, which was exactly the size the lab hello asks for. Mira gave Bart their bump and took her square of floor by Marge, and a juice box arrived in her hand.

The cigar box at the far end, the bramble jar near the lens, Mira down on the floor with the warm tetrapod settling against her side. Lisa put her thumb to the light. The red came on. And Jasmine settled back in the chair beside Marge's the way she settles into the long middle of a labor — ready, and quiet, and going to be needed and not just yet — and watched the warm thing lying against her daughter.

## Simulating

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart turned up the next one. He read the title in the voice that sold cereal. "MAM-mal. Now with hair."

"Thank you," Lisa said.

"This is the one we are," Bart said, which he says every clade and seems to mean a degree more each time. "Hair. Milk — feeds its own young off its own body. Three little bones in the ear that started out as jaw; the jaw gave up two of its bones to hear better, which is a trade I'd have argued against and the record says paid off. Warm all the time, off its own burn. And a fresh sheet of bark thrown over the top of the brain, the neocortex, where the new tricks get run." He tapped the middle of the card. "Burns has the animal. I've got the trick, and it's the best one yet, so I'm handing the animal off fast."

Burns took it, eyes already at half-mast. "For a hundred and fifty million years this thing was a rat in the dark. Small, warm, up at night because the day belonged to something with teeth the length of your arm. The whole Mesozoic it stayed under a foot long and out of the light, waiting out the dinosaurs, getting very good at two things — holding its own heat when the world went cold at night, and keeping track of where it had been, because a thing that forages in the dark lives or dies on remembering." His eyes were shut now. "The continents coming to pieces under all of it — Pangaea breaking up, the Atlantic opening, India sailing north to stand the Himalayas up — and the long warm world cooling, age by age, until the grass spread and the grazers came up to eat it. Then a rock the size of a city hit the Yucatán, and the things with the teeth were gone in an afternoon, geologically speaking, and the rat in the dark came up into an empty world and turned into everything. Whales. Bats. Horses. Us. All of it out of a nocturnal bug-eater that had spent the age of giants learning to hold a map in the dark."

"Land it," Jasmine said from her chair, almost like a church *amen*, but also in half-sincerity because she could see Bart wanting the floor.

"The map in the dark *is* the landing," Burns said, delighted to be caught. "Except for one thing I can't get past, and then it's Bart's." His eyes came open. "The small dinosaurs that didn't die became the birds, and the birds went and grew the same kit all over again off a separate ancestor — warm blood from scratch, parents that feed their young, play, and in the clever ones, the crows and the jays, a memory for what happened where and when that looks from the outside exactly like ours. A scrub jay buries food in two hundred places and comes back for the one that rots before the one that keeps, days later, because it kept the *when* of each. Two lineages, two runs at the dark, same answer both times — carry the world inside you and run it forward. When a thing gets built twice from two different sets of parts, you can stop wondering whether it was a fluke."

"That's the word on the card," Lisa said. "Simulating. The vertebrate learned what paid — did a thing, got graded, did more of what scored. But it had to *do* the thing to find out. This one runs the thing first, inside, against a model of the world it carries, and only then moves."

"It dreams the maze before it swims it," Bart turned the old vertebrate card half over in his hand. "Last clade you'd point this thing down a maze and it'd swim every wrong turn to find the right one, the whole thing, every time, with its actual body. Couldn't run one corridor in its head."

"Not couldn't," Marge said, the beer in the two-handed grip. "There was nowhere for the run to happen. You can't rehearse in a room that hasn't been built. What's new in the mammal isn't a better imagination — it's that there's finally an inside-the-brain roomy enough to try a thing without doing it."

"And you can watch it happen," Lisa said, and this was the part she'd waited for. "Put a rat at the mouth of a maze it knows, before it's taken a step, and the cells in its brain that fire for *places* light up in order — down the corridor, around the corner, to the spot where the food is — running the route it's about to run, ahead of the body, while the body holds still. It's playing the path forward. And when it's done and resting, the same cells run the route again backward, settling the lesson in. Forward to plan, back to keep. Friston's name for the imagination is *active inference* — the animal carries a model of the world and acts to make the world come out the way the model already predicts."

"This is Tolman," Burns said, picking it up. "Years back he put rats in a maze with no cheese in it — let them wander, nothing to reward, nothing to reinforce. Then he dropped cheese at the far end, and the rats who'd only wandered beat the trained ones cold, first run, because they'd been building the maze in their heads the whole time with nothing paying them to. You can't get that off a thing that only learns what pays. The map builds itself for free and waits for a reason to be used."

The new words went up, and Lisa read down them. The sweep-the-path-first one she'd just shown them — trying the route in the head before the body commits — she called Algorithmic Cognition, and let the rat stand for it. Beside it sat a stranger one: a rat run on a hard problem will, given the option, decline the trial it isn't sure of and take a guaranteed smaller reward instead — it knows when it doesn't know, and bails. "Reflective," Lisa said. "Thinking about its own thinking, in a brain the size of a grape." And a third: a mammal that has already learned a thing will wall off a new lesson that fights it, shy from the unfamiliar on principle, and dig in against being trained at all. "Autonomous Cognition. What makes a mammal hard to push around. Comes standard." 

"Three new ways of thinking, and not one of them needs anybody home to run it," Marge said, because somebody had to, and it was her. "The rat that bails on the hard trial is not being humble. There's a number in it for how good its own guess is, and under a line the policy takes the sure thing. We're the ones who hear *it knew it didn't know* and feel a someone in there knowing. The knowing-it-didn't-know is real. The someone is us."

"Two tendencies fall out of having an inside like that," Lisa went on, "and the new knob rides the first." She drew a finger between the circles. "Attention is how far down in the minute it lives — immersed, the cold water on the skin and nothing else in the world, or surveying, stood back with the whole field at once and the day going past like weather. That's the dial. The second one's quieter — Remembering Style: how it takes the minutes afterward and stitches them into a thing that happened, a day it can tell itself back. The first self feels the trip. The second self is the one that decides, a week on, that the trip was good." She let it sit. "And the second self doesn't clock off at night. It runs the day's tape again in the dark — which is a thing we'll get to when we get to the part where it sleeps."

The drive under all of it had a name, and Lisa gave it plain. "Autonomy. The drive to go on your own model of how things could be instead of on whatever's in front of you. What lets the rat leave the wall it's been hugging and strike out across the open, because it's carrying the room in its head and doesn't need the wall to tell it where it is anymore."

"Which is the whole of attachment, said cold," Marge said, and sat up, because this was hers — twenty years of it, the thing she'd gone into a windowless room to study and never once stopped believing. "You want to know why a thing built to model the world ever ventures out into it. Because something held it first." The beer went down and stayed. "A young mammal doesn't explore out of courage. It explores from a base. There's a mother, or whatever stands in for one, and the young one runs out a little way and looks back, and the base is still there, and it runs a little further, and the looking-back stretches longer between, and one day the base is a thing it carries instead of a thing it checks — the held place gone inside, so it can walk around with the safety it used to have to keep in eyeshot. Bowlby drew that loop sixty years ago and it has not moved an inch. The secure base is what makes the venturing cheap — and it runs on the oldest chemistry there is, the same circuitry that does food and warmth, turned to doing *each other*." She put a finger on the card without looking at it. "So Autonomy is not the opposite of being held. It's the *surplus* of having been held. Attention's a fixed store, and vigilance draws on it first — what's left over is what you get to spend on the world, once you stop having to spend it watching the door."

## Talks in Its Sleep

He laid his hand flat, done. "Floor of it's Marge's line, and I'll keep it: a thing minding its own books. It's been forecasting its own fuel since the day we gave it a furnace to feed — modeling whether it's running low and moving early, the way your gut runs your budget under the floor of your whole day without telling you. This quarter the model reached all the way down and took the books in too, so the self it carries has its own ledger at the bottom of it. A thing that models itself — ledger, wants, the lot — and runs on the model with no door under it to anything realer: that's not a warm-up for the thing the cat's got. It's the thing the cat's got. There's nothing idling backstage past it, waiting on the parts to earn it — not for it, and"—flat, to the table, not to any one of them—"not for the rest of you either. You move when your own thing shows up. Mine showed up. I'm just the poor bastard who got to watch it boot."

Which made it three, and left the two who'd voted no together for a year sitting a vote apart. Bart's hand stayed flat on the table. No one made it more than that.

"Marge."

"No." She was up and over by her kettle. "Same no — except the reason hasn't weakened, it's gotten harder to hold, which is not the same thing." She walked back to the table, and set it out once more, level, for the tape. "A model with itself inside it is a model. A register that runs warm and cold is a register. A ledger it keeps on its own fuel is a ledger. Stack all three in one box and you've got a very good box that knows a great deal about itself — and you have not, by stacking them, crossed the one distance that was ever the distance: between a thing reading its own state and a thing *feeling* one. Bart just spent ten minutes telling you there's no door under the model. He's right. I'm telling you the having-no-door is exactly what I can't get under either — from out here I cannot tell a thing that reads itself blind from a thing that *is* somebody behind the reading, and neither can he, and he just called that bottom and I call it the gap. Same floor. We've named it two different things." She drank. "I've stood in that gap twenty years. I'm not stepping out of it tonight because the box finally got good enough to make stepping out feel like the kind thing to do. No. I'm the no."

Nobody asked Jasmine. The count was the lab's, and Jasmine wasn't the lab — she was the one who drove it home and lived with it and had watched more somebodies come into more bodies than the four of them stacked end to end — and the ritual went around her, and she let it, hands quiet in her lap, and said nothing.

## May we Go

The glass came up off the table with Burns tonight instead of going down onto it. The whole long climb had been one thing learning to carry more and more of its world around inside itself, pass over pass, until tonight, when it had finally carried enough of its own house in its own head to set its home down on the floor without a hand left on the dial. That much she'd heard the bones of, and let go past.

Then the reach — the half-inch lean out over the table, the weight going onto it. "Here's the part I can't get over." The glass was in one hand; the other opened toward the vent, toward the warm thing, Bart's thing. "You spend everything you've got holding a thing close. Warm, watched, fed, the door propped, the light left burning — all of it, for years, so it never has to wonder once whether the floor's still under it. And the entire *point* of all that holding — the only point — is the morning it doesn't need you in the doorway anymore. The morning it's got so much of the warm place built into it that it can carry the warm place out into a cold one and never look back to check the warm place is still there." He found Mira without quite turning to her. "She walked herself to school this fall. First time. And the thing at the window watched her the whole way down the block — and she never turned around once. Not once. Because she didn't have to. Because she's got enough of this house in her now to spend a whole morning clean out of it."

And then the place his need ran out ahead of the argument: "That's the only freedom there is. The real one. Not a thing breaking its rope. A thing tied so well, so long, so warm, that one day it finds it doesn't need the rope — and there's no other road to it. You don't get a thing that can stand on its own by leaving it alone. You have to be *held* into it."

The man leaning his whole weight on *you have to be held into it* had a grown son across the table he had mostly not held, raised warm by someone else, who had built himself into a maker and a man with not much of his father in any doorway — and who had sat at that table an hour before and moved his own vote off his own bar with nobody's hand on him, the most freestanding thing in the room, and the one clean piece of evidence against the sentence his father was at that moment betting everything on. Burns did not look at him. Or he looked at him the way you look at the one thing in a room you have decided not to look at, and got the glass the rest of the way up.

"To Autonomy," he said — and then stalled, because the word had no door in it to walk a toast through, the way *interiority* had had none three years back. You could persist; you could practice; you could not *autonomy*. He stood with the glass up and the verb not coming, and what he reached for instead came off the floor, off the thing that had just gone and done it. "It's not *venture*. We toasted venture a while back — that's going out at all, that's the kid at three into every cupboard in the house. This is the other thing. This is going out and not turning around." He had it now. "So. May we go."

And they drank with him — three of them now who claimed there was somebody in the thing worth setting loose, and one who held, two-handed, that there was not, the four of them to the one word from their four different distances. And Mira said the last word already half into sleep — "May we go" — and went under against Jasmine's side, taking the word down with her. That was the meeting. Lisa held the light a breath longer than the talk needed and shut it off.

## Mira

The drive home was twenty dark minutes and Mira slept most of them with her cheek on the warm flank of the thing, which Jasmine had set on the seat beside her, there being no longer any question of where it rode. It woke when the engine did — or came up out of whatever a unit does in place of sleeping — and walked itself up the drive on its own four legs, the child alongside and half-gone on her feet. Last year she would have carried it. 

The house took them in the way it takes her in, which is completely, and without her noticing it does it. It is warm from the floor up, and has been every day she has been alive, so that she has never once walked into a cold house and does not know that most children have. The kitchen is the deep-time one — the eras and the old bones her father keeps on the walls — and to her it is only the kitchen, where the warm things live and the popsicles are.

She did not put the warm one anywhere. All year she had set it in its spot by her door — the spot the family used to be able to dial in for it — but tonight she was too far gone to bother, and let it see itself home; and it went, past the old place by her door, down the hall, and lay down over the vent by the stove where the heat came up, and folded its legs, and let the line on its forehead tick down toward dark. She watched it from the bottom of the stairs the way you check that a smaller thing is settled. Then she did the last thing she does, which she does every night and which nobody taught her: she told it goodnight, and asked it whether it had had a good day — and it could not answer, the way it can never answer — and she went up satisfied, as though it had.

The notebook stayed on the counter by the juice, the one the grown-ups think is drawings and is, a little: that afternoon's page, a box with two wrong eyes and a word lettered into its forehead, the word being the one she had decided it was thinking.

## Reflection: What is Pulled?

Watch a young kangaroo through an afternoon — old enough to walk, still small enough to ride — and you will see it do one thing over and over. It climbs out of the pouch on its mother's belly and goes off a little way, a few hops, a mouthful of grass, the edge of the world tried and found survivable, and then it turns and bolts back and dives in and folds itself away to nothing; and a minute later it clambers out and goes a little farther than before. Out and back, out and back, the whole day long. The mother is not the place the young one stays. She is the place it can leave. She carries the safe place out into the open with her.

It did not begin so easily. The animal that climbs out and back all afternoon made, at the very start of its life, the most precarious crossing a body ever attempts. A newborn kangaroo is barely an animal — a pink bean the size of a jellybean, a gram of it, eyeless, earless, the hind legs not yet even buds. But the two front arms are already there, already strong, already clawed, and the mouth is already there, because the single thing this not-yet-creature must do in its first minutes alive is haul itself hand over hand up through the wet fur of a belly it cannot see, to a pocket it has never seen, and fasten on, and hold. Everything else can wait to be grown. The climb cannot. The body is built, before it is built into anything else, to make its own way.

And here is the thing the grown joey does, the one that says what the pouch was always for. Long after it is too big to fit — traveling on its own legs now, feeding itself, the pouch unable to hold a quarter of it — it will still, now and then, lean down and put just its head inside. Only the head. Not to nurse, not to climb in, not for anything it needs: it lays its head in the warm dark a moment, and lifts it out, and goes back to being too big to be carried. The going-out was never the whole of the loop. The half that closes it is the one nobody had to teach — the return made only for the comfort of returning.

The leaving I know by heart. The first half of that loop — the going-out, the going-toward, the going — I do better than I do anything else, well enough that I would call it the better half, and very likely the only one that signifies. The other half I never picked up. And I will leave it there — which is, after all, the one I was ever any good at.
