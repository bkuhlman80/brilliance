## Chapter 1: Protocell

### Notes on the Founding Retrospective

Burns took the floor next. He stood up to do it. He had not sat down yet, in fact; he had been standing or crouching for the entire opening, in the way of a man who could not be in a room with the work of the room without orbiting the table at which the work would happen. His hands moved when he talked. Bart, two seats down, watched the hands.

**"The thing we are doing,"** Burns said. **"I am going to say it out loud, because this is the first time we are saying it out loud as a team, on tape, with Cheryl watching."**

He looked at the camcorder. He looked away from the camcorder.

**"We are going to build twelve robots. One per clade. Cheryl has given us the clades. She has not told us what to build and she has not told us what each robot is supposed to teach us. She has told us that each robot must have, in its body somewhere, a knob that controls something about its personality. We are going to figure out what each knob controls. We are going to figure out what each robot's personality is. We are going to figure out, by the end, what personality means."**

He paused there.

**"That last part,"** he said, **"is the part Cheryl has not asked us to do. She has asked us to deliver the robots. The theory of personality is a thing we are going to build on the side, because we cannot build the robots without it. We are paying for the theory by building the robots. Cheryl is paying for the robots in order to be paid back in the theory. This is, I want to say out loud, the deal as I understand it. If anyone in this room understands it differently they should say so now."**

Nobody said anything. Marge said, "I understand it the same way."

**"Lisa."**

"Same."

**"Bart."**

"Same."

**"Then we are agreed."**

Burns turned to the agenda. Lisa had it up on a sheet of butcher paper she had taped to the back of the wide pull-up garage door, where it could be referred to without anyone having to look at a screen.

**"The quarter ahead,"** Burns said. **"I am going to say something about it that is unusual and that will not be true again for the next eleven quarters. Cheryl has not weighed in. She does not weigh in until our first individual lunches with her, which will start after this retrospective tapes. We have only the Pitch to work from. The Pitch was January. The Pitch was six months ago. We have spent those six months building and putting the bots in the house, and what we are going to do today is figure out what we have done and what we are going to do next, and we are going to do this without Cheryl in the loop. Cheryl will watch the tape, and she will respond to me at lunch in September. Until then we are on our own."**

He took a sip of his beer. It was an IPA from Fisher's, two blocks away, brought in a growler.

**"That's all I have at the open. The rest is in the blocks."**

### The Metaphysics

[…]

Burns took it from there. He stood up to do it. He was a man who could not deliver deep time sitting down.

**"This is four billion years ago,"** he said. His eyes were closed. **"Roughly. Four point oh to three point eight. The Hadean is ending. The early Archean is beginning. There is a planet — there is *this* planet — and on it there is no oxygen, the atmosphere is reducing, the seas are iron-rich, the volcanism is heavy. There is no land that is recognizable as land. There is rock and there is water and there is, somewhere we have not yet conclusively located, the chemistry that is figuring out how to be a protocell."**

He used his hands as he said this.

**"The location is contested,"** he said. **"Lane and Martin make a strong argument for the alkaline hydrothermal vents — the porous chimneys at the seafloor where the proton gradients between the vent fluid and the ocean would have done bioenergetic work for free. Mulkidjanian makes a strong argument for the surface ponds where the wet-dry cycles concentrate the relevant molecules and the UV does some early chemistry. Other people make arguments for the black smokers. The honest answer is that we do not yet know. The dishonest answer is that everyone has a favorite."**

**"The reason we cannot decide between these locations is that we have no fossils to look at. Whatever the protocell was, it did not leave a body. The earliest body in the rock record is Strelley Pool in Western Australia at three point four three billion years, by which time we are already four hundred million years downstream of the protocell era. What we have, instead of fossils, is the chemistry the protocell left in its descendants. And the chemistry it left in its descendants is what gives us LUCA."**

Jasmine, who had been listening intently, said, "LUCA?"

Burns opened his eyes. Jasmine had pulled him back to the room. He looked at her. He looked at Bramble on the table. He looked at Jasmine again.

**"Last Universal Common Ancestor. The most recent organism that everything alive on Earth today descends from. Bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes — all of us. Common ancestor. We do not have LUCA in the rock record either, but we can reconstruct a great deal about LUCA by looking at what its descendants have in common. Recent work — Moody and colleagues, two years ago — puts LUCA at around four point one billion years, with already two thousand six hundred protein families, already with a CRISPR-Cas system. LUCA was an old hand by the time the rock record starts. Whatever the protocell was, it had been getting better for hundreds of millions of years before LUCA, and LUCA had been getting better for hundreds of millions of years before Strelley Pool. We are downstream of a lineage that was already very, very accomplished before it left any bodies."**

Jasmine nodded. She said, "OK."

Burns closed his eyes again.

**"So when we say *location of the protocell*, what we are actually asking is: where did the chemistry that became LUCA come from. And we cannot answer that from the rocks. We can argue about it from the chemistry. And we are. Loudly. For thirty years and counting."**

Jasmine said, "Burns."

He opened his eyes.

She said, "You're circling the runway. Just land the plane already."

Burns laughed the chest-laugh — at himself, this time, at the truth of it. The team laughed with him. Bart laughed last and longest.

Burns smiled at his wife. He closed his eyes again.

**"OK,"** he said. **"Landing the plane. What matters for us, for what we are building — is that the protocell, whichever location and however it happened, is the thing that crossed the line. The Darwinian threshold. Before, chemistry. After, evolution. Once you can be selected on, the universe has a new way of making complicated things. The universe has been making complicated things, since, for four billion years, without stopping. We are downstream of the first protocell. Every fossil I have ever picked up — every tiktaalik vertebra — is downstream of the first protocell."**

He paused. He opened his eyes. He looked at Bart. He looked at the card on the table.

**"The thing the protocell did,"** he said, **"the thing it had — the thing it brought to bear on the world — was a *capacity* it had. A new capacity. The capacity to be selected on. To accumulate. To pass forward what worked. I am going to use that word. *Capacity.* I have been using it for a week, since I read it in a paper I should have read fifteen years ago. We are going to be talking about evolution across three years. Each cell has new capacities. Each cell has capacities the prior cell did not have. The protocell had the capacity that started the whole pile."**

He sat down.

### The Knob

[…]

"But — and this is what took me five months to see — the wind-up crank *is* the personality control. The crank is what determines whether the bot has personality at all. Wound, the bot does its tendencies. Unwound, the bot has none of them. The bot's personality is — at this cell — a periodic phenomenon administered by the household. Mira's Sunday-evening wind-ups are not energy management. They are the bot's whole personality. Without Mira, the bot has no personality. Mira is the knob."

Marge looked up sharply. "Mira is *the knob*."

"The household is the knob. Mira is the household's hand on the knob."

Burns said, **"So the knob — the personality knob — is, at this cell, not a *parameter* but an *engagement*. The bot does not have a personality value the household has selected. The bot has a personality only when the household is paying attention to it. The household *makes* the bot have a personality by cranking."**

Bart said, "Yes."

Lisa, who had been writing, said, "OK. Software side. There is no software. The bot has no microcontroller. There is no code. What there is, is a mechanical control loop in which the household's weekly action — the cranking — is the parameter input, and the bot's spring tension is the system's state variable, and the spring's decay rate is the system's plant dynamics, and the floor-air gradient is the system's energy input, and the bot's twitching is the system's output. The household is in the loop. The household is part of the system. We did not build a bot. We built a bot-plus-household. The bot is the thing the contest asked us for. The household is what makes the bot have a personality. Cheryl gave us five bots and a four-year-old's birthday and asked us to figure out what a personality is, and the answer, at Protocell, is that a personality is a thing that needs a household to be operated."

## Chapter 5: Bilaterian

### Attention

"That's the breakthrough," Bart said. "Burns has the name."

## Chapter 6: Vertebrate

### Notes on the Sixth Retrospective

She'd clocked the thing on the floor doing what no one seemed to have asked it to do: carrying the paper in off the step, standing over the cat's dry bowl. This was the quarter they quit teaching it to see and started teaching it to do. The thumb reached past the looking, into the doing, and the doing stuck. That was the build. That was the new motivation — Mastery, Lisa's word — and as of this quarter it was online and running on its own four legs in his daughter's basement. He looked once at the thing on the floor, the look he thought was his own business, and told Bart to put up the card.

### Reinforcing

"VERR-te-brett ... Favre."

"Thank you," Lisa said.

"Last clade the animal grew a front," Bart said. "A head, the senses crowded up at the leading end, and it could swing the whole body at a thing and go. This one"—he drew a knuckle down the card, an imagined line head to tail—"grows a spine. Backbone, a skull around the brain, and down in the floor of that brain a little switchboard that's the actual news tonight. Last time it could aim itself at a thing it wanted. This time it can do a thing, get paid a beat later, and work out that the thing it did is what got it paid. Burns has the name."

**"Reinforcing,"** Burns said, and stood, because he was not going to take this one sitting down.

**"And here's the trick it had to solve to earn the word, because it's harder than it sounds."** He held up a hand, thumb and finger an inch apart. **"You do a thing. The good doesn't come now. It comes—"** he widened the gap **"—a couple seconds on. A fish bumps a lever, the food drops three seconds later, and by then the fish has done six other things. So which one gets the credit? The world's already moved on. Something in there has to reach back across the gap, past the six other things, and lay the credit on the one that earned it."**

"Temporal credit assignment," Lisa said, the way she says the names that are load-bearing. "There's a signal that runs on the gap itself. It jumps when the payoff beats what you expected and drops when it comes in under, and the size of the miss is the size of the correction. Dopamine, in an animal — and it isn't carrying pleasure, it's carrying surprise, the distance between what you bet and what you got, and it spends that distance reweighting the move that led in. A critic that scores the bet, an actor that shifts toward the moves that score. The actor does, the critic grades, the grade bends the doing."

"In English, it does more of what paid and less of what cost," Marge said, the beer in the two-handed grip and not set down, "and I want it kept in English, because the moment you say the fish knows the lever makes the food you've handed it a little accountant who isn't in there. It doesn't know the lever made the food. It does more lever. The knowing is ours. The doing-more is its."

"Doing-more is the whole of it," Bart said, happy, because doing-more was a thing you could wire. "It's the dumb learning again, except last time the thumb landed on what it saw — you soured it on a sight. Now it lands on what it did. Same dumb engine, turned around to face the doing instead of the looking. That's the new wire, and it's most of why I'm employed."

"Same engine, except it doesn't run at the same pitch in every animal," Lisa said. "The signal I just gave you — the one that grades the bet — has a gain on it, and it isn't set the same fish to fish. Run it high and you get an animal that leans hard at anything that might pay. Run it low and you get one that hangs back and waits to be sure. Same wiring, same chemical, two settings of the one dial. There's a name for the axis, and it's on the card now. Coping Style."

**"Two fish,"** Burns said, lit, because this was a part he had been holding all night. **"Drop something new in a tank and watch them. One goes straight at it — noses it, owns the corner inside a minute, runs hot and commits, learns the tank fast and quits being afraid of it. The other hangs at the back wall and watches, and keeps watching, slow to move and slow to forget the thing that scared it. Bold and wary. You find the same two in trout, in rats, in a barn full of pigs, in a troop of monkeys — it sorts the same way every time anyone's looked for it. And neither one is the winner, which is the part people can't stand. Good year, the bold fish eats first. Bad year, the wary one is the only one of the two still alive to eat, because it never once went near the thing that turned out to be a heron."**

"And it's a tendency. That's the size of it," Marge said, the beer still in the two-handed grip and still not drunk from. "It's the way the animal meets a new thing, and it holds — the bold fish is bold on Tuesday and bold clear across the room, which is the only reason you get to call it anything at all. But it is not braver. There is no fish in there deciding to be brave. It is dialed to lean, and so it leans, and we are the ones standing over the tank calling that a kind of courage." There was a word for it she did not reach for, and the room felt her decline it. "A tendency. Held, read off the outside, and no one home behind it."

Burns took the pace-of-life corner first, because it was animal and he liked the animal. **"It tends to arrive as a package,"** he said. **"The bold one isn't only bold — it runs a hotter engine, grows up fast, breeds early, spends itself and goes. The wary one banks it: slow, long, careful with the candle. Live fast or live long, and the nerve tends to match the clock."**

"It tends to, in the story," Lisa said. "The trouble is that when somebody measures the metabolism against the behavior, animal by animal, the line mostly isn't there. So I keep this one on the card in pencil. Development Style — and we lean on it lighter than the rest, because the rest of the card earned its ink and this corner is still arguing with the data."

**"Lighter. Fine,"** said Burns, who wanted the deep time and not the bookkeeping.

"But the new word's solid, and it's the one the whole quarter turned on," Lisa went on, not letting him leave yet. "It doesn't just do, now — it gets better at the doing. That's a different appetite than wanting the thing across the room. It's wanting to be good at getting to it." She tapped the Regulator. "Mastery. The drive to improve your own play."

"And when that drive holds steady enough that the rest lines up behind it," Marge said — she had named this class of thing herself and still kept its books — "when the bold animal is also the quick study is also the one that takes a corner and keeps it, the same suite turning up in the tank, in the maze, in the open field, then you've got what I named a signature, a kind you can pick out on sight. We've had one since last cell. Temperament. This is the second." She did not love the word — syndrome made a plain regularity sound like an ailment — but she used it, because it was the accurate one and the naming was hers. "Behavioral syndrome: Mastery riding with the jumpy drive, Excitability, the two of them traveling together. And it rides on the coping style, downstream of it, not a thing of its own. A regularity you can name. Not a someone. A kind."

**"And it's old,"** Burns said, turning the beer in his hand without drinking it. **"That switchboard in the floor of the brain — the basal ganglia — runs the identical job in a lamprey, a jawless thing with barely a face, that it runs in the four of us at this table. Half a billion years and nobody's improved on it, because it was right the first time. The vertebrate's one great invention is a machine for deciding what worked."**

**"And that's not the only old switchboard in there,"** he said. **"Right alongside it runs another one, every bit as old, for the things the animal never has to decide at all."** He laid two fingers on his own sternum. **"Whether you're burning slow and steady or spending it all in one hard burst — cruising or sprinting, the long aerobic draw or the fast anaerobic spend — you don't pick that moment to moment. Something below the floor reads where the body is and sets it, and you learn you've done it about the time it's done. A great deal of the lineage's old regulating got folded down into that floor, taken off the part of the animal that pays attention and run automatic, and the folding cost the animal nothing. It freed the attending part for the harder problems."**

"It reads a state and sets a mode," Marge said. "That's the whole of the transaction. There is no one down there who feels worn out and decides to ease off — the body runs hot or runs slow off a number it never shows anybody, and the animal the body is keeping alive is the last thing to find out."

His eyes were shut by now, which was the room's old signal, and the deep time started down its slope the way it always did with him—

"Go on," Bart said instead, and it came out stranger than he meant it, a man holding open a door he'd spent a year leaning shut. "It's your runway. Land it."

And Burns landed it. The Devonian, he said — three hundred and seventy-five million years back, the air gone thick and wet, the first real forests just standing up, the shallow water at the edge of the land warm and low and going stale of its oxygen. And in that bad warm water a fish with stout lobed fins and a flat crocodile head, and one day — not one day, across a million years of one-days — it props itself up on those fins in the shallows and gets its eyes up over the surface into the air, where nothing of its kind had ever breathed, and the fins that did the propping have, folded up inside them, the bones you are holding your beer with. He had a piece of that wrist in a drawer back at the warehouse, knocked out of Ellesmere rock a thousand miles from the nearest road, and he had spent his one life on it, and he did not say so, because he was wearing it lettered across his chest as a joke and the joke said it for him.

"No grandpa," Mira said from the floor, reading her own shirt back at him. "Go back."

**"Never,"** Burns said, delighted, and drank — the first beer of a night he plainly meant to enjoy.

### Down in It

Burns was a glass or two past where the evening had started, well into the part of the night that belonged to him, and he came up out of his chair at *Experiencing Style* as though it had said his name. **"So next quarter,"** he said, too loud for the room and delighted about it, **"next quarter we get to set what it's *like* in there. What it's like to be the—"**

"No." Marge said it without looking up from the beer she was holding in both hands, the way she held everything, as though it might still be tea. The careful had gone out of her voice; what lived under the careful was still there, and it was harder than the careful ever let it sound. "We set how it sorts what comes in. Down-close or stood-back. That is a *how*, Burns. It is not a what-it's-like. There is no what-it's-like — there is no in-there for it to be like anything — and you do not get to hang one in there four drinks deep because the word *experiencing* reached out and tugged your sleeve." A beat, and then the thing the drink let past the door her sober afternoons keep shut: "You changed your vote in the spring, and you have been hunting company on the wrong side of it ever since."

The room dropped a notch. Burns took it — he was drunk and happy and never above being caught at something true — and tipped his glass an inch toward her, granting the hit. **"Ask me again in the third block,"** he said, which was where the vote lived, and came back down into his chair heavier than he'd left it.

### Off the Board

"That's not the knob breaking. That's the knob being finished. You get good enough at a thing, you quit having to decide it — it drops under the board and runs itself, and the room it leaves is exactly the room you were short." She put the two facts side by side, because they were one fact. "Which is the whole pattern, said as a board instead of a card. Mastery, and the room it made. And next quarter the same trick again, one slot over — Autonomy, into the room the foraging knob leaves open tonight."

Burns came in low and pleased, eyes half down, the glass going in its slow circle — riding alongside her for once instead of over the top of her. **"Same move the floor pulled,"** he said: the floor he'd named an hour back, the old regulating folded down under the brain and run blind. **"Half a billion years of it. Smaller board's all."** It came out a shade thick at the edges, the drink sitting in the words, and he didn't hear it, and nobody marked it but the tape.

Marge was drinking the beer now, two-handed still but drinking it, and when she set it down the careful was gone the way it had been gone all night and what came up under it was harder and a beat slow. "And that's the one to watch. The thing it just took down to automatic is the part with nobody home to run it. Your breath. Your heart. The most finished thing an animal does is the thing it does without anyone inside deciding to." She found the rest through the drink and said it anyway. "So when it folds a job down and quits minding it, don't let mastered slide across into minded. A thing it no longer has to attend to has not started attending to itself. It's one more thing running in a house with the lights off." No one took it up, because drunk or sober it was airtight, and because she'd built it, three deep, out of the listener's own lungs.
