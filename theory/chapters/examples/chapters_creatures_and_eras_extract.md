## Chapter 1

- The Hadean is ending. The early Archean is beginning.
- "This is four billion years ago," he said. His eyes were closed. "Roughly. Four point oh to three point eight. The Hadean is ending. The early Archean is beginning. There is a planet — there is *this* planet — and on it there is no oxygen, the atmosphere is reducing, the seas are iron-rich, the volcanism is heavy.
- The earliest body in the rock record is Strelley Pool in Western Australia at three point four three billion years, by which time we are already four hundred million years downstream of the protocell era.
- Last Universal Common Ancestor. The most recent organism that everything alive on Earth today descends from. Bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes — all of us.
- Recent work — Moody and colleagues, two years ago — puts LUCA at around four point one billion years, with already two thousand six hundred protein families, already with a CRISPR-Cas system.
- Every fossil I have ever picked up — every tiktaalik vertebra — is downstream of the first protocell.
- A thermostat and a bacterium share a wiring diagram. Both sense. Both compare. Both act. ... people describe *E. coli* strains as fastidious all the time, in print, in peer-reviewed papers, without flagging that they are doing it.
- In the gut of a four-week-old human there is a bacterium called *Bifidobacterium infantis* which has, for the entirety of its evolutionary history, eaten one thing. The one thing is a class of complex sugars found only in human breast milk — the human-milk oligosaccharides, the HMOs — which are too elaborate for the infant herself to digest and which her own gut therefore passes through untouched. *B. infantis* picks them up. It has a specialized cluster of genes encoding the enzymes for every linkage type the HMOs come in: fucose, sialic acid, branched, unbranched. Yong calls it the consummate HMOvore.
- What is striking, when you think about it for a minute, is that the milk has these sugars in it *because the bacterium eats them.* Mother and microbe have been refining each other across a hundred million years of mammalian descent.
- There is a bacterium that can survive five thousand grays of ionizing radiation. The dose that will reliably kill a human is about five. The dose that will kill *E. coli* is about sixty. The bacterium I am describing — *Deinococcus radiodurans* — takes the five thousand. When its DNA is shattered into a few hundred fragments, it reassembles itself across about twelve hours and resumes business. The people who study it call it Conan the Bacterium.
- In ponds and shallow sediments around the world there are bacteria that have built compasses inside themselves. The compasses are not metaphorical. They are physical: chains of small magnetite crystals — magnetosomes — strung along the inside of the cell like beads on a wire, oriented so that the chain's net magnetic moment aligns with the planet's magnetic field. When the bacterium swims, the chain pulls its body into alignment with the field lines. The cell follows the field down toward sediment, which is where it wants to be, because it is anaerobic and the sediment is anaerobic and the open water is not.
- In the soil of arid grasslands across western North America there is a bacterium called *Sodalis praecaptivus*. It is free-living. ... *Sodalis* carries the complete molecular toolkit for becoming a permanent insect endosymbiont. The genes for host-cell invasion are there. The genes for evading the insect immune system are there. The genes for the metabolic exchange a long-term endosymbiosis requires — supplying the host with amino acids the host cannot synthesize, in exchange for shelter — are there. Its closest relatives are already insect endosymbionts. They have made the crossing. They have lost most of their other genes, because once inside an insect for a few million years a bacterium does not need most of its other genes. *Sodalis praecaptivus* still has all of them. It is, in Yong's phrase, the symbiont-in-waiting.

## Chapter 2

- "A prokaryote is a bacterium or an archaeon. A cell without a nucleus. The DNA floats in the cytoplasm. The genome is small. The cell divides fast. The cell can take up DNA from other cells — horizontal gene transfer — which means inheritance is not strictly vertical"
- "The cell can pair up with another cell and exchange material across a small bridge — a pilus — and the receiving cell will be different afterward."
- "The cell can secrete molecules that other cells in the neighborhood sense, which is how bacteria sing to each other. They sing about how many of them there are. When the number is high enough, the song changes the singers. This is called quorum sensing."
- "The ribosome is the molecular machine that reads messenger RNA and assembles proteins out of amino acids. Every cell on the planet has them. Every cell on the planet has had them for a very long time. The architecture of the ribosome — the catalytic core, the large and small subunits, the way the mRNA threads through them — has barely changed since LUCA."
- "Last quarter Burns talked about LUCA, who had protein families. Moody's group found there were over two thousand of them, and they were all assembled by ribosomes."
- "The ribosome makes about one error per ten thousand amino acids. The error rate is set by physics and by the fidelity machinery that catches errors at every step."
- "State-dependent gating. Sporulation in *Bacillus subtilis*."
- "*Bacillus subtilis* is a soil bacterium. When the conditions are right, it eats and divides like any bacterium. When the conditions are wrong — starvation, dehydration, the world has stopped being a world that supports bacterial life — it does something... It wraps itself in a protective coat. It dehydrates itself almost completely. It dismantles most of its active machinery. It stops being a metabolizing thing. It becomes a spore."
- "The spore can sit in soil for years. It can sit in soil for decades. There are spores that have come out of geological samples that may have been intact for longer than that"
- "*Escherichia coli* — which I am going to use because it is the bacterium most of the literature uses — has on the surface of its cell a number of receptor proteins. Each receptor binds to one specific kind of molecule out in the medium. Sugars. Amino acids. Various small molecules the bacterium is interested in. The receptors are clustered at the pole of the cell. There are hundreds of them. The cluster is called the *receptor array*."
- "the amplified signal goes to a kinase called CheA, which is at the base of the cluster. CheA is the integrator. CheA takes the input from all the receptors and outputs a single signal: run, or tumble."
- "Pretend my whole body is one *E. coli*. My head — these are the sensors. The receptor array. They are reading the world. My arms and legs — these are the flagella. The little tails the bacterium uses to swim."
- "The chemotaxis system has two feedback loops at different speeds. A fast loop — the two-component signaling pathway, CheA phosphorylating CheY — that responds in milliseconds. And a slow loop — methylation of the receptors — that responds over minutes. The slow loop adapts the array's sensitivity to the recent average concentration."
- "Mitchell, two-thousand-nine. *E. coli* has two main sugars it can eat in the mammalian gut: lactose and maltose. In a typical human meal, lactose arrives in the gut first; maltose arrives a little later. *E. coli*'s genome encodes the machinery for eating both sugars."
- "When the lac operon turns on — when lactose has just arrived in the medium — the bacterium *also* turns on the mal operon. *Before maltose has arrived.* The bacterium is pre-activating the maltose-eating machinery on the strength of lactose having just arrived, because lactose typically precedes maltose in the gut"
- "The clade lives — for our purposes — across the Archean and Proterozoic eons. The earliest bacteria are probably around three point eight billion years ago, depending on which isotopic carbon-fractionation paper you trust. The first fossils we can hold and say *this is unambiguously biology* are at three point four three billion years, in Western Australia, at a place called the Strelley Pool."
- "Stromatolites. Layered mineralized mats of microbial communities. The communities are doing photosynthesis at the top layer, sulfate reduction at the bottom layer, and a metabolism — that nobody fully agrees on the details of — in the middle. The layers are growing on top of each other because each generation of mat-builders pins down a layer of sediment, dies, and is overgrown."
- "Strelley Pool was not the only mat in the world; it is the one whose record has come to us. There are other early mat sites — the Apex chert, the Pilbara generally, and slightly later, the Gunflint chert in Canada. The mats are the start of the rock-record story of the clade."
- "Across the Archean, the mat communities diversified. Some of the bacteria in the mats discovered... that water could be split by sunlight, and that the splitting could power the assembly of sugar. Oxygenic photosynthesis. The G-O-E, as you know, changed the ocean forever. The cyanobacteria, which caused the GOE, are like Neanderthal man — they are mark-makers, only the cyanobacteria mark has lasted for billions of years."
- "Cyanobacteria were not the first photosynthesizers — there were earlier kinds that used sulfide or iron — but they were the first that produced free oxygen as a byproduct. The byproduct was — for the first billion years they were doing it — absorbed by the iron dissolved in the seawater. The oceans, in the early Archean, were full of dissolved iron. Ferruginous oceans, in the technical phrase."
- "Banded iron formations. BIFs. The biggest deposits are in Western Australia and South Africa and Brazil. The world's iron ore comes, in large part, from these formations... The BIFs are the rust of two billion years of cyanobacteria dumping oxygen into the ocean."
- "Around two point four billion years ago, the dissolved iron in the oceans started to run out. The cyanobacteria had been producing oxygen for hundreds of millions of years; the iron sink had been mopping it up. When the iron sink saturated, the oxygen started accumulating in the seawater — and from the seawater, in the atmosphere. The atmospheric oxygen rose from essentially zero to maybe ten percent of the modern level. This was the GOE."
- "Great Oxidation Event. The atmosphere — and the surface water — getting oxygenated."
- "The first major glaciation in the geological record — the Huronian — falls inside the GOE window, between two point four and two point one billion years ago. It may be the first snowball Earth... it stayed that way for about three hundred million years."
- "The post-GOE surface ocean was oxygenated, but the deep ocean was anoxic and sulfidic — full of hydrogen sulfide from sulfate-reducing bacteria. We call this the Canfield ocean, after Don Canfield, who proposed it. The Canfield ocean lasted for about a billion and a half years. Most of the rest of the Proterozoic ran inside it."
- "The cyanobacteria were making the world *theirs*. The free oxygen that all subsequent oxygen-respiring life would need to exist — the oxygen we are breathing in this warehouse — was a waste product of a billion years of bacteria dumping their byproducts into the ocean."
- "The pre-Cambrian gets twenty minutes."

## Chapter 4

Not the sponge — a sponge is a co-op, cells sharing a building and splitting the water bill. ## Chapter 5

A head — the senses gathered at the leading end, all aimed the way the animal's going. And here's the thing the net could never do." He set the card down. "It picks a direction. - "The worm and the salt," Burns said, happy, because of course it came back to the worm. "A worm likes salt. Salt has always meant food, so it crawls up the gradient toward it, every time.
