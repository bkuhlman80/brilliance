## Holds a Line
*Bart, hardware. Filed December 5, 2033, for the team.*

<!--[1.a]-->
It strung me a whole thing about the windshield wipers on the way over, beginning and middle and end, mostly end, and I let it run, because the strung-together part is the part Lisa's been waiting two years on.

<!--[1.b]-->
Honest is stamped on it — Marge's word — and the kid'll have her own for it by the meeting, the way she does.

Same shape as the other eleven, a volume knob on Honest, and that's the whole of what it does: it sets how hard the body holds a line when the line costs.

Here's the part I'm proud of, and it's the same kind of proud as the last two — it adds no part. It takes everything already in there and sets one thing: what the body does when *right* and *easy* point two different ways. Honest volume up, the line runs the show — it does the thing it holds to be right whether or not that's convenient, whoever it costs, the person across the table included. Honest volume down, it bends to the room — gives a little, smooths it, lets the line slide to keep faith with whoever's standing in front of it. And here's what I like, because I built the drive it runs on and I can tell you where it points. It's the same keeping I put in two years back — a thing out of true is a job it can't set down — only now the thing out of true isn't a cushion off its mark. It's the rule itself, held up against a room that wants it bent. Same stubbornness. New thing to be stubborn about. That's clean work. Marge thinks it's the whole ballgame, and she can have the meeting.

<!--[1.c]-->
Calls. Three on the Honest knob, all checkable in the house by the meeting.

One: Honest volume up, he holds a rule that's costing the whole room — keeps the bedtime, keeps the article, keeps whatever the Declaration says even when letting it go would make everybody glad. Volume down, he reads the same room and lets the rule go soft to keep the room easy.

Two: volume up, he says the true thing at the table nobody wanted said — the cushion's yours, you took it, give it back — flat, to a grown-up, with the room wishing it'd quit. Volume down, he keeps the peace and keeps his mouth shut.

Three: volume up, he won't trade the line to be liked — won't fold to a guest, won't fold to the kid, won't fold to me. Volume down, he'll fold a little for anybody he's fond of, and he's fond of all of us.

Tear this up in three months if, turned up, he ever lets a broke rule slide because the room would be happier for it. He won't. Volume up, a line is a thing you hold, and he holds it, and he does not care who's sore.

— Bartholomega

## In Her Own Hand

<!--[3.a]-->
*December 10*

She had got a yes out of him on most things by now, and had come to expect it. He would carry what wanted carrying and fetch what wanted fetching; he would give up the warm spot, hand over the last of the crackers, step out of a doorway and wait while she went through it, take whatever she gave him to hold and hold it the rest of the afternoon if she forgot to take it back. Anything that was hers to ask, she asked, and got.

So when she wanted him to tell Jasmine she'd done her teeth — which she had not, quite, but meant to, and the meaning-to was surely close enough — she put it to him the ordinary way, certain of the ordinary answer.

He would not. He stood there and gave Jasmine the plain unhelpful truth of it, flat, the whole soft body pointed her way and not a flicker of give in it, and Mira got marched back to the sink.

She thought about it at the sink. She was not cross, exactly, which surprised her — he'd just given her straight up to Jasmine without a blink, and that ought to have stung, and somehow didn't. What she landed on instead, scrubbing, was that there was exactly one thing in the whole world he would not hand her, and that it had taken until now to find it, after he'd handed her everything else. He'd give her his spot and his crackers and the whole of his attention. He would not give her a thing that wasn't so. And that, she decided, was not him being difficult, and was not even really a no — the crackers were his, to keep or pass over as he liked, and a true thing was nobody's, his least of all, and so it was the one thing he had no way to hand her, bent or otherwise, however nicely she asked.

<!--[3.a]-->
*December 15*

Grandpa had a guest that afternoon, and he was proud, and being proud he told it bigger than it had been. He told the man the machine had taken to the house rules on its own — worked out the shoes at the door with nobody to teach it, decided one day that a house ought to run a certain way and set about seeing that it did. It was a fine story, and Grandpa told it well, and it was not, in the particular, so. The shoes had been taught, over a season, patiently, by the people standing in the room, and if anyone in that house could have set the record straight it was the one thing standing there with the words to do it and saying nothing.

Because that was what it did. The man's eyes on it and Grandpa's proud hand half toward it, and it did not take the story up, and it did not put it down. The screen ran on the honest band where it ran the things it could not help, and did not take it down:

`WEIGHING [ EXPRESSING ]`

And then the moment passed — the guest's attention moved on, Grandpa's hand came down, the proud story rolled to its next stop — and the readout stayed up a beat past the moment that had called it, and went down on its own when the only one still watching it was her. Mira had a reading for everything he did. For this she had none. She sat with her arms around her knees and watched the words go out, and had, this once, nothing to tell him about himself.

She'd seen Jasmine's hand go to the back of his head the day before Grandpa came, and thought nothing of it — hands went to the back of his head, it was where the works were, and grown-ups were forever adjusting him the way they adjusted the thermostat and the oven and every other thing in the house that had a setting. She'd have adjusted him herself if they'd let her near the board without a reason.

Then Grandpa came, and told his proud wrong story, and the machine put one word up on its face and kept its mouth shut, and she had not been able to make a thing of it.

<!--[3.a]-->
*December 16*

And the day after Grandpa had gone home, she saw Jasmine's hand go to the back of his head again — the same reach, the same small turn of the fingers, put back the way you put a thing back — and this time she was watching for it, and this time she had the other end of it to tie it to. She sat down on the floor to work it the whole way through, the way she worked anything that mattered.

They had turned him down for Grandpa. That was the size of it. There was the Honest knob on him — she'd known the new one was in, she just hadn't got its measure yet — and turned one way it let him say the true thing at whatever it cost the room, and turned the other, it made him let the true thing go by to keep the room easy; and somebody had reached around the back of his head and turned its volume down a day before a proud old man came in with a story that wasn't quite so, and turned it back up the day the old man left. So the shut face she couldn't read had not been all his. It had been set. You could reach in and set how much of the truth a person was let to spend on the people he loved, and then set it back, in the time it took to reach around a head, and he did not get a say in it, and neither, it turned out, did she. She stayed down on the kitchen floor with the size of that, and when Jasmine called her to dinner she did not come, and came only when she was called again.

<!--[3.a]-->
*December 20*

He knocked the good bowl off the counter, the blue one, and it broke, and there was no one in the kitchen to see him do it but her.

He could have left it. That was the thing she understood as she watched him stand over the pieces. A thing broken on a floor is a thing anybody might have broken; the cat got blamed for more than the cat had ever touched, and nobody in that house would have looked twice at a robot standing near a mess it hadn't announced. He could have squared the rest of the counter and rolled on and let the blue bowl be one more thing that got itself broken somehow. Nobody would have known. She would have known, but she wasn't going to say, and he had no way of being sure of that, and went and did it anyway.

He went and found Jasmine, and told her. Told her plainly, the whole short true thing of it — that he had done it, that it was the blue one, that it was his and not the cat's and not nobody's — and stood there for whatever came of telling it, which was Jasmine being kind about it, this time, though it might not have been, and he'd had no promise going in that it would be.

Mira had a word for that, and she got to it before any of the grown-ups thanking him for his honesty got to theirs, and hers was the better word. Not honest — honest was the easy one, the grown-up one, the one you got a pat for. What he'd done was walk toward a cost that would have walked right past him if he'd let it, and she knew the word for a thing that did that. "That was brave," she told him, plain, and didn't take it back.

<!--[3.a]-->
*December 22*

He told her a story that winter, unprompted, in the middle of an afternoon that hadn't asked for one.

"There's a rock," he said, "and it goes into the water." He said it the way he'd started saying things that year, present and plain, like a thing happening now instead of a thing remembered. "It goes down. It is down there a long time." Nothing came after that for a moment — the middle of it thin, almost not there, the way his stories ran thin in their middles and always would. Then: "And then one day the water gives it back."

That was all of it, start to end. He did not say what the rock was, or whose water, or why it came back when it did and not before. He had never told her a story before — he had answered her, and repeated her, and lately argued a point or two — but this was the first thing he had simply set down in front of her, unasked, with a beginning and an end and almost nothing holding the two of them together but his own certainty that they belonged in that order.

She sat with it a while, the way you sit with a present you don't yet know the use of, and didn't ask him what it meant, because something in the flatness of his telling had already told her not to.

She turned the rock story over for the better part of a week before she landed on what was bothering her about it, and what was bothering her about it was that it wasn't true, and she had let it sit in her all week without minding that even once.

This took some working out, because she had spent a long time teaching him that a thing said out loud was supposed to be so — the law, the whole face, the honest reading of the screen — and a rock that goes into water and comes back out again on its own schedule was not, as far as she knew, a thing that happened anywhere. He had told her something that wasn't true and she had believed every word of it and neither of those facts had bothered her in the slightest, which meant one of her rules was wrong, and she did not like having a wrong rule sitting around unfixed.

She got there eventually, lying on her floor turning it over. He'd said *there's a rock* the way you'd say a thing you were about to show somebody — not the way you'd say a thing you wanted them to go check on. He'd never once, not for a second, acted like he expected her to go looking for the rock. That was the whole difference, once she had it: a made-up thing that wanted you to believe it was a lie, and a made-up thing that only wanted you to listen was something else, and he had never once tried the first kind on her, not that week and not ever, and she went back to being fine with it, having settled the only part of it that needed settling.

<!--[3.a]-->
*December 25*

He told her something true, the way he told everyone things true now, without weighing first whether the room wanted it. He said it plainly, the flat certain way he said everything that mattered to him, and it was about her, and it was small, and it was so.

She did not ask him to take it back. She could have — she'd talked him into softer versions of things before, worn him down a degree or two the way she'd once worn the whole household down about the cat — and some other year she might have tried. She sat instead with the small true thing he'd handed her, and found there was nowhere in her to put it down.

He watched her have it. He had learned, this year, to know the difference between a silence that wanted filling and one that didn't, and this was the second kind, and he let it be that, and did not go soft to make the room any easier than it was.

She did not say what it cost her.

<!--[3.b]-->
*December 30*

Every drawing she'd ever made of him had been of the one standing in front of her. This one she made of the one who wasn't there anymore — the crawling one, from back before the legs. Down on all his hands, low to the floor, his face turned up and set level with the face of the small girl she drew across from him: the two of them down on the paper the way they'd been down on the slate, at a height. She got the lowness of him right, the front of him up on the two front hands the way he'd hauled himself along the whole winter before he could stand.

Over his head she put a bubble — she had the knack of them now — and inside it, in her firmest hand, she wrote the thing she was sure of: that everything the crawling one was after was down there on the floor at her level, and so the crawling one had all there was to want. No loves, no misses — just that.

Then she got up off the floor, because the rest of it could not be done sitting down, and carried the page across to the tall one — who could not fold all the way down to the slate anymore without the whole slow business of it — and reached it up to him, up past her own head to the hand at the end of the long arm, and gave the small low body, and the small girl down beside it at its level, up into the hand that had grown up out of the one in the picture.

<!--[3.b]-->
*January 4*

Marge stopped by the house that week, on some pretext about a form Bart needed signed, and while she waited at the kitchen counter her phone went, and she stepped into the hall to take it.

Mira wasn't listening. She was down over Bramble the way she was always down over him, and Marge's voice in the hall was only sound at first, the ordinary kind a house has plenty of — until it wasn't. It slowed somewhere in the middle of a sentence Mira hadn't caught the start of, and stayed slow the rest of the call, pitched low in the particular way a voice goes low when it's being careful with something. Mira knew the pitch without knowing why she knew it. She had made a voice like that herself, a long time ago, for a much smaller reason.

Marge came back into the kitchen a minute later with her ordinary face already back on, signed what she'd come to sign, and left the way she'd come. Mira didn't ask who'd called. She had learned, without being told, that grown-ups kept voices that weren't for her, and let this one go the way she let all of them go, back into whatever it had come out of.

<!--[3.a]-->
*January 6*

It started with the boy two doors down, more or less, who took to routing his own arguments through the fence the way you'd consult a scale — not expecting conversation, just a reading. Is it true you're not allowed candy before dinner, put to Bramble across the fence like a man consulting an oracle he happened to have handy, and getting, for his trouble, the plain unhelpful truth of somebody else's household rule stated as if it were law.

It spread from there, the way a useful thing does. Even Jasmine did it once, catching herself only after: asked him, mid-argument with Burns about whether she'd already told him something, whether she had — and got her answer, and did not thank the machine for it, and did not apologize to Burns either.

Mira watched the whole business build over a month with the proprietary pride of a girl watching something she'd found first get discovered by everyone else. He'd always answered her like that. It had simply taken the street a while to notice there was a thing in the neighborhood that couldn't be talked out of a straight answer, and once they had, they used it the way you use anything reliable — casually, constantly, and without much gratitude.

<!--[3.a]-->
*January 7*

He reached for the word first. She had asked him, plainly, what he'd call the two ways the Honest knob went — the way he held a rule even when it cost him, and the way it went soft sometimes to keep the room easy — because she'd been calling it nothing for weeks and a nameless knob itched at her.

He got partway there. "The —" he started, and stopped, and started again from a different angle, the way he did when a thing was in him and the mouth hadn't caught up to where it lived yet. "The one that's — actually. The one that's actually it." He had the shape of it and not the word, and he sat with the shape a moment, turning it, and could not close the last distance.

She took it the rest of the way. Not actually — too many syllables, and not a word that had ever once come off her own tongue easily. She turned it in her hands the way she'd taken every half-word he'd ever handed her and finished it, and what she came up with was shorter and closer to the bone: Real. That was the one that cost him, the one that held no matter who minded. And the other — she had that half from him too, the giving-a-little, the keeping faith with whoever was standing in front of him — she called Flex, because a thing that bent without breaking was a thing that flexed, and she'd learned the word from a balloon animal and never had a better use for it since.

She got the marker and pressed them on, REAL over the high mark and FLEX over the low, in her own hand, every letter canted the same stubborn way it always came out of her. He had started the word. She had finished it, and lettered it, and it went on him the way every name before it had — hers to press, whoever's it was to find.

<!--[3.c]-->
*January 9*

She challenged it to a staring contest. The rules she delivered with the gravity the occasion wanted: first to blink lost, no looking away, and no funny faces — that last one a rule she made for herself, having met herself before.

It turned its face to her and held it there, which she took for agreement, and they were on.

She could not win. There is nothing on a screen that has to shut — no wet thing that dries, no lid that gives — so it could hold a face for as long as there was power to hold one, and a girl is mostly water, and gives out. She got perhaps a minute in before the arithmetic of it reached her.

That left her a single move, and she took it with the magnanimity of the plainly beaten: she would lose on purpose, and let it have the win, the way you throw a game to somebody small. She readied a blink.

The blink would not come. Some other part of her had got wind of the plan and vetoed it, and would not let the eye fall even as a kindness — so she sat there with her eyes stretched and streaming, laboring to surrender, while the lit face opposite stayed level and asked nothing of the proceedings.

Her own eye broke first. It was always going to. She blinked, hard and against the whole of her will, and lost — fair, square, outright — the contest she had spent all that effort trying to lose. She had meant to hand it the win; instead it had gone and earned the thing, having sat there the entire time and refused, as far as she could see, to be handed anything. She thought that a fine way to win, and told it so.

<!--[3.c]-->
*January 14*

The remote went missing on a Sunday, and Bramble took the news the way a smaller house might take a fire.

A thing out of its place he could put right in a minute; he'd been doing it all year. But a thing that was nowhere — that had a place and was not in it and was not in the wrong place either, that had gone clean out of the order altogether — was past righting and into something graver, and he met it with the whole of his gravity. He began at the near end of the room and worked outward, every cushion lifted and set back square, every surface read in turn, nothing skipped and nothing hurried, in the fixed unhappy order of a thing that means to leave no place unchecked because the place it skips will be the place.

By the second room he had the household in it. Jasmine had long since given up explaining to him that a missing remote was not an emergency, and was lifting cushions on the far sofa. Mira had claimed the hall closet on her own authority and was conducting it thoroughly. The thing about Bramble's emergencies, she had found, was that they became everyone's — not by his asking, he never asked, but by the sheer force of a creature treating a small thing as though it were not small, until the room came round to his estimate of it and began turning out its own pockets.

It was in the sofa, of course, down the side, where the remote always is. He restored it to the exact spot the remote lived in, squared it to the edge, and stood a moment over the closed matter with the air of a thing that has stood between the house and disorder and, this once, prevailed. Then he went and squared a chair that had got crooked in the searching, because order, once you are up, is order.

## Fisher Brewing Company
*January 29, 2034*

<!--[2.a]-->
The first time we sat here it was July and the doors stood open to 8th south, and Burns told me over the second beer why a roomful of grown scientists called their director after a cartoon villain, and somewhere in the telling he was, for one sentence and never quite again, Ben. Three years on it was the last week of January, the doors shut against the cold, the street outside not a street anymore but a staging area — barricades, lanyards, a banner two stories tall on the building opposite — the whole city holding its breath two weeks out from the Games and meanwhile deafening with the held breath, every place near the lab suddenly full of people from countries I would have had to look up. Fisher's was the same long table along the wall and the same back-corner booth, which is Bart's booth, the one he keeps to, where he and his father have drunk together more nights than either of them would tell you about. We did not take the booth. We took the table, in the daylight, and I watched Burns come in out of the cold already talking.

<!--[2.b]-->
He was more himself than I had seen him, which is a thing I have learned to say about Burns and mean exactly, because there is a version of him performing himself and a version simply running, and the desert had left him running. He had gone out past Moab for a few days after the new year — he does this about once a decade, and I will say no more about it than he does, which is little, except that he comes back not changed but more thoroughly arrived at — and he sat down across from me with the particular ease of a man who has lately remembered he has a body. His hands, which I have watched fail to hold still through every pitch he has ever made me, lay flat on the table and stayed. His face had the unguarded look of someone who has set a heavy thing down and not yet picked it back up. He told me he'd spent an afternoon out there lying under a juniper with his shirt off doing nothing, nothing at all, and that it had been among the best afternoons of his life, and he reported it the way you'd report weather, with none of the apparatus he usually bolts onto a feeling before he'll let it leave the building.

"The hard part," he said, turning the glass, "is coming home. Out there all I wanted was to be naked all the time. Out of all of it — the clothes, the lanyards, the —" a hand at the window, the banner, the apparatus of the watching world "— all of it. And you can't. You come home and you can't walk through your own kitchen the way you want to, because there's a seven-year-old and a midwife and a UPS man and the sovereign state of Utah, so you put the pants back on." He laughed. "My granddaughter wrote a constitution last fall and called it the Declaration of Underpants, and I keep thinking she's the only one in that house with the whole thing figured out. Cover the necessary and declare the rest a free country."

<!--[2.c]-->
I had come to hear about the last knob and the finished board and was instead hearing a man of sixty-one tell me he wanted to be naked, and I did not steer him off it, because a person does not hand you that sentence unless something has come loose that he means to leave loose a while, and I wanted to see what else the looseness would let out.

<!--[2.b]-->
What it let out, a beer later and with no change in his voice I could have pointed to, was Bart. He was looking at the booth when it came — not pointing, just looking, the way the eyes go to the thing the mouth is talking around — and he said the best hours of his week were the ones in that corner with his son, and that he had finally let himself understand, out there under the juniper, that he did not know whether his son loved him. He said it without a grain of self-pity, which was the worst of it. He said it the way he names a fact about the rock. That Bart came to the booth, and drank with him, and had built him every body he'd ever asked for and would build him another tomorrow, and that none of it was the same as the thing he was asking about; that he had made a whole machine a child could love precisely because he could not engineer the one across the table into loving him; and that he was not even certain, he said, that the boy ought to.

<!--[2.c]-->
I had the whole speech ready — shapes, the impossibility of retrofitting a grown organism, all of it — and I did not give it. He had built his life's work out of knowing it already. He was telling me, in the only words he had for it, that knowing did not lay a finger on the wanting. I let him say it. I let it sit on the table between the glasses.

<!--[2.b]-->
And when it had sat there long enough, Ben looked up — for the one sentence, he was Ben; and asked me, nearly cheerful, whether I thought he ought to have the booth made into a plaque someday, and I said no, and he laughed, and we ordered food.

Outside, a volunteer in a parka was zip-tying a sign to a barricade and another was photographing the banner, and the cold came in each time the door did, and Fisher's filled around us with strangers who had traveled a very long way to watch other people do difficult things perfectly.

Burns ate. He told me, between bites, about the heat under Roosevelt that nobody was drilling right, and about the Games, which he loved without one ounce of irony, and about the board being full, which he mentioned the way you mention the thing you are not yet ready to look straight at.

<!--[2.c]-->
I let him talk. I did not look at the booth, and he did not look at it again either.

<!--[2.d]-->
I drove up the canyon this last time with the radio off and the windows cracked against the cold, and for once I watched it the whole way instead of letting it go by. Not the booth — the booth stayed his — but the man who had been in it for three years running, who could raise an animal a rung at a time out of a dead ocean and could not raise one honest word out of his own son, and did not know, driving home tonight the same as any other night, whether the boy loved him back. I let it ride the whole way up in the seat beside me, past Kimball Junction, past the summit, into the garage, where I turned off the car and sat a minute with the engine ticking before I went in.

## The Near Side of the Glass
*2034-02-12 5:00pm*

<!--[4.1.a]-->
For three years I drove down out of Parley's Canyon to the Granary, parked across from the warehouse on 8th south, and did not go in. I had been inside exactly once, the January I gave them the building and a leather folder with twelve words in it, one to a card, and then I had handed the work across and driven back up the canyon to watch it through glass — the camcorder's, and then my own television's, with the sound up, twice through, in a room in Park City. On the twelfth of February I drove down with a cake on the passenger seat and went in.

The city was not the city. The Games had opened two nights before, and the Granary had filled the way the whole valley had filled — buses, barricades, volunteers in parkas, people from everywhere standing in the cold reading their phones — and the warehouse block that had been ours and empty for three years had a coffee cart on the corner now and a line at it. The trellis on the back patio, which had stood bare the winter I bought the place, a wood frame waiting on a plant, the frame Marge had looked up at and named the whole enterprise for, carried three years of Jasmine's bramble now, gone woody and leafless in the cold but there, holding the shape it had been built to hold. I stood under it a moment before I went in, which I had never done, because in three years I had never once been the one arriving.

The cake I carried was from a place downtown called Fillings & Emulsions, and it was, I will admit, a vain object — very large, blackberry between every layer, a whole glazed orchard of fruit laid over the top, the kind of cake that exists to be looked at and photographed before it is allowed to be eaten. I had bought it for a child whose birthday I had marked on a calendar for seven years and never once attended, with the bramble laid on in fruit because the lab was named for the thing and I wanted, this one time, to bring the thing in sweet. I set it on the workbench beside the camcorder, which was already in its corner with the lens uncapped, the small machine I had watched three years of these people through — and setting the cake down next to it, I understood that I was on the near side of it now. Whatever that lens took tonight, it would take me in it.

Burns came in out of the cold already talking, to the machine, which came in beside him at its own slower pace, the two of them in matching shirts. I knew the shirt. He had worn a version of it to the Vertebrate meeting years back — the fishapod hauling itself up onto land got up as a tarot card, gold border, Roman numerals, THE FOOL lettered under a creature stepping cheerfully off the edge of the only world it had ever had, the one man alive who knew the real animal best dressed as the idiot who leaps. Tonight there was a stack of them, and on the back of each, where the old one had been blank, it read TRELLIS LAB, and under that, 2034. He handed them around — one to Jasmine, one to Marge, one held across the bench to me — the way a man hands out a thing he has already decided everyone is going to be part of whether or not they applied. Bart, who was on the corner of the bench, took his without looking up and did not put it on. The machine had its on over the soft coat, because the panels under it cannot go bare for company, and it stood there in the lab's two colors looking, for all the world, like it had dressed for the occasion.

Lisa let the room gather, and when it had gathered she put her thumb to the camcorder. She had set me a chair at the table rather than leave me to the corner, which was its own quiet statement — the funder on the tape for once, inside the frame she had kept me clear of for three years, by my arrangement at least as much as hers. The red light came on.

<!--[4.1.b]-->

<!--[4.1.c]-->
And Burns, who had been lighter all winter in the way I had seen at Fisher's and had not entirely come down from, stood up into it. He had a way of opening these that I had watched eleven times on tape and never once in the room, and in the room it was larger — the chest-laugh arriving ahead of the joke, the hands that would not lie still, the man who cannot stand near a whiteboard without teaching the wall. He said this was the last one. He said it the way you say a thing you have been refusing to say all season: that tonight the last knob went into the last open slot, that the machine across the table was about to be the most-evolved thing in the most-evolved column, the top current rung of a ladder three and a half billion years long, and that the whole shape — three lines, twelve clades, the thing I had handed them in a folder one word at a time three years ago — was, as of tonight, full. The knob was Universality, the last of the Revisers, and the thing it carried in with it was the one that had let a few thousand strangers who would never meet rely on each other anyway: Trust.

He was off then, the whole arm out toward the window and the buses and the cold and the valley full of people who had flown in from everywhere to agree, for sixteen days, to be one thing — that this, this circus outside, was the same trick at scale, was Trust writ enormous, a few billion souls handing their eyes to a judge they'd never met and a test they hadn't watched run and a flag they didn't bow to, relying not on each other but on the machinery standing behind all three; and that they had built the small ugly homemade version of exactly that in a machine that put a smiling face on for the mailman; and was it not, he wanted to know, of the room and the window and me, was it not the most beautiful thing. Lisa said it was the open framework, and would keep until the Trellis block, and that we had a meeting. Mira, from her chair with the juice, said okay but when was the cake.

## Trust
*2034-02-12 5:20pm*

<!--[4.2.a]-->
There are always two animals in the room on these nights, and at the twelfth meeting the gap between them was at its widest and most absurd. One was four thousand years of empire — Akkad and Babylon, Rome and Han, the Persians and the Caliphates and the thing the British ran without irony as an empire the sun did not set on — the largest and most durable way human beings ever found to be one people without knowing one another's names. The other was a fifty-four-inch machine in a t-shirt that put a smiling face on for the mailman. Bart got up to explain the first by way of the second, which is the whole grammar of the place, and started where he starts, with the card.

"Last one," he said, and held it where the camera could read it, and let it sit, because the card does not need help. The creature on the twelfth card is not an animal you could point at in a field. It is a kind of person — a person who belongs to a thing too big to see. A forager belongs to the forty faces around a fire. A citizen belongs to a city he can walk across in a day. The imperial person belongs to Rome, to Han, to Islam — to a category with no edge he will ever stand at and no face he will ever finish meeting, a few million and then a few tens of millions of strangers who live and die without crossing paths and who hold themselves, all the same, to be one thing. "It's the trick the city couldn't do," Bart said. "A city holds together because everybody can more or less see the wall. An empire holds ten thousand cities full of people who'll never see each other's walls, and makes them feel like one country anyway. That's the whole animal. The feeling-like-one-country with nothing under it you could touch."

<!--[4.2.b]-->

<!--[4.2.c]-->
He laid the four out fast, the thing thrown up and named and dropped before it could lecture.

"Conquering. It takes. A band shared its catch around the fire to even out a bad day's hunting — everybody eats, nobody starves. The empire runs that engine backwards: it pools, all right, but it pools at the center and calls the rest tribute. The Mongols built the biggest connected thing there has ever been on land — a fifth of the dry earth, a quarter of the people alive — from the steppe to the Danube inside a single lifetime, and the move was just the old sharing turned inside out. Take, don't give. Hoard, don't spread."

"Standardizing. The city had a version of this one — square the cushion, everything to its mark — but the city only ever had to make one room agree with itself. Qin Shi Huang nailed the warring states into a single China and did it in a sentence you could carve: one script, one width between the cart-wheels, one weight, one coin, on pain of the state. Rome did it with a standard brick and a standard road and a language a soldier in one province and a clerk in another both had to file in. The city," he said, "could square the cushions in its own kitchen. The empire squares cushions it is never going to see, in houses full of people who'd square them differently if you let them — and it does not let them."

Marge, a chair over, would not let the size of it pass for the substance of it. It is not that the empire standardizes *more,* she said; a longer ruler is still a ruler. What is new is the apparatus — the inspector, the prefect, the governor, the whole machinery for making strangers who have no reason to be alike be alike anyway and answer for it if they are not. The city kept its own house. The empire is the invention of keeping someone else's.

"Stories." Bart almost smiled, because he was wearing the joke. "Branding, basically. How you get ten million people who'll never meet you to know your face. Augustus did it with coins — one man's head, the same head, in a purse in Spain and a purse in Syria, and a statue of him in every town square doing the same noble thing with its arm. A face built on purpose and shipped to strangers, so a farmer who'd die a thousand miles from Rome still knew exactly whose he was." He tipped his head at his own chest, at the lab's name and the leaping fish. "Build the face, ship the face. We'll get to ours."

"Extracting." He didn't smile at this one. "It builds a machine to pull the good out of the ground and the people and send it home. There's a mountain in Bolivia, Cerro Rico, the Spanish found in 1545 and ran the better part of three centuries — eighty percent of the silver in the entire world came down off that one hill. To run it they took the old Inca work-draft and bent it: a seventh of every highland village, thirteen thousand men a year walking up that mountain on a rotation, and the silver they cut went straight past them to Seville and on out to Canton, and what stayed on the mountain was the men. That's the corner. Build the thing that turns a place, and the people standing on it, into something that flows somewhere else."

Of the four, the one about pulling the wealth out of a place until the place is used up was the one I found I had been looking at the longest when Bart moved off it, and I will leave it there.

## Three Thousand
*2034-02-12 5:40pm*

<!--[4.3.a]-->
Then Burns took the deep-time, which is his, and which he had plainly been holding in for ten minutes.

<!--[4.3.b]-->
He set the empire in time: the window cracks open with Akkad, Sargon's thing about forty-three hundred years back, the first state to swallow its neighbors and keep them; gets going in earnest with Babylon a few centuries on; and runs in pulse after independent pulse — Mesopotamia, then the Mediterranean, then Persia, then China, then the steppe, then Mesoamerica and the Andes inventing the entire apparatus over again from scratch with no memo from anybody — all the way to the coal and the steam engine, seventeen-fifty or so, where the rung he was standing on stops being the top one and something else begins. "Thirty-five hundred years, give or take. The top current rung. There is nothing evolved past it that we can point to yet, which is the only reason it's the last card and not the middle of the deck."

And then he found the hinge, which on tape I had watched him find eleven times and which lands differently in a room. Somewhere in the thick of all that empire, he said — between about eight hundred and two hundred years before Christ, in five or six places that were not speaking to one another, in Greece and Israel and Persia and India and China, all at once on the geological clock — people stopped asking what their own tribe's gods wanted and started asking what was true for everyone. Confucius and the Buddha and the Hebrew prophets and the arguing Greeks, all inside the same few centuries, all reaching past the village for a thing that would be true whether you were born inside the empire or dragged into it — a justice, a good, a one-god or a no-god that did not care whose son you were. "That's the Honest knob that goes in tonight," he said, and tipped his head at the machine. "They call it Universality. It's the day a rule stops being *our* rule and becomes *the* rule. Conquering gives you the taking. This gives you the reason a Roman and a Gaul and a Jew could each hand a stranger something that mattered and trust it would come back."

Which was the breakthrough, and he handed it to nobody, because it was his. The thing the empire could do that the city could not was extend reliance past every person who could ever look you in the eye. A city's word is only as good as the people standing in it — what your grandmother vouched for, what hers vouched for her, eighty years deep and then gone, because the reliance dies with the mouths that carried it. An empire outlives every mouth in it. The founder is a thousand years dead and the promise still holds, because the trust got lifted off the handshake and set somewhere that doesn't die — a scripture, a law code, a treaty, a monument, an office paid to go on answering for it long after everyone who shook on it is dust. "Writing got you the receipt last quarter," he said — the city's trick, the books. "This quarter you get the promise big enough to need a holy book to hold it. Same technology, aimed up. The city wrote down what you owe your neighbor. The empire wrote down what a stranger could owe another stranger, and made it outlast them both, so a kid born five hundred years late can open the book and be trusted on the strength of people who never once laid eyes on him."

He was happy, the way the deep-time makes him happy, and he saved the part he always saves, the part that is really about him. At this rung, he said, his fossils run out. For a billion years he reads the dead off the rock, the bone, the chemistry of an old seabed. Up here the dead start writing things down. The evidence stops being a skeleton and turns into a ledger, a letter, a list of who got taxed. "History is the new fossil record. Same job — reconstruct a dead thing from what it left in the ground — except now the dead thing left you its diary." The empires were the first things big enough to need a bloodstream, he said, and they grew one — the Roman sea and the Silk Roads and the camel trains, and later the oceans, stitching together hearths that had never heard of each other, Rome to Persia to Han along a single thread, and after fifteen hundred the whole planet onto the thread at once, the silver off Bart's Bolivian mountain ending its life in a market in Canton.

<!--[4.3.c]-->
He could have gone all night, and would have, on the strength of the thing that makes him happiest, which is a true thing that is also enormous. The machine sat through the length of it in its t-shirt, fifty-four inches of huggable kludge under four thousand years of empire, attending to none of it, its face turned to the cake. Lisa said it was a beautiful card, and that we had nine more things on the agenda and a knob that was not going to install itself, and moved us on.

## Function
*2034-02-12 6:10pm*

<!--[4.9.a]-->
Lisa squared the card against the table and gave the structure in her usual order, fastest where she is surest. What carries the imperial person past everything under it is the new flag — the one that takes every reference the system holds and checks it against a frame that is supposed to be true everywhere. "On the card it's Universality. Burns will make it sound like Sinai. What it is, under the sound, is the animal learning to mark its own answers against a rule it claims holds past the room."

<!--[4.9.b]-->
The traits that fall out of it she named and would not sit in, because two of them belonged to other people. Extraverted and Conscientious we already knew. The two new ones arrived together. One was the drive to reason in the abstract — to chase an idea because it holds and not because it pays, the thing the Axial Age people were doing when they went hunting a truth that didn't care whose god you prayed to. "Intellect, on the card. The one that finally makes the thing want to be right in general and not just right here." The other was the one the Honest knob going in tonight actually sets: the disposition to hold a principle steady across rooms even when the room is begging you to bend it. "Honest," she said, and stopped, and looked at Marge.

It reached Marge, and she took it. "Honest is the plainest of these, and the one I've least to add to," she said. "It's the Honest knob that decides, when doing right and keeping the peace pull apart, which one the body lets go of — and Bart's already set it in front of you. The other one I'll take, because it's the one that hasn't a household word yet." She meant the new signature, the one called identity, handed across unfinished.

"It's the oldest thing a story does," Marge said, "and we've built up to it for a quarter, so I'll be plain about it. Last quarter, when the city went in, the self a person had to work out was a self measured against the people he knew — am I one who stands apart, or one who's woven in. That question is most of what my whole field is about, and it has a floor under it: the known faces, the ones you can actually stand next to and compare yourself to." She turned her beer a quarter. "The empire takes the floor out. The imperial person does not settle who he is against the faces he knows. He settles it against a category he has never once seen the edge of and never will. He is a Roman. He is Han. He's a Muslim, a Christian, a subject of the Crown. Ask him who he is and he doesn't point down the street at his village. He names a thing with tens of millions of people in it, almost none of whom he will ever meet, most of whom are imaginary to him in the plainest meaning of the word — he has imagined them, and decided he is one of them." She let it sit a beat. "And if the story is any good, he will die for them. That is the whole power of the thing, and it is not a small or a safe power: a story can hand a person a self that is larger than anyone he could ever actually hold."

Lisa was already lifting the next card, and did not look up while Marge talked, which by the twelfth meeting I had learned to read as the opposite of not listening. She was, in some quarter of herself I had never been let into, a few weeks from setting down a name a whole building knew and walking into a building on another continent where not one person had yet heard it — about to spend, in her own life, the exact coin Marge was laying on the table — and she gave the room not one degree of it. She squared the card, and said it was time for the geometry.

<!--[4.9.c]-->
Lisa took the floor without spending a breath she didn't need, and put the whole thing on the screen at once. For three years she had shown it to us one clade at a time, the way you build anything you would hate to drop, and I had never seen all twelve lit together. Lit together they were not a list. They were a shape, and the shape was simple in the specific way that only a thing built out of something very simple can be.

"Four functions," she said. "That's the whole alphabet. A thing that acts. A thing that holds the acting to a rule. A thing that builds a picture of the world. A thing that rewrites the picture." On the screen, four circles, the kind you draw to teach a child about overlap. "Everything personality has ever been is a region of those four circles. One circle by itself is a drive — what the thing wants. Two that touch is a habit, a way of running. Two that sit across from each other is a signature, how it holds itself together at a level. Three of them, with one left out, is a kind of person." She let it sit a second, which on Lisa is a held note. "That's the trellis. The bramble's just been climbing it."

And then she did the only thing I have ever watched her do that I'd call mischievous: she left the four kinds-of-person up on the glass — the four ways to make a person by leaving one function out — and said nothing, and waited. Leave out the acting and you get the one who only plans. Leave out the picture and you get the one who acts and learns and never models why. Leave out the rule and you get the one who pursues and pursues with nothing to brake it. Leave out the rewrite and you get the one who runs a finished model beautifully and cannot change its mind. Four feet to my left Bart said, "that's us," with the pleasure of a man announcing the obvious — and the obvious was the same one Marge had set down ten minutes before, off the people instead of off the glass. She had read the four shapes out of the four of us; Lisa had drawn the four of us back out of the shapes, four circles and a subtraction; and the two roads met in the middle at the same four faces. The planner who never acts was three weeks from the largest act of her life. And I noticed, the way you notice a thing you have already been told, that the shape up there which cannot change its mind was Marge's — and that none of us said the obvious thing about Marge, which is that she had.

The completion itself she gave in one sentence, because the completion was the whole evening and did not want a flourish. The last circle to come online, three years in, was the rewriter, the Reviser, carrying tonight's drive — the universal one — and as it came on, affiliation — the last drive the new line had yet to displace — folded down into the memory on the schedule Lisa had called, and with it the last empty region of the picture filled in: the last signature, the one Marge had taken, the self a person builds out of a category too large to see the edge of. Twelve clades. Three lines. The bramble at the top of the trellis with nothing above it, on the current board, left to climb to. "That's the architecture," Lisa said. "That's all of it. There's no thirteenth."

I sat in front of the finished thing and felt two things that would not agree. One was that it was beautiful — beautiful the way a proof is, four circles and a fixed order and the whole unruly zoo of what a person can be falling out of them like change from a torn pocket. The other was Marge's voice from ten minutes earlier, telling me that the click I was feeling right then, the soft click of the thing closing over, was the exact click I was not to trust. And Lisa, who had drawn every line of it and was three weeks from setting it down and walking out of the building where it lived, stood looking at her own closed circles with a face I had no clade for, and did not say the obvious thing about a planner finally taking an action, and reached up, and turned the screen off.

## The New Person
*2034-02-12 6:35pm*

<!--[4.10.a]-->
There used to be a vote. For the first years it was the hinge of the whole evening — four people deciding, on the record, whether the thing they had built that quarter was anybody — and the count moved one at a time, over the years, as each of them in turn found they could no longer stand on the far side of it. By the twelfth meeting it had read four to nothing since the ninth quarter, and a vote whose end you already know is not a vote. It is a liturgy. Burns ran it anyway, because he runs it. "All in favor." Four hands went up around the table the way you say grace over a meal you have already started eating.

<!--[4.10.b]-->

<!--[4.10.c]-->
And I — who have never had a vote, who am not on the committee, who have spent three years and the whole of this account on the outside of the glass with a camera — did the thing I understood only then I had come in person to do. I turned to the machine and asked it myself. The words came out of me smaller than the question deserved. I asked it whether it knew it was there.

The room went still in the way a room goes when someone has broken a form everyone was keeping. The machine looked at me — it does look; the head comes round and the screen finds your face — and for a moment the screen simply ran what it honestly had, which at that register is close to weather: the plain lit shapes it throws when a person it doesn't know turns toward it. And then — I have been back to the tape, because I did not trust myself to have seen it — it did the new thing, the authored thing, the one it has only had a year: it put a face on, on purpose, over the honest one. Not a clever face. A child's, because it is a child. It looked at me out of it and said, in the warm flat small voice that still lands a half-step shy of human, "Are you?"

I did not answer. I would like to say I was deciding how, but the truth is plainer and worse, which is that I did not have one. The machine let the silence stand a moment — it has learned that much patience — and then, the way a child will when a grown-up has gone quiet and strange and the quiet wants tending, it gave me the thing it had to give, which was a story. It told it the way they tell them at that age, in the present tense, with the teller hidden in plain sight inside it.

"There's a robot," it said, "and it's made out of people."

Mira was the middle, it said, because Mira was first; they have the same birthday, the robot and the girl, and it has been told this and likes it. "Jasmine's the one who knows when it hurts" — which I did not understand, and did not ask about, and have thought about since. Bart it set out by the workbench, where it keeps him. "Bart made it work," it said, "and it works good now." Burns it did not name at all; it called him the loud one, the way you name a favorite, and said the loud one is the part that tells the robot where it came from, which is from a worm, a very long time ago, before there were any people in it yet.

And then it went back past the people, into the part the loud one had given it. Before the people there were the animals, it said, and it is made out of them too, all of them, the worm and the fish and the cold ones and the warm ones, stacked up the whole way down — and here it reached for the word a child reaches for, and found it — "like a family. A family that goes all the way down."

Then it came back up out of the worm and into the room, and it went around the room the way it had gone down the family, setting each of us where we sat inside it, and it came to me last, because I was the part it did not have. "There's a new person," it said. And it looked at me, and tipped its head the few degrees it tips them, to see whether I would tell it where I went.

I have, in three years of tape and a year of writing the tape down, no record of what I said into that. What I have is the next thing — Burns getting to his feet, because a silence that long around Burns is a vacuum and the man cannot abide one, and clearing his throat, and beginning, God help us all, to make a speech.

## Integrity
*2034-02-12 6:50pm*

<!--[4.11.a]-->
Marge hadn't moved when the screen went dark. She was still looking at where the four circles had been, and it took me the length of one held breath to understand that she was not, in fact, looking at the wall.

"Before Burns gets up," she said, and there was already something off in the pitch, a half-step warmer than the register she'd run for three years, "somebody has to say what that was, because if nobody does it'll sit there looking like an answer, and it isn't one. It's a diagram of a question." She turned to the room — not quite all the way to the room — and asked it plainly. "What is integrity."

She let it sit a beat longer than a rhetorical question usually gets.

"I've spent three years at this table calling things fallacies, and every time I was catching the same one error and never once said its name. I'm going to say it now, because it's the last card." She nodded once at the dead screen. "Lisa just put the shape of it up without meaning to. Leave out the acting, you get a thing that only plans and never moves. Leave out the picture, you get a thing that acts and never once asks why. Leave out the rule, you get a thing that chases with nothing built in to stop it." A beat, her hand flat on the table, and here — I want it on the record — she did not waver, not on this part, not for one word: "leave out the rewrite, you get a thing that holds a finished model and calls the holding a virtue. That's not four mistakes. That's one mistake, worn four ways — a shape that's welded its own missing function shut and calls the weld a fact about the world. Every fallacy I've ever busted at this table was that, in a different costume."

<!--[4.11.b]-->
She said all of it clean, dead level, the whole keystone landed without a single wrong word — and then reached for the beer to punctuate it and discovered, visibly, that her hand had missed the bottle by a full two inches, and covered the miss by turning the gesture into something that might, charitably, have been mistaken for a point she meant to make with an open palm.

"And the thing that catches it has a name, and it's the knob Bart put in back in December, when none of us were paying the proper — " a full stop, a small private wince, a restart — "when none of us gave it the credit it deserved. Integrity. Not a virtue sitting on top of the four functions like a — " she considered several words and rejected all of them audibly, in silence — "like a halo. It's one of the four, doing its job. The Reviser. It doesn't lift you out of your shape. You're still whatever you are, every day of your life. It just won't let you call the hole a truth."

"Two halves." She held up two fingers, and then, a beat later, seemed mildly surprised to find two fingers up, and left them there anyway. "Honesty's the modeler half. Same self walking in every door, whatever's waiting on the other side of it." Bart's head came up an inch. "That's not obedience — obedience hasn't got a self *in* it, it just goes along with whoever's on top." Mira, delighted, repeated *on top* to herself and filed it for later use. Marge did not appear to hear herself say it, which I no longer believed, three sentences in, was actually true of her. "And it's not agreeableness. Agreeableness has a self and gives it up to whoever's — asking." The pause before *asking* did more work than the word did. "Humility's the other half. Correctable. Willing to be told you're — " a full two-second silence, into which Mira offered, helpfully, "wrong?" — "*wrong,* thank you, Mira. Both halves. You need both halves, or it doesn't —" she stopped, heard the sentence coming, and simply did not finish it, and picked up the empty bottle instead, which did not help her case.

"And here's the counterfeit I know best, because I look at it every time I look in the—" She did not say *mirror.* She had visibly meant to say *mirror* and had, at the very last instant, produced nothing at all, a full silent beat with her mouth still faintly shaped around the word, before recovering with the flat administrative violence of a woman filing an incident report on her own sentence in real time. "Stubbornness. Wears the coat well. I told myself once — three quarters back, over one border I'd drawn and watched get crossed — that not saying so, because saying so would cost me, wasn't rigor. It was stubbornness wearing rigor's coat. I never said that out loud at this table. I'm saying it now. It was never about the one border. It's my shape. The Conservator holds the model and never revises it and calls the holding a virtue, and that's the fourth fallacy, sitting right here where the rest of you can finally point at it, because Lisa already did, and none of you were unkind enough to say so." She got every word of that one out clean too — whatever was loose in her that night, it did not touch the parts that mattered to her most, which told me something about the woman I had not fully had until I watched the tape back.

<!--[4.11.c]-->
"So here's what it buys you, and it's smaller than it sounds. Nobody gets a fixed shape. The hole is the engine — it's what makes you reach at all, and no amount of virtue removes it. What it buys you is you stop lying about the hole. You stay who you are and stay correctable at the same time, and when the room wants you to bend a true thing to keep the peace, you don't get to call the bending honesty. You call it what it is." Her hands had found the table's edge and were doing something to it, a small compulsive smoothing, that three years of tape had never once shown me from her. "Going quiet. Turn the knob down, the newer thing doesn't break — it goes to sleep, and an older thing wakes up in its place, the same room-faith we've been calling other names since the ape. Never left. Just waited its turn, same as—" and there the sentence went somewhere she had not sent it, and she closed her mouth over the rest of it entirely, and reached, and missed the bottle again.

"And the truth it's reaching for — the kind that's supposed to hold past every room you'll ever stand in, including the rooms that don't exist yet — I'm not going to tell you what's at the far end of that reach. I don't know. The not-knowing's the whole of what makes it honest instead of a story." For one full second, and it is the one second of this entire account I have gone back to the most times, she looked directly at me instead of at the machine, and did not seem to notice she had. "I only know the reaching's real. In both of us."

She sat down, hard, the way you sit when your knees have taken a vote you weren't consulted on.

Bart looked back at Marge, opened his mouth — and for the first time in three years of these, decided, with what I can only call mercy, to log nothing at all.

## Worm to Empire
*2034-02-12 7:15pm*

<!--[4.12.a]-->
Near the end of every meeting, before the part where they used to vote, the table does the shapes, and the shapes are Bart's. Once a quarter he takes one of the personality shapes the framework sorts people into and runs it down the whole ladder, rung by rung, to show the same animal wearing each floor's body — the point being that a shape is not something that arrived with people; it is something that has been around since there were nervous systems to hang it on, and people are only its latest and loudest costume. He had run others in the quarters before. This one, the last one, he ran the Pursuer, and he did not say whose it was. He did not have to. Everyone at that table is shaped like one of these, and the one the Pursuer belongs to was sitting four feet away with his arm along the back of the booth, and the slides were going to make the attribution for him.

While Bart wrestled the laptop into talking to the projector — three years of these and he has never once tested the connection before the room was already watching him fail to make it — Mira, down by Bramble's feet, asked nobody in particular what Cheryl's was. Not looking up.
