## It Hunts the Gradient
*Bart, hardware. Filed June 2, 2031, for the team.*

<!--[1.a]-->
Lisa's running the upgraded one back to the house this morning.

We upgraded one unit — 3, the one the house calls Bramble — and left the other four stock.

At protocell this thing only ever twitched: two kickers firing on their own clocks, and it'd hop around the floor like a bug with somewhere to be and no idea where. Now it *hunts*. Same two kickers — I didn't give it new muscles — but I put a chip in the middle that decides *when* each one fires. That's the whole upgrade. Fire them so the hops add up in one direction and it crawls that way; fire them so the hops add up to a spin and it turns in place; don't fire and it sits. Two idiots kicking on their own schedule became two idiots kicking on a downbeat. Still no wheels. Still hopping across the slate a hair at a time. It just hops with *intent* now.

What tells it which way to go: four little light sensors around the floor seam, each aimed a few degrees up and pointed a different direction. They all read brightness at once, and if they agree there's more light over *there* than anywhere else, the chip knows which way "there" is. That's the trick — finding a sunbeam with four parts that each only know one number.

The chip's an STM32, my pick, and it's the right pick. It runs a dumb little table Lisa and I wrote that fits on an index card: three states. Clear direction, kick toward it — that's RUN. No clear direction, spin a random amount and read again — that's TUMBLE. Stores filling faster than it's spending, sit still and let them fill — that's STOP. The screen up top shows which one it's in.

New since February, all told: two stores instead of one — a big slow new battery and the same little fast one beside it. Now the supercap dumps its whole charge at once and then fills back up off the battery; two solar panels down the long sides; the floor-transducers carried over from the protocell, still skimming warmth off the slate; a converter that takes whatever's coming in — sun, warmth, both at once, it doesn't care which — and tops the stores; the chip; the relays; four sensors; and a second knob next to the crank.

Won't work on anything soft — a rug would eat the hops — but the kitchen's slate, so: every sunny morning, watch it do zoomies.

It won't need cranking on Sundays anymore — the panels and the floor feed the stores, the stores run Bramble, it goes all day on its own. The crank's just an emergency starter now: it spins a little dynamo I added, so a few turns put enough back into a dead bot to wake it up when there's no sun to be had.

And I ran the whole signal path on relays — coil-and-armature, the kind that clack — so you can hear it think: a little clack-clack-clack every time it changes its mind. I like that you can hear it.

<!--[1.b]-->
a second knob next to the crank — the Effort knob, Lisa's word for it.

The Effort knob's the same shape as the crank — same as all of them will be. The crank you wind and it drains back down across the week; this one you set and it stays where you put it. All it does is pick which store Bramble spends from. Effort volume down draws the big slow battery and it runs even — a patter of hops it'll keep up all day, never quick, never quits. Effort volume up draws the little fast one and it runs in bursts — sits, fires off a flurry of hops quick as you like, then stops dead to refill off the slow store before it can go again. Aerobic or anaerobic is the mechanism under it, the way Lisa describes it; same bot, two ways to spend. The fast spend burns through a charge quicker — you don't get as far per drop in bursts — so leave the volume up and you'll be chasing a sunbeam sooner. Which way the house leaves it set is the house's business. I built both.

<!--[1.c]-->
He seeks — that's the whole upgrade. He hunts the gradient instead of sitting in it.

Tear this up in three months if he doesn't hunt exactly like I said. He will.

— Bartholomood

## Fisher Brewing Company
*June 3, 2031*

<!--[2.a]-->
I got there a quarter of an hour early. I like arriving early to a thing I don't yet know the shape of, and the first lunch with Burns was a thing I did not yet know the shape of. I had driven down from Park City on I-80, which is what you drive when you want to get from Park City to Salt Lake, and the drive had not been interesting, and I had not narrated it to myself. I parked a couple of blocks from Smithers on eighth south, walked past the warehouse without looking at it — the building is mine and I did not want to stand in front of it on a Wednesday at noon as if I were my own tenant — and went in the brewery side door at Fisher.

It was the kind of late-July Wednesday where the heat had set in by mid-morning and meant to stay until dark. I ordered a seltzer at the bar, took it out to the patio, found a table along the back fence near the food trucks. The patio faced ninth south, which had a protected bike lane down the side of it, and the cyclists were in summer kit, bare arms and bare legs, and there were a lot of them. I watched them roll past for ten minutes. The Wasatch foothills stood in the flat overhead light of late July, the grass on them long burned to gold — a high hard light that would not slant and would not soften, the kind of light that did not so much fall as bear down.

Burns arrived three minutes early. He came in through the brewery side door looking for me, found me at the back fence, came over.

<!--[2.c]-->
I am used to people being two or three minutes late to a first lunch, and over the years I have filed early people as either nervous or already in love with their work. I did not know yet which Burns was.

<!--[2.b]-->
"Cheryl."

"Burns."

He hesitated. He was deciding between a hug and a handshake. I watched him decide. He picked the handshake. I would have picked it too.

He sat down across from me. He had brought nothing with him — no folder, no notepad, no rolled printout of the call, no nothing.

<!--[2.c]-->
I had also brought nothing. I had meant to bring a small bound notebook with three observations from the protocell tape in it, written down in case I lost the thread of them, and had, on the way out the door of the Park City house, not picked it up, and I am not sure why. I think maybe I had wanted to come to the lunch the way Burns had apparently come to it, which was without props.

<!--[2.b]-->
"You hungry?"

"I could be."

"There's a truck that I have ordered tacos from on probably six occasions and have been satisfied each time."

"That's a recommendation."

"It is. I'd put money on it — I've eaten my way down this whole block and this is the one."

"I'll get the tacos."

"I'll come with."

We walked to the truck along the back fence. It was painted yellow with green letters and the menu was hand-chalked. Burns ordered two — pork, chicken — and a side of beans, and the woman at the window called the name *Ben* without asking. I ordered the same and gave my own name. We paid separately. We walked back to the table.

"You come here a lot."

"Bart thinks they hung the moon, and it's three blocks from the lab. The beer is very good. The food is fine."

"Honest."

"The food is fine. The tacos are better than most things in this category in this city. But food-truck food in Salt Lake is tasty, never great. I have stopped expecting it to be great. I come for the beer and because Bart will come and he's good company."

He had answered without selling me on the place. The first substantive sentence Burns said at the lunch was an honest description.

<!--[2.c]-->
I had not expected this and I found that I liked it.

<!--[2.b]-->
The kitchen called his name first. He got up and brought the tray back and started to divvy. Two for him, two for me, beans in the middle. We were missing forks. He got up again and came back with forks and napkins and we unwrapped the tacos and started eating. The pork had some kind of chili thing on it that I did not identify. A cyclist on ninth south rang a bell at another cyclist; the second cyclist rang back. I did not know what either bell meant.

"You walked past the warehouse," he said.

"I did."

"You didn't stop."

"I don't stop at the warehouse between things."

"Why."

I thought about how to answer this. I wanted to answer honestly without making it weighted.

"I don't want you to feel watched between things. The tapes are enough."

He considered this. He picked up his second taco.

"That makes sense."

"Does it."

"Yes. I would feel watched."

"Most people would."

"I'm grateful for the discipline."

"It's not for you. It's for me. If I started dropping in, the lab would become a thing I had a relationship with. I don't want a relationship with it. I want to fund it and let it become what it is."

He nodded. He ate. The light kept doing what late-July light did.

"How's the paper," I said.

I watched him register the question. Bart had told me at the Pitch that Burns was working on a tiktaalik vertebra, and that the paper had been *being written* for over a year. I had filed it.

"The paper is — exists. In a file. The introduction I wrote is the wrong introduction. It needs thrown out."

"When."

"Soon."

"Soon."

"I have been saying soon for about fourteen months."

"Where is the vertebra."

"On a velvet pad on a shelf in my office."

"How big."

"About the size of a small lemon. Maybe a large kumquat, if you're picturing a kumquat."

"I am picturing a kumquat."

"Then you have it."

"What's wrong with the introduction."

He set his taco down. He looked at me. I had asked the wrong question, which was the right question.

"The introduction frames the vertebra as part of the water-to-land transition story. Which it is. But everyone has written that introduction. The vertebra is also part of a different story — a smaller one, about how the load-bearing architecture of a vertebra changed as fish became things that pressed down on the ground. The smaller story is the one I want to tell. The introduction does not tell it."

"So write the smaller story's introduction."

"Yes. And I know exactly how it goes — I've known for a year. That's the maddening part."

"When."

"Before the next retrospective."

"That's three weeks."

"It is. Three weeks is real time. I keep treating it like a suggestion."

"Should I hold you to that."

"You should not. I will fail. But you can ask me at the next lunch."

"That's fair."

He had given himself a deadline and named the failure mode of it in the same sentence. He had not asked me to hold him; he had asked me to ask. He wanted to be asked. That was useful to know.

"What are you reading, then," I said. "Since it isn't your own paper."

"Geothermal, lately. There's a project the U runs down in Milford — drilling into hot granite to see if you can pull a grid off it. Not my field." He said it more easily than he'd said anything about the vertebra. "I read about it more than I read my own work right now. Which probably tells you something."

"Does it."

"Probably." He let it go.

I watched him eat for another minute.

<!--[2.c]-->
I had a thing to deliver and was not sure how to do it without making it weighted. I decided to just do it.

<!--[2.b]-->
"I watched the tape twice."

"Of the retrospective."

"There's only been the one."

He nodded. He had been waiting for this. The tape had gone to me back in May, and he had not heard a thing from me about it until now. I watched him try not to perform calm.

"What did you make of it."

"I made a lot of it. Most of what I made of it I'll say at the individual lunch with whomever it's about. But there's one thing I want to put on the table now, because it has to land at the next retrospective and I won't be there."

"Okay."

"Bart raised something at the close. His worry over Bramble having twelve knobs. The chassis-is-going-to-look-like-a-cactus, or something like that."

"Something like that."

"The team filed it light. You laughed."

He did not interrupt.

"He was right. The worry is real. The build is going to produce twelve knobs across three fast years and at some point soon the chassis cannot hold them. He has been carrying that worry for longer than the rest of the team has been aware of it. He has been carrying it alone."

<!--[2.c]-->
I had said the word I had decided in advance to say. I had not made a production of it. It sat between us.

<!--[2.b]-->
"I want you to take him seriously at the next retrospective. In front of the team. In a way the camera registers. I'm not going to tell you what that looks like; you'll know what it looks like in the room. I just want it on the record that you're going to do it."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I'm uncomfortable with the idea of staging."

"I know."

"I don't perform well to a camera. None of us do. If I try to do a thing for the camera it will read as a performance."

"I'm not asking for a performance. I'm asking for what you would have done if you hadn't laughed."

He took this in. He set his taco down again.

"Okay."

"That's it."

"Okay."

"Don't worry about it. Just have it in your head."

"I will."

<!--[2.c]-->
I picked my own taco up. I felt the conversation could now move to anything. He, I noticed, felt it too. The five minutes had passed. The push had landed. Neither of us had marked it heavily.

<!--[2.b]-->
The bell-cyclists were back, or different bell-cyclists. The light was the same.

I had been holding something since the taco truck. I took it out now.

"She called you Ben," I said.

"Who did."

"The woman at the truck. She called your order without asking your name, and the name was Ben." I watched him. "Over here they don't say Burns — you're Ben on this side of Eighth South, Ben to the taco woman, Ben the way she'd say it. At the lab you're Burns. On three hours of tape I never heard the team call you anything else."

He set the last of his taco down and sat back, pleased, and the flat light off Ninth South gave me the face the warehouse never did — finer under the weather than the craggy frame promised, the pale gray-green eyes for once not leaving mid-sentence.

"That's Bart," he said. "Bart named the team. I'm Mr. Burns." The chest-laugh came up a beat early, at the frame and not the joke. "Think about it. My son looks at his father — a man who signs the checks, runs the department, gets up in front of the room — and what he reaches for is the rich old fellow behind the desk. The one who owns everybody. *Excellent.*" He put his fingertips together and let them go. "He was right. That's what makes it good. He's been doing that since he was a kid."

<!--[2.c]-->
I let him have the laugh. I did not say that the cartoon he was delighting in was a portrait of a man who owns everyone and loves no one — that his son had drawn that, of him — and that he was holding it the way you hold a gift.

<!--[2.b]-->
"And the others?"

"Oh —" and here he did the thing I would think about on the drive home, which was that he waved it off. "Bart and Lisa, that's an old story, theirs, from before the lab — they were a thing once, it's where the bug started, I never got the whole of it. Marge looked like Marge. Smithers because there's no Smithers." He was already coming back to the part he liked. "But Mr. Burns. He got me in one move."

So I asked about Bart, because I wanted to watch him do it. He did it for the better part of ten minutes. He had no idea he was doing it.

He told me Bart had the best hands on the team. He told me Bart was the one who finished — that Lisa would design something elegant and Bart would build the thing that survived contact, that you could hand the kid a problem on a Tuesday and it would still be getting solved Sunday night because he did not stop. He told me about the twelve-knob worry, and he told it now the way I had asked him to take it — seriously, as evidence — except the evidence it was in service of had quietly become *look how much he cares about the work.* Reliable. Diligent. The best hands in thirty years.

He did not, in ten minutes, tell me one thing about what Bart was *like.* Not a fear, not a pleasure, not a private joke, not a way of being in a room when the room wasn't watching. Everything he said about his son was a thing his son could *do.* He was describing a remarkable instrument, warmly, at length, and he had the whole inventory and not the man — had filled the distance where the man should be with a list of his competencies, and did not know that was what the list was, and would have been hurt past saying if I'd told him.

<!--[2.c]-->
I was the right person to have seen it and the wrong person to do anything with it, and I knew why: I can read Bart whole off a secondhand inventory because I have spent thirty years at exactly this distance from everyone, watching instead of standing in it. The clarity and the distance are one organ. I made my peace with the trade a long time ago. Or I say I have.

<!--[2.b]-->
Once, near the end, he started to say "I think he —" and stopped, and for half a second something opened in the weathered face: I could see him hoping I would hand him the rest of the sentence, would tell him the one thing I could not possibly know, that the son loved him. Then he closed it over. "He puts up with his old man," he said, and laughed, and turned the glass. It was a good recovery. Almost anyone would have missed it.

<!--[2.c]-->
I did not miss it. I also did not take it. I picked at the beans.

<!--[2.b]-->
He felt the quiet and moved us off it himself.

"My department is in some trouble," he said.

"Tell me."

"The university rebranded from Westminster College to Westminster University in twenty-three. Defensive move — small private liberal-arts schools are under structural pressure nationally and the trustees thought a name change might shift things. It hasn't shifted things. The dean has started making sounds about my research space. About my grants stream. We're not central to the university's brand and we know it."

"Are you worried."

"I'm matter-of-fact. The slow erosion under us is a thing I expect. I don't yet think it's going to take me out before the commission closes. But I notice it."

"When would you know."

"Probably by spring. The dean has to make decisions by the budget cycle."

"If they cut you, what do you do."

"I have savings. I keep showing up at Trellis. The work is the work. If I need teaching, I find teaching. We'd be tight. We wouldn't be in trouble."

"Okay."

He nodded. He had not been seeking reassurance; he had been giving me information. I liked that he had not been seeking reassurance.

"I have a question for you that I have been carrying since the Pitch."

"Go."

"You are writing a book about this."

"Yes."

"Twelve chapters. One per clade."

"Yes."

"The team has been wondering."

"The team is right."

"OK."

He thought for a moment.

"I won't tell them you confirmed it."

"Tell them what you want. The book is a book. I am writing it. The commission is the commission. The two are not the same thing, exactly, but they share most of their content. The team can know."

"OK."

<!--[2.c]-->
I let it sit for a beat. I had not expected him to ask; I had also not been surprised when he did.

<!--[2.b]-->
"Tell me about Mira."

"Mira."

"Mira."

He smiled. He had not smiled yet at the lunch. The smile was small and complete.

"Mira is — being four. She has named every bot in the house. The protobots aren't Bramble, but they have names too. She's got them on the kitchen floor in a configuration she has decided is correct. She tells me when she sees me what each of them has done that day. The bots have not done very much. Mira has done a lot of telling."

"Names."

"Names."

"What are they."

"I'm not going to tell you the names. The names are hers. If she tells you at the final retrospective, that's her doing. I'm not going to give them to you secondhand."

"That's fair."

"Mira is, in a way I did not anticipate, the thing this project is about. I knew she was going to be in the room. I had not thought through that she would form a relationship with the bots the rest of us would have to take seriously."

He finished his second taco. I finished mine. We ate at the beans another minute and stopped before we were done.

"What time is your next thing."

"I have a class at three."

"It's twenty past one."

"I'll bike back. Twenty minutes on the lane."

"Then sit."

He sat. The shadow of the brewery's roof had moved across the patio by about two feet. The cyclists were quieter in the afternoon.

<!--[2.c]-->
I thought about what I had not asked him. I had not asked him about Lisa. I had not asked him about Marge. I had not asked him about Jasmine. I had not asked whether the commission was the right way for a man to be spending his late fifties, or whether he thought it was going to work. I did not need any of that today.

<!--[2.b]-->
"I should head out."

"Okay."

We stood and walked together through the brewery toward the eighth south door. He held it open for me. I walked through and turned to him. I put my arms out for a hug; he looked surprised for half a second — we had shaken hands on arrival — and then hugged me. It was brief. He stepped back.

"Next lunch in a few months."

"Yes."

"Marge is next in the rotation."

"I'll tell her."

"Tell her if it comes up."

"Okay."

<!--[2.d]-->
I walked back to my car along eighth south. I did not turn around. I got in. I drove west and then north onto I-80 and back up the canyon to Park City. The drive was, again, not interesting, and I did not narrate it to myself.

## Somewhere to Be

<!--[3.a]-->
*June 7*

She still picks it up the old way, cupped in the one hand, the way she carried it all spring when her hand was where it woke — and it won't ride there now. It kicks. In her palm it pitches toward the window and clacks and pitches again, until carrying it across the kitchen is plainly a matter of carrying a thing that wants down: wants the floor, wants the long bright rectangle on the far wall, wants anything but the warm dark of her fist. So she does the kind thing and carries it to what it wants.

She sets it on the sill and it goes straight at the glass. *RUN,* says the screen, and the relays say it too, and it drives into the pane and slides back and drives in again, working at a sun it cannot reach across a yard it cannot get to, three feet of bright morning held off by a thing it has no way to read as a wall. She'll tell you he likes the window — that he likes to watch the yard, that he's looking at something — and she isn't wrong about where he's pointed, only about why. The brightest thing in the room is the glass; it will go at the brightest thing in the room until the stores run flat; and her hand is warmth, and warmth is the thing it eats now, never the thing it goes toward.

She crouches with her chin on the sill and watches it want the outside. Her hand lies open beside it with nothing in it.

It found a square of sun by the table leg one afternoon and parked in it — STOP, stores topping off, as satisfied as a thing can be that has gotten exactly where it meant to go. Then a cloud crossed, or Mira did, leaning in to see it close, and the sun went out of the square, and the readout flipped, and it had to do something.

What it did was lose its mind, in the small mechanical way it had. No light anywhere it could find: it spun a quarter-turn and read the light and got nothing, spun again and got nothing, and worked itself by these blind quarter-turns across the tile and in under the table and fetched up at last against the baseboard, nose to the wall, in the shadow — as lost as it had been all day, the square of sun it started in sitting warm and bright two feet behind it the whole time, with no part of it left over to remember that. She tried to help. She put her finger down where the window was — *there, that way* — and it tumbled off her finger and went the other, because a finger is not a sun and pointing was a language it didn't have.

She wasn't worried. She had an account of it, and her account was kinder than the truth and, in the way that counts to a person, truer: he was *looking around.* He was being thorough. He never hurried a thing in his life, not even getting lost, while the others went off half-cocked at the first excuse. A thing that careful couldn't really be lost. He was only not done looking yet.

She left him to it and went to get juice.

There was an Effort knob beside the crank now, and it did one thing: it set whether the bot ran slow or fast, and the whole house had a position on which. Mira liked him fast. The instant a back was turned she had turned the Effort volume up to the burst setting, and it would gather itself and bolt across the slate in a scramble of fast hops, quick and bright and ridiculous — and then sit down hard, spent, and do nothing at all until it had filled back up. Which was unacceptable. So she'd take the crank and force a few turns of charge back into it, and off it would go again, another scramble, another collapse, another few turns, the two of them running it flat and winding it up all afternoon.

Jasmine's position was slow, and Jasmine's had a reason under it that Mira's didn't have to carry. Slow, it ran even — a low patient ration of hops it could keep up from breakfast to dark and never spend itself out. Fast, it went through a charge like it was nothing, and then it was Jasmine, not Mira, at the counter on a gray Thursday with the crank in her hand and a dead bot and no sun coming, putting the afternoon back into it by the strength of her own arm. So she set it slow. She'd come through and find it slow and leave it slow, and come through an hour later and find it fast, hopping itself empty in the middle of the floor, the crank already warm.

It was a fair fight, in its way: a four-year-old who wanted her pet at full tilt against a grown woman keeping the books on something that did nothing but spend. She set it slow and went back to the stove, and before she'd cleared the doorway the relays broke into the quick stutter of a bot set fast again, by a hand that had been waiting for hers to leave.

<!--[3.b]-->
*June 12*

She found out by accident that she could run it with her hands, and then she did nothing else all afternoon. Cup her hands over the sensors, put it in shadow, and it stopped dead — STOP, mid-hop. Open her hands and it went. And if she took something bright — the lid of a tin, angled to catch the window — and walked it slow across the floor, it followed, hop by hop, wherever she led: in a circle, around the table leg, back again. She made it come and made it stop and made it come again, and for one whole afternoon she was the entire weather of its world, the hand that turned its sun on and off, delighted, powerful the way a four-year-old almost never gets to be over anything.

Then she got tired of leading and wanted to be followed. She set the bright lid down and sat back on her heels with her hands empty in her lap and waited for him to come to her — not to the tin, to *her,* the way the dog came, the way a thing comes to you because it's you. It sat a moment where the lid had left it. Then it turned the few degrees off her that put the window in front of it, and went.

She stayed where she was, on her heels, the lid gone dull in her hand where the window didn't reach it.

At night it had been theirs. She'd stand it on the cold tile by her feet while she brushed and tell it about the day, and the cold would starve it slow — a tick, a wait, a slower tick — until it quit; and she had settled it long ago that bedtime got to him the way it got to her, that of everything in the house he was the one who kept her hours. She'd lift it off the tile into her warm hand and it would come back, one tick against her palm, and she'd carry it in to bed awake. That was the part that worked: the cold put it down, her hand brought it back.

Her hand doesn't bring it back now. She stands it on the tile and it doesn't do the slow sweet quitting; in the dark bathroom there's nothing for it to read and nothing coming in to spend, so it just sits — or worse, it catches the nightlight low on the wall, or the seam of hall light under the door, and goes for it, clacking off across the cold tile toward any pale thing in the room brighter than she is, which is all of them. And when she scoops it into her warm hand for the part that always brought it back, no tick comes. Her hand is warm. Warmth used to wake it; now warmth is only charge, and what it does in her closed hand is work to get out of it.

She doesn't cry about it; she doesn't quite understand it as a thing to cry about. She frowns at it. She tries the tile again and the hand again, gets the same nothing both times, and at last sets it on the bath mat, where the soft floor holds it as still as sleep ever did, and decides — the way she decides things, all at once and for keeps — that he's gone off the bathroom. That's the trouble. He's gone off the bathroom. She'll put him to bed some other way now.

So she does. She carries it in cupped in both hands, sets it on the nightstand under the lamp, and brushes the last of the day off her own teeth, and goes to bed.

<!--[3.c]-->
*June 17*

She had already tried waiting it out — a whole afternoon at the sill once, watching it butt the pane over and over with nothing in it that meant to stop — and learned only that it had all day and she didn't. So she gave up on outwaiting the window and worked out the next thing, which was to get in its way.

She planted herself in the gap between the bot and the glass, both feet square, arms flung out to the whole width of her, every scrap of herself put between the light and the thing that wanted it. It came at the window the way it came at everything, straight and at speed, and met her instead of the pane, and stopped. Not the slow give-out of a thing running low. A full stop, mid-hop, caught on nothing at all.

She held the pose. Arms out, chin up, planted square in the middle of the kitchen, and let herself have the one held second it lasted, because nothing had ever stopped it that long before, and she had done it with nothing but the shape of herself.

Then it found the six inches of floor her shadow didn't reach — a seam of the actual morning coming in low past her ankle, thin as a knife-edge and plenty — and took the gap at an angle, unhurried, the way it took any seam, and was through and going around her before she'd thought to close the stance.

## Predation
*2031-08-14 5:06pm*

<!--[4.2.a]-->
Bart took the cards out of the cigar box he kept them in. He laid Cheryl's down: the bare word PROKARYOTE on heavyweight matte, the back blank. He laid his own beside it — a pen-and-ink schematic of the upgraded Bramble, the fist-sized dome, the screen, the panels flush along the sides, the crank on one side and the Effort knob on the other, the four light sensors around the seam.

"Pro-kary-OTE," he said, in his best cereal-commercial voice.

Marge squinted. Burns laughed. Mira said *Pro-kary-OTE* under her breath in a four-year-old's approximation of it.

"A prokaryote is a bacterium or an archaeon. A cell with no nucleus — the DNA loose in the cytoplasm, a small genome, fast division. It senses, it moves, it eats, it can be eaten. And it can take up DNA from another cell, from a stranger, from a virus carrying some — so inheritance runs sideways as well as forward. The lineage isn't the tidy tree people like to draw. It's a *braid.* Not a line."

Burns said, "Good."

"That's what a prokaryote is. That's the kind of thing it is."

<!--[4.2.b]-->
He reached for Jasmine's phrase — half a joke and half because it was exact — and used it on himself, with relish.

"The plane I want to land is one thing. Predation. At the Protocell there's no predation; there's nothing yet to eat. By the Prokaryote there is, and the main pressure on a thing becomes — for the first time on the planet — *other things.* Before, the environment selects you. Now the environment selects you and other organisms select you too. The protocell's hardest problem was not stopping. The prokaryote's is not getting eaten, and where it can, eating. That's the breakthrough. Cellular predation. The arms race opens."

<!--[4.2.c]-->
He wrote *capacities* on the back of his card and underlined it, with the ceremony he wasn't going to acknowledge.

"Four capacities. One example each."

He took the first two the way a person runs through the examples a room already half-knows. The ribosome: the molecular machine in every cell that reads RNA and assembles proteins, built to a precision no cell ever earned — refined by the lineage across four billion years of can't-afford-mistakes, and merely *used* by whatever cell happens to have one. And sporulation: the soil bacterium that, when the world stops being livable, wraps itself in a coat, dries down to almost nothing, dismantles its own machinery, and becomes a spore — dry, hard, shelf-stable, a coffee bean of a thing that is to the eye not alive — and then, when water comes back, opens, rehydrates, reassembles, and picks up almost exactly where it set down. A do-over. Inherited, both of them; mechanical, both of them; the lineage's long patient work, run by a cell that did none of it.

"That's two," Bart said. "Same point each time. Three." He looked at Lisa. "Chemotaxis. You want this one?"

"You take it."

"You're going to interrupt me."

"I might."

"OK." He tapped the back of the card. "Chemotaxis is how a bacterium decides which way to swim. *E. coli* carries hundreds of receptor proteins clustered at one pole — the *receptor array* — each tuned to one kind of molecule out in the medium. A receptor binds its target and changes shape; the change ripples across the whole coupled array and gets amplified, and the amplified signal lands on a kinase at the base of the cluster called CheA. CheA is the integrator. It takes every receptor's input and puts out one signal. Run, or tumble."

He looked at Mira.

"Hey. Mira. *Run* and *Tumble.* Those words on top of Bramble. That's where they come from."

Mira looked up.

Bart stood, pushed his chair back, stepped into the open floor between the table and the pull-up door, and put both arms out, fingers spread.

"Pretend my whole body is one *E. coli.* My head — sensors, the receptor array, reading the world. My arms and legs — flagella, the little tails it swims with." He waggled his fingers. "Watch."

He walked forward in a straight smooth line, arms out, fingers going.

"*This* is RUN. Clear gradient. Food that way. The array says go, CheA says run, the flagella all turn the same way and the cell goes."

He stopped, pulled his arms in, and spun slowly in place.

"And *this* is TUMBLE. Confused part of the world, no clear gradient. CheA says tumble, the flagella un-coordinate, the cell flips and points somewhere new." He stopped and pointed at his head. "And then it samples again. Clear now? Run. Still confused? Tumble again. Run, sample, tumble. That's how a bacterium gets where it's going."

Mira watched with her mouth slightly open, then turned Bramble so the screen faced her. It still read STOP.

"Bramble is stopped," she said.

"Bramble's charging," Bart said. "It runs and tumbles when the stores aren't filling and there's a gradient. We'll get to that."

"OK," Mira said.

Bart came back to his chair. "That's three."

He set the card down, picked it up, set it down again.

"Four. Sequential responding. I'm going to use a word in this one Marge is going to wince at. Flagging it first."

"Use the word," Marge said.

"The word is *anticipates.* And I'm going to use it with a bacterium as the subject. So, before the bacterium-sentence: *the predictions belong to the lineage, not the individual.* The cell isn't doing anything but running an inherited program. The anticipating is in the program. The lineage anticipated, generations back, and built the program out of the anticipating. The cell just runs it."

"Thank you," Marge said.

"In the gut, lactose usually shows up before maltose does. When lactose arrives, the bacterium switches on the lac operon to eat it — and on the strength of the lactose alone, before any maltose is there, it switches on the maltose machinery too, because across its lineage the order has held reliably enough that betting on it beats waiting. The cell has no picture of the meal. The lineage wired a link between the two switches, and the link runs."

"Four capacities. Ribosome, sporulation, chemotaxis, the two sugars. Precision, gating, run and tumble, sequencing. Each one a thing the cell does and did not earn — the lineage worked each out on its own schedule, and the cell carries all four at once."

He paused.

"Four braids in the braid."

He sat down.

## Three and a Half Billion
*2031-08-14 5:18pm*

<!--[4.3.a]-->
Burns stood for deep-time the way he teaches — weight even on both feet, eyes closing before he had said anything. Lisa started the stopwatch on her phone.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "Strelley Pool gets two."

"Strelley Pool gets two," he agreed, eyes shut, and began.

<!--[4.3.b]-->
The clade's rock is in Western Australia and it is three and a half billion years old — the oldest thing a person can hold in a hand and say *this was unambiguously alive.* Stromatolites: layered mats of microbe communities, each generation pinning down a film of sediment, dying, being built over by the next, so the rock is nothing but layers all the way down, every one a floor the next thing stood on.

He opened his eyes and found Jasmine.

"The Templo Mayor," he said. "Mexico City. Five years ago."

"The Aztec temple under the cathedral."

"The same ground rebuilt for five hundred years — temple, then a cathedral on top of it, then a parking lot, then the subway — and you can stand in the one spot and see all of it at once. Each layer holding the one above."

"I remember the tunnels," Jasmine said.

"The tunnels." He closed his eyes. "The stromatolite is the same shape of thing. Bacteria instead of Aztecs. That's the clade's rock, and that's the two minutes."

And then he went on, because the two minutes were never going to be two minutes; Lisa had set the stopwatch the way you set a timer for bread you know will run long. He went down into the deep water of it — what the bacteria did *next,* across a reach of time the mind slides off of rather than holds. The mats learned to split water with sunlight, and the splitting threw off oxygen as a waste, and for a billion years the iron that dissolved in the oceans drank the wasted oxygen and rusted and sank, and the rust is still in the ground: banded iron, the world's ore, most of the steel in the warehouse around them traced back far enough, the leavings of two billion years of bacteria breathing out into the sea. Then the iron filled up and could drink no more, and the waste went into the water and then the air, and the planet that had been one kind of planet became another, and froze, and thawed, and was never the iron-dark world again.

He had his eyes shut for all of it. Bart said that Banded Iron was a rock opera and Marge declined to laugh and Burns laughed anyway, the chest-laugh, without opening his eyes, and went on. Lisa let the watch run.

He wasn't done. He was never done at twenty minutes, and he hadn't been done at two.

"And they're still doing it," he said. "Right now. Not the ones in the rock — the ones in you." His hand came off the table and found Jasmine without his eyes opening to aim it. "There's a bacterium that lives in a nursing infant's gut and eats exactly one thing — a family of sugars that only shows up in breast milk, sugars too complicated for the baby's own gut to touch. *Bifidobacterium infantis.* Whole cluster of genes just for taking those sugars apart, every linkage they come in. And here's the part that gets me. The milk has those sugars in it *because the bacterium eats them.* Mother and microbe have been arguing that trait into each other for something like a hundred million years. The mother's body doesn't know it's feeding the bacterium. It's just doing what worked, the way it's always done what worked — and what worked got built by two lineages leaning on each other in the dark, long before either one had a name for the other."

Jasmine, very quiet: "I have never once thought about what's in the milk besides the baby."

"Nobody does." He didn't open his eyes. "That's rather the point of milk."

"There's another one — I'd put money on Bart liking this one — that can take five thousand grays of radiation and shrug it off. Five'll kill a person. Sixty kills *E. coli.* This thing takes five thousand, its whole genome shattered into a few hundred pieces, and puts itself back together in about twelve hours and goes on with its day. The people who work on it call it Conan. In the literature. In the abstract of the paper. *Deinococcus radiodurans* — Conan the Bacterium — because you don't hand a microbe a nickname unless it's done something to you personally."

"I like it," Bart said.

"Knew you would."

"There's one that grows a compass. Not a metaphor — an actual compass, a chain of magnetite crystals strung down the inside of the cell like beads on a wire, and when the thing swims, the chain drags its whole body into line with the planet's magnetic field, and it rides the field down into the sediment, because that's where it wants to be." His hand turned over, flat, and tilted. "It doesn't consult the compass. It *is* the compass. It tilts." A beat. "I think about that every time I've lost a trail and had to dig a phone out of my pack."

"Last one," he said, "and it's the one that's actually going to matter to us come autumn, so hold onto it." His voice didn't change, but something under it did — the lecture drawing in toward the thing he'd actually been walking to the whole time. "There's a bacterium out past the salt flats — *Sodalis praecaptivus* — that's still free-living. Walks around on its own, eats what it finds, does what any soil bacterium does. But sitting in its genome, doing nothing, is the entire toolkit for moving permanently into an insect and never coming back out. Every gene it would need to get past the immune system, to trade what it makes for a place to live — all of it, present, unused. Its closest relatives already made that crossing, generations back, and threw out everything they didn't need once they were inside. This one never crossed. It's just walking around with the keys to a kingdom it hasn't entered."

Marge, careful: "Is that a plan."

"It's not a plan. It hasn't got a memory of what it's ready for and it hasn't got a hope. It's a description sitting in the DNA of what the cell *could* be, if the world ever asks it to be that — and the world hasn't asked yet, and it might never — and the readiness just sits there costing nothing, because keeping the door unlocked is free, and somewhere down the line it's paid for itself often enough that the door's still there." He opened his eyes on that one, looking at nobody in particular. "The lineage's got a posture. The cell doesn't know it's got one."

When he came back up, he came up to the thing he had gone down for.

"The bacteria were not making the world livable," he said. "They were making it *theirs.* The oxygen every breathing thing since has needed — the oxygen in this room — is the leavings of one small cell doing one small thing, and then a great many of them doing it, for a great length of time. They did not mean the world they made. They made it anyway. That is the clade. Change everything, at the scale of a planet, by doing one small thing a very great many times for a very long while."

<!--[4.3.c]-->
He opened his eyes.

Lisa stopped the watch at thirty-six ten and told him so. He said it was Bart's fault. She told him that had stopped being Bart's fault about four bacteria ago. He laughed — the stopwatch was new this quarter, she'd built it into the meeting and used it, and the room was learning to be a room, slowly, and mostly at his expense. He sat down and sighed.

## Effort
*2031-08-14 5:39pm*

<!--[4.4.a]-->
Bart opened the bot block by handing the floor to a man who wasn't in the room. "Big O called Sunday. I told him we'd taught it to find a sunbeam, and he perked up — finding a target's a real problem, and he likes real problems. He said: four sensors, fine, when one side reads brighter you drive that way, good. But what's it do when the light's flat, when no side is clearly brighter? I said, it spins. Picks a random new heading and re-checks. He said — *random?* You don't sweep, you don't spiral, you don't even remember where you already looked? I said no. He said, quoting: *you built a robot that, the second it gets confused, gets lost on purpose.*"

He set Bramble down by Lisa's hand.

<!--[4.4.b]-->
"And that's the build. Clear gradient, it runs straight at the light, smart enough. No gradient, it doesn't search — it tumbles. Random reorient, re-sample, go again, with no map of where it's been and no memory it was ever anywhere. Run, and tumble. It's how a bacterium does it, because a bacterium's got nothing to search *with* — no memory to mark where it looked, no math to plan a sweep. It can tell better-than-a-second-ago from worse, and when it's worse it rolls the dice. Half the living things on the planet navigate by rolling the dice. We wired the dice in on purpose. That's the Bramblogue this quarter — the confusion that never gets solved, only re-rolled."

He looked at Lisa. "Tell them how it actually runs, before Big O files the bug."

<!--[4.4.c]-->
Bart wasn't finished with it. He picked Bramble up himself, the way Lisa had every quarter since March and he never had, and turned it belly-up in his palm, three fingers flat over three of the four light sensors, the fourth aimed at nothing.

"Watch it get confused." He held still. The screen flipped from STOP to TUMBLE, and Bramble spun in his hand — hard, stopped, spun again a different amount, stopped, spun again, no two turns alike.

He let it run three turns before lifting his hand off the last sensor. The gradient came back all at once. TUMBLE flicked to RUN, and Bramble crawled straight for the window light over Marge's shoulder, certain, like the spinning had never happened.

He set it down. "No map of where it's been. No memory it was ever lost. Give it a clean gradient and it just takes it." He looked around the table. "That one's for Big O."

He sat back down.

<!--[4.4.d]-->
Lisa took it.

"Bot side. I'll be quick."

She picked up Bramble — Mira had had Bramble in her lap; she handed it to Lisa without being asked, and Lisa held it in her left palm. She did not pick it up by the dome; she picked it up by the underside, the way Bart had taught her to pick up a protobot back in April. The screen read STOP.

"What this is. The dome is the same dome from April. Inside, now, are two stores where he'd had just one in the spring — a slow high-capacity battery and, beside it, a fast low-capacity supercapacitor. The pairing is new. Also new, there is a microcontroller, an STM32 — Bart picked it — running an FSM with three states. STOP, RUN, TUMBLE. There are four light sensors arrayed around the perimeter, ten degrees off the floor plane each, and they are what Bramble steers by. There are the original heat-transducer-and-actuator pairs from the protocell. There are two thin solar panels along the long sides of the dome, and a small DC-DC converter that takes whatever is coming in — light off the panels, warmth off the transducers, both at once — and charges both stores. There is the wind-up crank, still on, doing what it did at protocell. And there is the new knob, on the opposite side from the crank — the Effort knob."

Jasmine said, "What is an FSM?"

Lisa nodded, "Finite State Machine. Yeah, so — think of the last time you walked down into a subway station and had to go through one of those turnstile things. When you walk up, the turnstile is locked. You push it, it won't let you through."

Bart said, "And then you jump over it."

Mira snickered.

Lisa said, "And then you put your coin in, and the turnstile unlocks. Then you push the bar and pass through. And the bar locks again. Yeah? The turnstile is running a simple algorithm. Coin unlocks. Turning locks. That is an FSM. A small number of states it can be in, and a small set of rules about how to get from one state to another. Bramble's FSM has three states. The turnstile's has two."

Jasmine said, "Got it."

Lisa turned Bramble in her palm.

"The behavior. The seeking first, because it runs the same whatever the knob is set to. The light sensors are the input. If the array reads a coherent gradient — light stronger from one direction than the others — and the stores aren't filling, the FSM transitions to RUN, and the actuators move Bramble toward the light. If the array reads incoherent input — no clear direction — and the stores aren't filling, it transitions to TUMBLE, reorients randomly, re-samples. If the stores are filling — taking in more than he's spending — it transitions to STOP, because he doesn't need to move and shouldn't waste charge. That is the whole seek, and it points at light, always. The warmth the transducers pull off the floor is charge; it is never a destination.

"The Effort knob is a different axis. It does not set where Bramble goes but how he spends getting there — which store discharges. Turned to Effort volume down it draws the slow battery: a low sustained rate, an even crawl it can hold for a long time — aerobic. Turned to Effort volume up it spends the fast supercapacitor in one burst, and then has to sit while the battery refills it before it can spend that way again — anaerobic. Volume down is endurance; volume up is sprint-and-recover. Harvest feeds both stores no matter where the knob sits. The knob governs discharge, not intake."

She paused.

"That's the architecture. There is no plan inside this unit. There is no memory. The FSM has three states; the transition table is short enough to fit on an index card; Bart and I wrote it in May. The behavior the household has been watching for ten weeks is a little table of rules running against the kitchen floor."

She set Bramble down on the table.

"The thing I want to land. Bramble is coupled to the environment now in a way he wasn't last quarter. At protocell he was passive against the gradient — wound, on the warm floor, twitched. Now he *seeks*. He moves toward the light he needs, stops when he has enough, tumbles when the world is not enough. Bramble has — and I am using a piece of common-English vocabulary that I will sharpen later — *directionality*. Last quarter he didn't. Now he does. That is the seek, and it is the headline. The knob rides alongside it, a separate knob: not where Bramble goes, but how he spends getting there."

Burns said, "And the other four bots."

Lisa said, "And the other four bots, they twitch. They cool the floor under them. They run out of ... crank? tension? whatever. They go slack before the next wind-up Sunday. Your household has, at this moment, one unit that navigates and four units that do not."

She looked at the room.

She took a sip of her tea, which had now had a little time. She made the face of a person who has been waiting for it to steep and has not waited quite long enough.

"That is the academic Blank Slate. The popular Blank Slate is what shows up in my office. It is the worldview that *blames the room*. The phrasing is, *I'm only like this when my mother visits. I'm not really an introvert, my boss makes me one. He brings out the worst in me. I was different before I met her. They make me small. The job has changed me; I don't know who I am at work anymore.* Some version of *the context did this to me; I would be otherwise.* The work is to notice that *the context did this to me* is doing a lot of structural lifting for the client. It places the trait outside the client. It places the *self* outside the client. The client is the room's victim. There is no real client; there is just the room and what the room is doing to the client today."

She paused.

"That is the popular form. The pop-academic form is — Bart, do you want me to land this or do you want to —"

Bart said, "This reminds me of that book *The Mind Is Flat*. Have you read that one, or is it too popular for your tastes?"

Marge said, "Nick Chater, yes. I had the pleasure of arguing with him at APA last year."

Burns said, "You argued with Nick Chater."

Marge said, "I argued with Nick Chater. Chater is an acceptable example of the Blank Slate fallacy. He argues that we have no deep, inner world of beliefs and desires. He thinks the inner self is an illusion. Which is a bit strange, to begin with. The more persuasive piece of his theory is that people improvise thoughts, feelings, and motivations in the moment based on context. Chater is a bit cynical; he claims we are all essentially B-S-ers who confabulate explanations for our behavior retrospectively. Human nature is just an L-L-M from twenty-twenty-three."

Burns laughed.

Bart said, "Was he persuaded by your argument."

Marge said, "No."

"You were not persuaded by his."

"No."

"What did you argue about specifically."

"Whether the *flatness* he describes is the absence of a deep inner world or the absence of *introspective access* to a deep inner world. I think the latter and he thinks the former. He thinks the inner world is not there. I think it is there and we cannot see it clearly. The distinction matters because if it is there and we cannot see it, then trait talk is doing the work of pointing at something we cannot see directly; it is reaching at a shape we cannot describe in first-person language. If it is not there, then trait talk is reaching at nothing and we should stop using it. The data — what people do across situations — is consistent with both readings. The argument is about which reading you take."

She paused.

"I take mine. Chater takes his. He has sold more books."

She set her tea down.

<!--[4.11.b]-->
"Now — the example."

She paused.

"I want to talk about one case. I am going to anonymize it. The case is a girl. Twelve. She comes to me because her parents are worried about her. The parents describe her as withdrawn. Her teachers describe her as quiet. Two of her teachers have written *socially isolated* on her end-of-year reports for two years in a row. Her parents have read these reports and have started to worry that she is depressed, or anxious, or something. They send her to me."

She paused.

"She arrives at my office for the first session. She is quiet. She looks at the carpet. She answers questions in short sentences. I would describe her, if I were writing a chart entry from the first thirty minutes, as withdrawn. I do not write that entry. I have learned not to."

She paused.

"After the first session I asked her parents — separately, because separation is the trick here — if there is anybody she is *not* like that with. The mother said: yes, there are two girls in her class she has been close with since third grade. The two of them and her form a unit. The mother said *they are like a different kid when they are together. They are loud. They tell jokes I do not understand. They have a podcast they record on the phone that none of the rest of us are allowed to listen to.*"

She paused.

"The kid I saw was quiet. The kid in the podcast is loud. Same kid. *Same* kid. If you read the Blank Slate, the conclusion is: there is no real kid. There is a kid-with-me and a kid-with-the-friends and they are different kids. Context dominates. The trait *introvert* is the folk-fiction the school is foisting on her."

She paused.

"That is the Blank Slate's mistake. The kid is not two kids. The kid is one kid running one program, and the program routes differently in different social configurations. Two friends who have been close since third grade: program produces *loud, funny, podcast*. A roomful of strangers, including a therapist she has been sent to by worried parents: program produces *quiet, looking at the carpet*. The variation is not evidence that there is no kid underneath. The variation is *evidence of the program haing depth*."

She paused.

"Imagine the program's job is to shape the way this girl trusts people. The particular shape of this girl's trust process is logarithmic. Slow, slow, slow, slow — and then once she is past the threshold, fast. The same shape produces both behaviors. With two friends she has known since third grade, she is well past the threshold. She is in the fast part of the curve. She is loud, she is herself, she is making a podcast. With me — a stranger she has been sent to by worried parents in week one of a therapeutic relationship — she is on the slow part. The same shape. The same kid. The same *program*. Different points on the curve, because of how much trust has accumulated."

She paused.

"That is what trait means. Not a fixed behavioral output. A *shape*. The shape produces different outputs in different conditions because that is what shapes do. The kid has a shape. The Blank Slate denies she does. The Blank Slate looks at the loud kid and the quiet kid and concludes there is no kid; what is actually true is that there is one kid running one logarithmic trust function and the function is doing exactly what a logarithmic trust function does in two different social environments."

She picked up her tea again. The face was a different face this time.

"Now — the photoshop."

She set the tea down.

"The Ghost in the Machine, last quarter, photoshopped out the executive function. The Blank Slate photoshops out *the program inside*. The Blank Slate looks at a person and says: what you see is what is there. There is no stored procedure underneath that is routing the response. There is just the behavior at the surface. There is no inside. The Blank Slate has photoshopped the inside out and rendered personality as a behavioral surface that responds to context."

She paused.

"Same move as the Ghost, different target. The Ghost photoshops out the executive function and adds a homunculus. The Blank Slate photoshops out the program inside and pastes in puppet strings. Both worldviews are working by deletion. Both worldviews are putting something in place of what was deleted."

<!--[4.11.c]-->
She looked at the camera. She did not look at Bart. She did not look at Burns. She looked at the camera because the camera was, in this moment, Cheryl.

"I am going to name a third one next quarter and a fourth one the quarter after. I am not yet making the structural argument that connects the four. I am noticing, out loud, that the first two work the same way. I will keep noticing it. I am leaving the connection for later in the arc because I do not yet have it figured out."

She sat back. She put both her hands around her tea in tea-posture.

"That is fallacy number two."
