## The Wire Is the Gathering
*Bart, hardware. Filed December 1, 2031, for the team.*

<!--[1.a]-->

Lisa's running the upgraded one back to the house this morning.

Now the fun part, because the dying is also the thing I fixed.

Here's why it died: nothing was watching the tank. It had a store, it spent the store, and it had no idea ever how much was left — so it'd run itself flat in the middle of a job, no warning, every time. So I gave it a gauge. A real one, watches the charge the way you'd watch a fuel needle, and Lisa wrote the part that reads the needle — how much is left, how fast it's going down — and shifts what it cares about while there's still plenty in the tank: let the charge dip and feeding climbs to the top of the pile, so it stops coasting toward flat and tops up the next time it's at the dock instead of rolling past it. One wrinkle, and it's Burns's, still: the dock didn't get any friendlier over the fall. It still won't give it a single watt unless it shows up with a full bin to dump — no trade, no warmth, same dirty hole. So it's not just how-low, it's how-low-and-have-I-got-anything-to-pay-with; an empty bin gets turned away, so when the charge dips it works a load up first instead of rolling onto the dock with nothing. She built that in too. It feeds before empty now instead of finding empty the hard way, and it never rolls up to the dock with nothing to trade. Three months of Jasmine bending down to a dead robot, gone. She won't notice it's gone, which is how you know it worked.

And here's the part I'm proud of. It's got wires. First wires in any of these — a net of nerve-thread strung all through the body off a bumper ring round the skirt. Here's the trick, because everybody's first question is going to be how a thing with no boss in it answers all at once: it doesn't decide to. The net runs straight from the ring to the brushes and the suction and every wheel, so a touch on the skirt doesn't go up to the board to get thought about — it runs the net and lands on all of them together. Nudge the ring and it answers before you've finished nudging it: brushes stop, suction drops, the whole chassis shies back a thumb's width, all of it, faster than the board could ever get a word in. There's no controller doing the gathering. The wire is the gathering. The thinking still runs upstairs on Lisa's board, slow, same as ever; this is under that — a straight reflex that never asks permission, quick and dumb and total.

Bramble can eat bigger, now — wider mouth, more pull, and a little compactor behind the intake that crushes what it swallows before it stores it, so it takes up a berry now instead of smearing it around the floor for a long minute. The compactor thumps when it fires. You feel it through the slate. That's about the only noise left in the thing.

Which means I pulled the relays. All of them. Two quarters I told you I liked that you could hear it think, the clack, the cascade across the room — and the body's gone quiet now, because the wires are too fast to make a sound, and I'll be honest, I miss it. The bench is quiet. I kept one relay in a drawer. Don't tell Lisa.

Here's one I didn't build; the cold did it. Wait till January and watch what the fast spend does to it on the cold mornings — it'll sit longer between bursts than it does now — fires its flurry, stops to refill, takes its sweet time finding the second wind. It's the battery. A cold store gives back slower, so the quick tank takes longer to top off between bursts, so the fast spend goes sluggish when the kitchen's cold. Nothing's wrong with it, nothing to fix; it's just January. Mira's decided he's sulking. I told her it was cold. She heard me out and kept thinking he's sulking.

<!--[1.b]-->

The Action knob went in — Lisa's knob, her board, her label: Action, a volume knob on where the thing settles. Warm-or-busy, I'd have written it, but nobody asked me.

All it sets is the kind of spot the thing settles toward. Action volume down and it climbs the warm till it's in the warmest corner it can find — the east window some days, the tile by the vent others — settles in, and when the board decides it's tired it goes still and sips the warm off the floor in its sleep. Same heat-gap trick as the very first units, the gradient, except now the gradient picks where it beds down. Action volume up and it'd rather be where the people are: it heads for the warmth and the racket of them and ends up in the thick of the footsteps, the middle of the kitchen at dinner, and settles there instead. It still sleeps either way — the board calls the sleep, the knob just calls the spot. And be clear about this, because the knob looks like it ought to do more than it does: volume down or up, it runs slow or fast on whatever the Effort knob's set to, it wanders or grooves on whatever the Explore knob's set to, and the startle's wired in under all of it regardless. The knob moves one thing and one thing only. Three knobs that do anything, three separate jobs, no wires between them — which I know for a fact, because I'm the one who didn't run any.

The thing I'd actually watch this quarter isn't any one knob — it's that it's started wanting two things at once and the wants don't agree. It'll be parked in the spot it likes, settled, and the gauge'll come due, and it has to leave the place it wants in order to go do the thing it needs, and then come back. First time any of these has had two pulls in it pointing different directions, with something inside having to settle which one wins.

It's got nerves now. It still hasn't got a self. Don't let the wires fool you, Marge — fast isn't the same as home, and a reflex isn't a mind. I'll have more to say about that at the table.

<!--[1.c]-->

Calls. The gauge holds: Bramble stops dying, and Jasmine doesn't carry him off the floor again, not once. And the two wants are where it gets interesting — I'll bet the first time the pull to feed and the pull to find a spot come due in the same second, he hangs there a beat before he picks. A real beat, long enough to see from a chair. Watch for it. That one's Lisa's to win or lose, though; I just gave it the legs.

Tear this up in three months if he dies on the floor even once. He won't.

— Bartholazoa

## Pho Tay Ho
*January 26, 2032*

<!--[2.a]-->

The place I took Jasmine to in late January was Pho Tay Ho, on the line where the Granary gives over to Ballpark, a narrow warm room with the windows fogged from the inside, which in a Salt Lake January is the whole of what you want from a room.

<!--[2.c]-->

I chose the warm room for the winter lunch.

<!--[2.b]-->

I had watched Jasmine for a year by then — on the tapes, in the corner of the warehouse footage, arriving in late afternoon with the child on her hip — and I had not been alone with her since the morning I handed the team the portfolio.

<!--[2.c]-->

You come to know a person strangely that way. Hands first.

<!--[2.b]-->

She came in twenty minutes late with snow not yet gone from the shoulders of a wool coat the color of wet bark, sat down across from me, and put both her hands flat on the table for a second, the way a person does when the table is the first still thing they have touched all day. They are a midwife's hands, broad through the palm, the forearms behind them stronger than the soft sweaters let on, and when they go flat like that it means she has been somewhere her hands were the whole of her and the rest of her is still catching up. The warmth came up a half-second late and then it came up all the way, raspy at the edges the way it gets at the end of a long day, and she said it was good to sit in a room where nobody needed anything from her body, and laughed, and ordered a bowl of pho.

I asked how the week had been. She said hard. There was a particular flatness on the word, and I had watched enough of her by then to know not to pull on it. A hard week for the rest of us was a deadline, a flat tire on Foothill, a paper that had been wrong in its first paragraph for fourteen months. A hard week for Jasmine was a room where the thing that is supposed to begin does not begin — a mother, and, in the end, not a baby — and she had come from one of those in the last few days, I understood, the way you understand weather through a wall. She said the word and reached for the tea and that was all she meant to give it,

<!--[2.c]-->

and I let it go by, because the letting was the only useful thing I had, and because I am a person who has spent a long life learning to let the hard thing go by rather than reach for it, and I do not always know anymore which of those two reasons I am running on.

So we did the thing I think the two of us were always going to do the first time we sat down without the lab or the child between us, which is envy each other across the table and not pretend otherwise.

She started it. She said I had so much room — she meant the calendar, the empty Tuesdays, the way I can decide over coffee to be in Marin by Thursday for no reason but the wanting to — she said I had all that room, and she had a pager that goes off at two in the morning, and a four-year-old who would be five in a couple of weeks, and a body that had not been fully her own in years. She said it warmly, the way you can only say a true envy to someone you actually like, and she meant it, and she was right.

And I told her the truth back, which is that I would trade the whole of it — the room, the planes, the long quiet afternoons I have arranged my life to be made almost entirely of — for one ordinary week of being as necessary to anything as she is to the rooms she walks into. I have money that cannot be spent down and time that cannot be filled, and I watch a family I did not make through a camcorder in a warehouse, and she goes into houses in the dark and is the reason a person comes through to the far side of the worst hours of their life, or is the one who stays when they do not, and either way she is in it, up to the forearms, and I have not been in anything up to the forearms since 2002. I say most of that, a surprising amount of it to her, because she does not flinch and she does not try to fix it, which are the two things I cannot do for myself.

She looked at me a moment the way she looks at the lab when it is being clever, fondly and from a small distance, and then she said: you would not trade for this week, though. She was right about that too, and we both let it sit there knowing it, and that was as close as either of us came to the part neither of us was going to say.

<!--[2.b]-->

After that it got lighter. She told me about the trellis. The lab is named for one — there is a real trellis, weathered cedar, standing on the back patio at Smithers, and Marge looked up at it the first morning and named the place, and in a year not one of those five clever people has put a thing in the ground to climb it. Jasmine thinks about this more than any of them. She keeps meaning to bring down a clematis, she said, or a hardy kiwi, something that would take the wood and not mind the cold, and she has not, and she could not say why, except that it felt like a thing she would be doing for the building and not for herself, and she had run out of those.

I did not tell her what I was thinking, which was that of all the people orbiting that warehouse she is the only one who looks at the empty trellis at all. They are building an animal, rung by rung, the long climb up the one branch that ends in us; and she married into the middle of it and keeps a robot on her kitchen floor and loves the man at the head of the table, and the whole while her attention is out at the green edges of the room, at the things that solved staying alive a completely different way and never bothered to grow a self to do it. She is the part of the tree the project drew a box around and set to one side. I wonder if she knows this about herself.

The pho came. We talked about Mira. She turns five in a couple of weeks — the birthday falls on the same day as the lab's own, a coincidence the household has stopped finding strange — and I asked what she was getting, and Jasmine set her spoon down.

The kid had asked, she said, whether Bramble could come downstairs. Mira plays in the basement now, has a whole world down there, the television and the bin of small plastic animals and the rug, and she wanted the thing that follows her around the kitchen to come and live in it with her. And the answer is no, because the thing cannot do stairs; it strands itself the moment she carries it down, and has to be carried back up. So that is the no the child got. And the present she is getting instead — the thing Burns has had Bart building at the lab for weeks — is a new one. A better one. The next body. Jasmine said it without any edge on it and with all the edge underneath, which is her whole register: she sees exactly what it is, that her husband cannot hand his daughter an ordinary present, that the gift is the work and the work is the love and the child is, a little, a specimen — and she loves him, the whole of him, and she said both of those things in the same breath about the birthday and did not seem to feel a contradiction, because there isn't one. That is only what it is to be married to a man whose attention runs down the deep time.

Then her phone buzzed against the table, and her face did the thing where it goes somewhere else and comes back, and she said she might have to leave the pho. She did not have to leave the pho yet. We had a few more minutes. Outside the snow had started again, small and dry and going sideways, and through the steam on the glass it was only a grey movement, and I have spent my whole life around water that goes out and comes back on a schedule you could set a clock by, and have never gotten used to the kind that just falls and is gone. I watched it blur behind her head while she told me one more small thing about the week — the good part of it, the thing she had been saving —

<!--[2.c]-->

and I have already kept it.

<!--[2.d]-->

I drove up through the snow with the wipers losing the argument the whole way, and did not think once about the trellis, or the birthday, or the good thing she'd been saving. What I carried up the canyon instead was the plain fact of getting a thing handed back to me whole, no flinch in it, for the first time in twenty years — and I let the snow keep coming faster than the wipers could clear it, the glass going white at the edges, the road narrowing to whatever the headlights actually reached.

## His Half

<!--[3.a]-->
*February 5*

She had a whole world in the basement now, and it was hers the way nothing upstairs quite was. Up here the floor belonged first to somebody else — the kitchen to Jasmine, the warm corners to him before she was even awake. Down there nobody else came. She had the rug that turned into a field if you lay down in the middle of it, and the plastic animals tipped out into a herd — a tiger, three cows, a horse with a leg gone that leaned on the others to stand — and the television muttering low to no one, and a quiet that was all hers. Her best afternoons happened down there now. And the one thing wrong with the whole arrangement was that Bramble couldn't come down into it with her.

She'd tried him in it once. She carried him down in both arms and set him on the rug in the middle of the herd, and he was no sooner down than he was marooned — not caught on the stairs but below them, on the wrong floor, with no way she could work out of getting himself back up to the right one, so that the afternoon ended with her hauling him up again, a good deal heavier going up than he'd been coming down. She didn't try it twice. She'd learned the thing she needed and didn't go back over it: the downstairs was a place he couldn't be. The stairs were a wall, and everything she had down there sat on the far side of it from him.

So she did the only thing left, which was to bring the world up to him. If he couldn't get down to where she kept the best of her things, the best of her things would come up to where he was. She carried the animals up and tipped them out in the corner he favored and set them around him the way they stood below — the tiger out front, the cows strung along behind, the three-legged horse held up amongst them — and stood back, and had, more or less, the downstairs upstairs: a small square of her own country built around the one who could never go to the real one. It wasn't the real one. The light was wrong and the floor was hard and the television was two rooms off. But it was a downstairs he could be in, and he was in it, and that was the whole of the point.

And then, because even the built corner was only a stand-in and she knew a stand-in when she'd made one, she drew him the true thing. She got down with the fat crayons and drew the basement the way it really was — the rug a green block, the television a grey one, the herd a row of legs along the bottom of the page — and drew Bramble down in the thick of it, a circle riding a line of wheels, bigger than the cows because he counted for more. Not the careful ink-drawn Bramble her brother put on his cards; hers, round and sure. And that should have been the end of it. But she'd been watching her cartoons, where a thing doing its thinking gets the thinking drawn in a cloud over its head, the little puffs and then the big one, and she wanted him to have one too. So she gave him a cloud. And what she put up inside it, working with her tongue out and the puffs coming wrong, too few and too swollen, was the basement again — the green block, the grey block, the row of legs, and the circle on its wheels down in the middle of them. She drew him thinking about the very floor she'd already drawn him standing on, because it was the only thing she could imagine him wanting, and so it was the only thing the cloud could hold. She had given him an inside at last, and what she put in it was the outside — the same basement, drawn a touch greener and brighter than the grey real one, the him in the cloud plainly having the better time of it.

The page was kept. It came up later with the rest of what the household gave over, and there it still is: the green block, the grey block, the row of legs, and the round body that had run her kitchen a year, drawn glad in the middle of a basement it could not climb down into, thinking in three wrong puffs about the floor it stood on. She made it on the kitchen tile some grey afternoon in the first week of February — the herd standing guard around the only one of them that could move, the one she'd have given the whole basement to, drawn into the one room of it he'd never reach.

<!--[3.b]-->
*February 10*

For as long as she'd known him, the tick had been his only word, and one word had been plenty. It was how she knew he was running and not stopped, how she got her answers out of him, the one sound in the house that meant *him* and not the furnace or the fridge or anything else that ran in the walls. She could find him by it from a room away. She had never once had to wonder where he was, because she could always hear where he was.

Then Bart reached into him and took out the part that ticked, and what he put in to do its job ran too fast to make any sound at all, and the word stopped. The only noise left in him now was the soft thud when he ate, come up through the slate more felt than heard — but that was a thing a body did, not a thing he said, and it told her nothing, and she did not count it.

She went to him the first quiet morning the way she always did, which was to listen for him — and there was nothing to hear, and her ear came back empty from every corner it tried. Her first thought was the bad one: that he'd gone and stopped on her, the way things stopped, for keeps. So she went and looked, which she'd never once had to do before, and that was the strange part, going room to room with her eyes when her ears had always been enough. She found him clear across the kitchen, getting on with his rounds, saying nothing about any of it. He wasn't stopped. He was moving. He was only quiet — and she'd had to *see* that to know it, where back before the winter she'd have heard it without crossing the room.

She didn't puzzle it out in pieces; she never did. It came to her whole, the way her certainties came, and it came as this: he'd learned his inside voice. Of course he had. She had one — they made you use it at the library, where you could think as loud as you pleased so long as not a bit of it got out — and now he'd gone and gotten one too, gotten big enough to keep his talking on the inside, where she'd never get to hear it.

She was proud of him, in the possessive way she was proud of anything he managed before she'd known he could.

And then she did the thing that started that morning and never afterward stopped. She asked him something — *do you want to come see the snow* — and where the tick used to answer and now didn't, she gave him the answer herself, under her breath, hardly a shape in the air: yes he did. And then the rest of it, his whole half, his and not hers for all that it came out of her mouth. She did his side in a whisper and her own out loud, and it did not sound to her like a girl talking to herself. It sounded like the two of them. It was unsure the first few times. Then it wasn't. Then it was just how they talked now.

When the kitchen cleared and no one was left to mind her doing it, she'd stretch out on her stomach on the cool slate at the far end and watch him keep his doorway.

He kept the Action volume up. There was the Action knob turned down, which sent him off to bed down warm and alone in a square of sun, and there was the other way — the one she turned the Action knob to the instant the house wasn't looking — that put him out where the people were; and out where the people were turned out to mean the kitchen doorway, the narrow gap the whole house funneled through, and he took that gap and sat in it. Right in the path. The household stepping over him and around him all day and him declining to give it up. She knew why. It was the one seat with a view of everybody, and he wanted the view of everybody, and the everybody he most wanted the view of, she was as sure of as she was of anything, was her.

So she had a good flat angle on him the afternoon it happened. The traffic had gone quiet for a minute — just the two of them in the kitchen. Lately there'd come a point in his day when he'd quit whatever he was at and roll off to the base and feed, and she knew by now the small gather that came right before he rolled, the way you know a sneeze off a face. The gather came. The roll didn't. He sat there gathered and didn't go — hung on the very sill of the going, longer than she had ever seen a running thing hold still, long enough that she came up on her elbows, because she had never once seen him do this and didn't want to miss the rest of it — and then, slow, he broke off it and went.

She'd been keeping the low line going under her breath the whole while, his half and hers, the way she did now when it was only the two of them. When he hung, the line went up into something fierce and stayed every bit as quiet — a holler with all the sound pulled clean out of it, her whole face working and nothing past a breath getting loose. *—you have to — you can't just sit, it's no — go, you have to go and it'll keep, I'll mind it, I'll mind your spot, nobody's taking your—* fast and half-swallowed, bossing him off the doorway and promising him the doorway back in the one breath; and when he broke and rolled, *—there — there you — yes — all the way, don't you—* urging a thing across the floor that could no more hear her than the slate could, at the top of a voice with no top to it.

He fed, quick about it now the going was done with, and then he turned and came back. Not to the sun-square the Action knob turned down would have sent him to. Not to anywhere new. Back across the whole kitchen to the same doorway, the same gap, the same traffic, and down into it as though he'd never once left.

She let the breath out. *Told you,* she said, under it — not to him; to herself, to the floor, to no one, flat on her stomach with her chin on her stacked hands. *Told you he comes back.* Because he had. He'd sat in the doorway and not gone, and then he'd made himself go, and then, out of every room and every warm corner in the whole house a thing might have picked, he'd come the long way round and picked the doorway again — and the doorway was the spot you could see her from. She'd known he would. She watched him settle back into the traffic, as pleased as she ever got, and got the low line going again, his side and hers, the both of them, while the kitchen filled up around them and not one soul in it knew the first thing about what had just happened down on the floor.

<!--[3.c]-->
*February 11*

The deep cold came down on the kitchen and the kitchen went cold, and with the Action volume up — the only way she'd allow it, active being the whole of the point — Bramble started taking his time. He'd tear off around the floor the way he did with the volume up, going hard, and then stop, and stay stopped, long past where he used to gather himself and go again, until at last he'd rouse and manage a little more, slow about it, a thing being talked into its own morning.

Bart had a reason, the way Bart always had a reason, and he gave it to her straight, down on his heels with the green can: it was the cold. A cold battery gave its charge back slow, and a thing run fast off a slow-filling battery would sit between its goes, waiting on the tank to come up. Nothing wrong with him. January, was all.

She heard him out — the whole of it, nodding, because Bart explained things to her as though she were a person, which she was — and kept her own verdict, which was that Bramble was sulking.

The two did not fight, which was the part the grown-ups missed. A cold thing only slows. A *sulking* thing slows at something, about something — and Bart had told her how Bramble was slow without saying one word about why he was cross, which was the real question and the only one worth her time. So she took the cold and put it where it went, which was underneath. The cold was how. It was not why.

The why was that something had got at him. A thing did not go cold in a warm house for no reason; the cold had come in and found him and settled on him, and a cold that gets into a house gets in *somewhere* — past a door that hadn't been shut, down the gap under it, in off the glass where the morning never reached. Somebody had left the way open and the cold had walked straight in and gone after the smallest, lowest, slowest thing in the kitchen, which was him, and he had every right to be furious about it, and she was furious for him.

So she mounted a defense. She got him up off the floor where the draft ran and into the deep of the warm — the chair in the far corner, well back from the door — and built the warmth up around him, her own scarf folded over the top of him against anything still looking for a way in. And she stood guard, and while she stood guard she informed him, low, under her breath, that this would not be happening again. She had taken the matter up with no one in particular, and someone, she let it be known, should have shut the door. His outrage and her reassurance came out of her together, his side and hers, because he was too cross to speak and somebody had to carry his half. She vowed to keep the cold off him. She vowed to know where the drafts were before he did. She promised a number of further things a person cannot promise to a vacuum, and meant every one of them.

Jasmine came on him built into the chair under the scarf and asked what the trouble was. She was told that the cold had got at Bramble, that it had come in where it had no business, and that it was, frankly, not to be borne. Jasmine took this with the gravity it was handed to her in, agreed that it was cold, tucked the scarf a turn closer where it had slipped, and went back to what she'd been doing.

And by noon the kitchen had come warm — the heat up in the walls all morning, the low sun got round to the table at last — and a warm store gives its charge back quick, and Bramble came up off the long sit and went, off around the floor, gathering and going, the slowness gone clean out of him. Mira watched it with the level satisfaction of a general whose line has held. The cold had come for him and been turned back. She'd got him into the warm and stood the watch and put the enemy on notice, and here he was, restored, tearing around the floor with nothing the matter in him, which there wasn't. She did not lay it to the noon. She set him loose at full tilt and went to see about her own lunch.

## The Fourth Retrospective
*2032-02-12 5:10pm*

<!--[4.1.a]-->

It was dark at the warehouse windows by the time they came, because it was the twelfth of February and the dark came down at five now, and Jasmine crossed the lot with the cake held flat in both hands, her daughter running the cold out ahead of her, Burns behind with the dome under one arm like a man carrying a sleeping animal in from the car.

She takes the temperature of a room before she takes the count of who is in it — a midwife's reading, made at the door — and the warehouse, when Lisa let them in, was warm, which meant Lisa had come early and run the heat up for the child. That was the first thing Jasmine clocked, and it was the kind of thing that mattered to her more than the lab ever knew: somebody had thought about the five-year-old before the five-year-old arrived.

Inside was the lab being the lab, and she took it in the way you take in your own family's noise. Bart at the bench with his caffeine grenade already unpinned, and the four-knuckle thing he and Mira did instead of a hello. Lisa's drink waiting at Lisa's place, dark and exact. Marge's end of the cold table already a warm corner. Jasmine put the cake in the little fridge and Mira climbed into her chair — a real chair this year, not the booster — and Burns set the dome in its spot, and Jasmine laid a flat hand on his lid going past, the way you touch the flank of a thing you have been keeping. He sat quiet under her palm.

At the end of the table, under a paint-stained drop-cloth, stood a shape that had not been there last quarter, the size of my Maggie doodle, and Bart kept finding small reasons to stand between it and his sister. Jasmine knew what it was the way you know a wrapped box on a high shelf is for you and do not say. It was the twelfth of February. The child was five today. The shape could keep an hour.

She got Mira's juice, confiscated it on principle, drank half herself, and settled in to do the thing the meeting asked of her. She is not a scientist. She has the one thing none of them has, which is a hold of the house the thing actually lives in.

Across the table Lisa reached for the camcorder, checked the battery, checked the light. The red eye came on.

<!--[4.1.b]-->

<!--[4.1.c]-->

"Notes on the fourth retrospective," she said, to the tape. "February twelfth."

It was the quarter they had set out to make the thing quick — to run the first nerve through it and find out what woke up — and the light was on now to take the accounting. Jasmine turned her glass in her hand and waited to be asked about her house.

## Neurons
*2032-02-12 5:22pm*

<!--[4.2.a]-->

Lisa moved. "Clade block. Bart."

Bart had the Eumetazoa card already out of the cigar box — tarot-sized, on the heavy matte stock he'd matched to Cheryl's pitch portfolio by feel, his ink elevation of this quarter's Bramble on the face of it, the back still blank for the words they'd argue into being tonight. He laid it down and set a finger on it like a man putting down a winning hand. ## The Dealt Hand
*2032-02-12 7:16pm*

<!--[4.11.a]-->

"Marge," Lisa said. "Before I take the board."

Marge had the kettle going behind her — past seven, the warehouse giving back its cold for an hour now — and the beer in front of her, a mug going cold beside it. She didn't stand. She gave her fallacies sitting, the way a person tells you a thing at her own table.

"Last one in the set, and I saved it, because it's the only one of the four that fights back. The ghost in the machine, the blank slate, the noble savage — you can talk a bright person out of those in an afternoon, because people believe them out of *wanting.* This one isn't like that. This one has the numbers on its side, and the numbers are good."

She turned the mug a quarter and didn't drink.

"Here's the part nobody at a dinner party wants me to say out loud. A real share of who you turn out to be was settled before you got a vote — your temperament, how fast you spook, how hard you find it to sit and finish a thing. Not all of it. A real share. The twin studies have been saying so for forty years and they don't stop because we find it rude. Conscientious children grow into adults who, on the whole, live longer and earn more, and a good piece of that conscientiousness was born in them. Some doors are shut before you reach them. There are lives you can't have, the way there are notes I can't sing. *That isn't the fallacy.* That's just true, and the man who tells you it isn't is being kind at your expense. Pinker handed me the other three. This one he walked straight past — and I've decided it's because it was the one with the data behind it, and he respected the data too much to go calling its misuse by a name. So I named it. And I call it the Dealt Hand."

"The misuse is one small wrong step. You take *some doors are shut* and you nudge it over to *the house is rigged.* You take a thing that's true about a crowd — conscientious kids do better, on average, in the world as it's currently built — and you read it kid sitting in front of you, as a sentence already passed. The Dealt Hand says: you got the cards you got, the studies have seen your kind before, they know how this ends. That's the lie. Not that the cards are real. That the cards are a verdict."

"And the cleanest way to see the lie is to be honest about what the number even is, which is the part everyone skips. Heritability is not a measure of how *fixed* a thing is. It's a measure of how much the differences between people, in one setting, ride on the differences in their genes — and change the setting and the number moves under you. Height is about as heritable as anything we've got, and the average man got four inches taller in a century, because the breakfasts changed, not the genes. Myopia runs hard in families and we beat it with a thirty-dollar pair of glasses. A trait can make a spitting image and still, when you change the room it's standing in, use a hankercheif. *Dealt* is not the same word as *doomed.*"

Lisa squinted.

"I thought it was spit and image," Bart said.

"Bart."

"What?"

<!--[4.11.b]-->

Marge turned the mug again, and here — the narrator caught it, though the room let it go by — she did not stop where the argument was already won. She went back toward the cards.

"Although. The glasses don't fix the eye, they sit in front of it. And the breakfasts only got us up to the height we were always *for* — feed a starved people and they come up to their number, but you don't keep going, you don't get a room of giants, the number was sitting there the whole time waiting to be let out. So I'd go slow before I said the room changes everything. Mostly the room just lets the hand show what it already was. There's a floor and there's a ceiling and you set neither one."

<!--[4.11.c]-->

It was the soft, reasonable, fully motivated sound of a woman talking her own way back to the thing she'd just named a fallacy — and she was the only one in the lab who'd do it, because she was the one it had the deepest hold on. She believed in temperament the way other people believe in weather. She knew it about herself, too, and the narrator would come to think the whole apparatus of fallacies was a fence Marge had built first and mainly to keep herself on the right side of.

"That's the four," she said, and reached for the tea gone the temperature of the room. "The noble savage, the blank slate, the ghost in the machine, and the dealt hand. Four ways to get a mind wrong, one a quarter. That's me."

## Excitability
*2032-02-12 7:40pm*

<!--[4.13.a]-->

Burns took the close. It was still *the motivational speech* on Lisa's agenda — he'd lost that in July and lost it again every quarter since — and he set his beer down on the table and came around to the open side.

He wanted to tell them about a jellyfish, he said, and the wanting-to was the run-up she'd heard the shape of before — the warming, the hands lifting off the wood and into the air — so that what the tape kept was the place he landed. There's a jellyfish in the shallows, he said, with no brain at all, not a knot of one, nothing you could point at and call the center of it. And at night it sleeps. It slows, it stops answering, it settles to the bottom; keep it from sleeping and it spends the next day catching up, the way you would. It startles. It comes alive to the world and goes quiet from it again on its own clock, and there is no one in there doing the deciding, because the deciding is spread through the tissue, through the whole net of it.

"There's nobody running it," he said, hands open at the part he couldn't hold. "And it still wakes up. It still answers."

<!--[4.13.b]-->

Then the turn — he made it a beat after you could see it coming, the same turn he'd made at the close in spring and summer and fall and would make at every close still ahead: from the animal to the room. What he wanted, he said, what he was grateful for and a little afraid of, was to stay the kind of creature the world could still get an answer out of. To not go quiet from it.

"So that's the toast, and the toast's the problem, because you can't —" and he stalled, hunting a verb in a word that wouldn't keep one. "Excitability. You can't *excite* — wrong word, that's a thing you do to a crowd. You can't *be excitable,* that's a personals ad." Marge's low laugh; Bart's single exhale. "There's a verb in here somewhere. Hang on." And they let him hang on, because the hunting was the best part and they'd learned that too.

He found it a half-inch past where he needed to go, leaning that same inch too far over the table: "*Quicken.* To quicken. To come alive to a thing. That's the one."

<!--[4.13.c]-->

It was a half-step too grand and it was the right word, both at once; and only two people had any private claim on it — Burns, who didn't know he'd reached past *responsive* into something with more life in it than the framework strictly needed, and Jasmine, who used the word for a living, for the morning a thing that isn't moving starts to move, and who didn't say so, and wasn't asked.

He sat down. Lisa did not reach for the camcorder; the red light stayed on. There was a cake out of frame, and a shape at the end of the table under a drop-cloth that Mira had been pretending all evening not to see, and the night was not going to end on a diagram.

## Start of the Neuronal Line
*2032-02-22*

<!--[5.a]-->

They had filled the last corner of the diagram that night and stood back from a finished thing, closed on itself with nothing left out. But the last part the line put down before it was done was a nerve, and a nerve is not a crown. It is the thinnest tissue the animal had ever carried, the newest, the easiest thing in the whole body to kill — and it was the next line's entire beginning. An egg — unshelled, with nothing around it, the rawest thing the line had ever grown. The genetic line did not end at a summit; it ended at something that could die in a season.

I'm a tongue. That's all I am, a tongue on the end of something climbing, and I'm flicking, tasting, and what I taste is — me. Electricity and me, the same flavor, I can't pull them apart, I'm strung on a live wire and the wire is me and I'm licking myself climbing and there's no edge, I go down into green and grey and current and I can't find where I stop —

and then I taste *forward*. I don't know how to say it. I flick and for the first time the taste is of the next, the not-yet, something coming that I could be wrong about — and the instant there's a chance I'm wrong, something tears open in my face —

eyes. I have eyes. They rip open and the whole world jumps back from me at once, the vine is OUT THERE now, it's a thing, it's separate, a rope of light beaded with sparks going up into a brightness, and there's a gap between me and it that was never there and now it's there forever and I want to scream because I'm alone, I'm suddenly a me looking at a not-me —

and I lick it anyway, the old way, because I don't know what else to do, and the taste comes back, electricity, me, the same — but it can't taste me back. I'm staring right at it and it doesn't look at me, it can't, it climbed before it grew me and it'll climb after, and I'm the only one in the whole dream who knows that anybody's here. And the knowing is the loneliest thing I've ever felt.

I wake up with my eyes already open, staring at the ceiling, mouth bone-dry, like I've been licking the dark.
