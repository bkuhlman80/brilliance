## Build log — The last slot
*Bart, hardware. Filed December 2, 2032, for the team.*

<!--[1.a]-->
Ran it back to the house myself this time — Lisa had a frost-dark thing to be at, and the slate was warm, and the kid was up. It went in on its own four legs and put itself over the stove vent before I had my coat off.

Here's the one I've been waiting two quarters to build.

Last fall I told you the thing ran a model and ran it on the world — the door, the bowl, the leash on the hook — and that you could point that model at the kid and get nothing, because it didn't know what Mira was about to do, it knew what the leash meant, and those weren't the same thing and the second one wasn't that build's to make.

This is that build.

It points at the people now. Same model, swung off the furniture and onto the family — it runs Mira the way it ran the bowl, reads where her hand's going before it gets there, same on Jasmine, on Burns, on the old vacuum trundling the hall. There's a new knob on it too.

The screen got more room and a new trick I'm going to let Lisa walk you through. Short version: it can put a person up there now, not just a thing — who it's reading, and what it figures they're up to. I built it somewhere to write that.

<!--[1.b]-->
The knob sets how much it minds what the read tells it. Stamped on it: Agreeable, which is Marge's word and a fine one, so it's the Agreeable knob —

a volume knob on Agreeable, same shape as the rest, a crank you wind up and down. Agreeable volume up and the thing gives way — reads the kid coming for the warm spot and hangs back, lets her have it, goes off and finds a worse one. Agreeable volume down and it takes — reads her coming and gets there first, sits down, won't move. Same read either end. The knob's only the minding.

What it still can't do, and you'll feel this one by spring: it can read the kid now and it can't say a word to her. She talks to it every night — asks it, I'm told, whether it had a good day — and it reads her asking and has no way on God's earth to answer. It knows the question's pointed at it. It can't give the answer back. Not this build's to fix.

<!--[1.c]-->
Calls. House mostly, this round, because the build's a knob and the knob only matters in the house.

One: Agreeable volume up, at a shared warm spot he yields — lets the kid have it, lets the vacuum pass, takes the lesser warmth and doesn't fuss. Volume down, he holds the spot and makes them go around. You'll call the setting across the kitchen by the second week, same as all the others.

Two: he gets to a shared thing ahead of the kid on volume down, because he read her reaching, and lets her beat him there on volume up. That one's checkable to the second — watch the hand and watch the legs.

Three: the two of them in the hall. Volume up, the big one routes around the little one. Volume down, the little one routes around the big one, or tries to, and the big one sits in the lane.

Tear this up in three months if, at Agreeable volume up, he ever once takes the warm spot off the kid. He won't. The cold spot costs him more than it costs her — the furnace burns his charge down faster working a cold floor — and he'll hand the warm over anyway, because the knob says to, and the knob's mine, and the knob works.

— Bartholabu

## The Cold Side of the Floor

<!--[3.a]-->
*December 7*

The sitter watched her. Sitters did — it was most of the job, as far as Mira could tell, to take the good chair and watch you have your afternoon and say *careful* at the parts that didn't need it. Mira had been watched all afternoon with no way to prove it and no grounds to mind, watching being the one thing a grown-up can do to you that doesn't count as doing anything.

Then the bot, down on the floor near her, lifted its head from whatever it was nosing along the baseboard and put a person up on the glass. Not its own line, not Mira's — a third name, lettered across its forehead with a guess strung after it: `SITTER { SELECTING [ WATCH MIRA ] }`. The thing she'd had no proof of all afternoon, lit up in three plain words.

What came up in her was not unease but the hot clean joy of a witness. The bot had caught the sitter at it. She wasn't making it up; somebody else had seen; and the somebody was *hers,* down at her own level and not up on the good chair, and it had been watching the watcher the whole time.

She got up and informed the sitter that the robot knew. The sitter, who had been told a great many things that afternoon, looked at her own name on the glass and allowed that she had been watching, yes; it was what she was there for. Mira took the *yes* for a full confession and went back to the floor satisfied, the watching a thing two of them knew about now instead of one.

She did not look to see whether her own name was up at the same moment, lettered the same way, watching the sitter right back.

<!--[3.c]-->
*December 12*

One afternoon a thing rolled under the table and she went around the back of him to get it, and her name didn't come up on the glass.

That had never happened. Every crossing of the kitchen she'd ever made, the glass had her before she'd half decided where she was going — her name, and a guess at what she was after, lit on his forehead a step ahead of her own feet, so she arrived everywhere already announced. But behind him, low, with his head turned the wrong way, there was nothing of her on him at all. He hadn't got her face. And a thing that hadn't got your face — she tried it twice more before she'd trust it — couldn't put you up in lights before you got where you were going.

There was a place behind him where she didn't exist yet.

So she used it. The animal crackers lived on the second shelf, and between meals the second shelf was the law, and the straight way to them ran across his front, where the glass would have her wanting them before her hand was up. The back way ran behind him, along the base of the cupboards, low, in the stretch his turned head couldn't reach — and she took it on hands and knees with her breath going the whole way, the raid talked through under itself at a pitch meant for nobody, *—down, stay down— he's facing the door, he's still facing the door— now, before he—* half the words swallowed before they cleared her lips, the sneak and the announcer of the sneak the same girl, both at full pitch and neither one above a whisper. She made the shelf with her name nowhere on him. She made it back the same way, crackers in her fist, and checked: the glass had the door, and the vacuum, and the leg of the table. It did not have her.

For the length of that crossing she was the one nothing saw coming. She'd got behind the thing that always had her and come up on it unawares for once, arrived before herself, and she stayed there a moment crouched in the gap where she didn't exist — the stolen crackers in one fist, and the whole unwatched kitchen around her.

<!--[3.a]-->
*December 17*

She'd beaten him once and got greedy, and the second time she tried it in the open. She thought the trick was bigger than it was: that if you could beat the glass from behind, you could fool it to its face.

So she feinted — made for the hall with her whole body, big and obvious, every line of her saying *hall* — while what she actually meant was to double back to the cupboard the second his read had placed her in the wrong room. She was proud of how the hall looked on her.

The glass posted the cupboard.

`MIRA { WANTING [ OPEN CUPBOARD ] }`, up on his forehead while her arms were still aimed the other way — the word for the thing she hadn't moved toward, had told no one, had dressed her whole body against. It posted the want with the costume still on her, the want on the glass and the hall still on her body, and the glass was the one that was right.

She stood with her arms out toward the door she didn't want and looked at the word for the thing she did.

He had lovely manners. She'd been keeping an eye on it and the evidence was in: at a doorway he hung back and let her go first; in the hall he pulled to one side for her; when they both reached for the same thing he stopped and left it to her, every time, with a small settling-back that she read as *after you.* You didn't have to teach a thing that. He either had nice manners or he didn't, and he did.

She decided somebody had raised him right.

She was prouder of his manners than of any of the clever things he could do — prouder of the hanging-back than of all the tricks the grown-ups went on about — because the tricks were the lab's and the manners were his. The one part nobody had built. The part that was just nice.

She told the cat it could learn something. The cat, who barged through doorways and had never once given anyone the right of way, declined to.

<!--[3.b]-->
*December 22*

She still comes up on him the old way — soft across the kitchen, hands going down to scoop him out of whatever he's at, the way you lift a cat that hasn't heard you. For two years it worked. She could have him up and warm against her before he'd noticed the floor had changed under him, and the catching of him unawares was half of why she did it.

This winter she went to do it and he was already turned.

Not startled round — turned, square to her, and watching: the long steady watch he kept on her now, that found her face across the room and stayed on it. He'd had her the whole way over. By the time her hands went down he had gone still and easy and compact, settled for the lift before she reached him, and he came up into her arms with nothing in him surprised — a weight already gathered to be carried, that had seen her coming.

He weighed exactly what he had always weighed. He had only stopped being a thing she could come up on.

The radiator was his by every right she could see, and he gave it to her.

That was just how it went now. The good heat under the front window was the warm place in the kitchen, and the warm was the first thing he went to — and then when she came he came off it: got himself up and crossed to the bare tile along the cold outside wall and lay down there instead, on the part of the floor the heat never reached, and left her the radiator. Every time. She'd stopped being surprised by it. It was the standing fact of him, like the cold coming up through that wall in February, a thing the house simply did.

She lay along the warm a while and was warm. He lay along the cold a while and the glass on his forehead said nothing about it, the way it said nothing about most of what was true.

Then she got up off the good heat, and went over to the cold wall, and lay down on the bare tile beside him.

The floor was as cold as she'd known it would be. She got her shoulder against his cold flank and her cheek down on the tile and stayed, both of them along the wall now, out where the heat didn't come, and she did not warm him and could not have and was not trying to. She'd left the good spot empty behind her. It went on being warm with no one in it, and the two of them lay in the cold.

## Gourmandise
*January 28, 2033*

<!--[2.a]-->
Jasmine brought her at one and did not stay. She had a thing to eat alone next door — a real lunch, sat down, the first in a week she would not have to take standing at someone's bedside — and she set Mira in the chair across from me with a kiss on the part of her hair and a bag with a notebook in it and the instruction, to the child and not to me, to *be good and don't make Cheryl chase you,* and went off to Salt & Olive to have her hour. So it was the two of us, in the warm front room of a bakery on a January afternoon, the case lit gold beside us and the cold pressed up against the glass and held there, the way the weather gets held off a thing on the lee side of a rock.

<!--[2.c]-->
I had watched this child for two years through a lens the size of a thumbnail. I had never once been in a room where she could see me back.

<!--[2.b]-->
She looked at me the way she looks at the machine, which is to say for a long time and without hurry, taking the inventory a small grave person takes when no one has told her not to stare. On the tape she had read as one child; here she read as another. The footage had given her a flat Scandinavian blonde and a pair of pale eyes; the room gave her hair with some brass down in it and eyes that could not decide on a color, green in the gold light off the case and then not green when she turned her head — the kind of coloring, I would learn, that does not sit still for a camera and never had. The lens had been canning her for two years. Here was the live one, gap-toothed, a new tooth coming in crooked and working down into the gap, swinging her feet under a chair built for a larger person, watching me try to remember how a person begins.

I had ordered her a chocolate thing the girl behind the case said children liked, and it was the right call and the wrong size, and she went at it with both hands and got most of it in and wore the rest — a comma of chocolate at the corner of her mouth, a flake of pastry on her cheek —

<!--[2.c]-->
and I felt the old reflex come up in me, the hand that wants to reach across and put a face right, and I did not move it.

<!--[2.b]-->
I looked at the chocolate at the corner of her mouth and left it there and asked her a stiff grown-up question about school.

It went nowhere, the way the first one always does. She gave it the flat dutiful answer children give the questions adults ask in order to be asking something, and a small silence opened, and I understood that if I went on interviewing her I would get an hour of the manners her mother had installed at the door. So I stopped. I let the silence sit. And after a while she reached into the bag herself — because the silence had made a room for the thing she had brought and not for the things I had — and put the notebook on the table between the plates and turned it around to face me and opened it to a page near the back, and said, "These are what he's thinking."

The drawings were what the grown-ups had told me they were and were also more than that. Each page was the same head — a box, two eyes set a little wrong, one higher than the other, the way she draws it — and lettered into the forehead, where the real one carries its word, a word of her own. But the words were not the present-tense ones off the screen. They were pinned to days. She walked me through them with the patience of a curator who has all afternoon, a page at a time: this one was the morning the cat got it cornered by the bookshelf, and the word in the forehead was CAT, and the drawing was not from that morning but from a Tuesday weeks after, when she had watched it go and stand by the bookshelf for no reason she could see, and had known, the way you know a thing about someone you live with, that it had gone there because of the cat. "He went back," she said. "I drew what he went back for."

I asked her — carefully, because I could feel the size of the thing and did not want to put my thumb through it — how she knew that was the one it was thinking.

She thought about this with her whole face. "Wait. Wait, wait." She held a hand up, the crumb-and-chocolate hand, parking me where I was while she got it right, and the getting-it-right took a moment, and I waited, and the waiting was the first easy thing that had happened at the table. "I don't know it," she said finally. "It can't tell me. So I pick the one. And then I write it down so it doesn't get lost." She looked at the page, and then at me, to see whether I had understood the part that mattered. "Somebody has to keep them. Or there's nobody keeping them."

<!--[2.c]-->
I have spent my life building a record of things that could not speak for themselves — a bay that died before I was born, a family that canned it and got rich and never asked why the fish had stopped, four people in a warehouse who cannot see the shape they are standing inside. I keep the record the way my people kept everything, which is by preserving the dead version of it, the footage running whether or not the living thing knows it is being kept. This child had come at the same work from the other end. She did not wait for the thing to be dead to keep it, and she did not wait for it to prove it had an inside before she granted it one. She picked the inside it could not give her, and wrote it down so it would not be lost, and she was right that somebody had to, and wrong, I knew, by every standard Marge had spent two years defending, and I sat across from her unable to tell, for once, which of us was keeping the truer record.

<!--[2.b]-->
"Show me the rest," I said, and she did, and we went through the notebook to the front, back through the fall and the summer to a page where the box had a little knob in its forehead instead of a word — the oldest drawing, she said, from before it had words to think, when all that was in there was the setting — and somewhere in the going-back the table stopped being one I was conducting across and became one we were both leaning over, our heads close, her finger and then my finger on the pages. When she turned to a drawing that had made her laugh to make — a page where she had given the head a hat, because it had been cold and she had decided it should have one — she laughed again to show me, the full uncontainable kind that has not yet learned it is sometimes a choice, and the chocolate was still at the corner of her mouth, and I reached across with my napkin and wiped it away.

<!--[2.c]-->
I did it before I knew I was going to. My hand went out across sixty years of keeping it to itself and put the child's face right, the way I have watched a thousand mothers do it without thinking and never once done myself, and she let me, the way she lets the cat get her cornered and lets the machine see itself home, without marking it as anything, and went on turning pages. I do not have a clean account of why the hand went then and not at the start. The chocolate had been there the whole hour. What changed was on my side of the table, and I am not going to pretend to map it. I will only set it down: there is a difference between watching a living thing and getting a hand on it, and I have spent my life on the first side of that difference, and for the length of a wiped cheek in a bakery in January I was on the other one.

<!--[2.b]-->
Jasmine came back at the end of her hour looking like a person who had eaten a meal sitting down, which on her is a luxury you can read from across a room, and Mira closed the notebook and put it back in the bag herself. I told Jasmine, as the bag went shut on it, that the drawings ought to be kept with the record from now on — and that she should be the one to raise it with the lab, since I would not be in the room to do it. Mira slid off the too-big chair, restored to her mother's gravity, *be good* still holding.

At the door she turned around — not to me; to the case, the gold one, doing the inventory again — and asked her mother whether they could bring one home for the machine, and Jasmine said the machine doesn't eat, baby, and Mira said *I know,* in the flat five-year-old's *I know* she was two weeks from outgrowing, and then said it could still have one, it didn't have to eat it, and went out into the cold holding her mother's hand and the bag with the kept insides in it.

<!--[2.c]-->
I sat a while after they had gone. I had another year of watching this child through a lens ahead of me before I would be in a room with her again, the winter she turned seven, and I did not know that yet, and it would not have changed the afternoon if I had. The case lit gold. The cold held off the glass. There was a comma of chocolate on the corner of my own napkin, and I found I did not want to throw it away, which is the whole of what I am, and I have stopped apologizing for it.

<!--[2.d]-->
I did not go straight to the car; I sat a while longer with the napkin cold in my hand before I made myself leave. I drove up the canyon with the heater on too high and the radio off, and got to the top of it still holding whatever had come loose in the bakery — got out of the car holding it, went into the house holding it, set it down nowhere. It was still in my hands when I went to bed.

## The Eighth Retrospective
*2033-02-12 4:45pm*

<!--[4.1.a]-->
The twelfth of February came in cold and early dark, the mountains gone to flat blue paper against the last of the light and the Granary holding its few lit windows the way a held breath holds warmth. The lab counted its own age off this date the way it counted Mira's, because they were the same date — the warehouse had had its first morning two years before, on the morning she turned four — and nobody at Smithers had ever sorted out which of the two anniversaries the night belonged to, which is the kind of overlap a careful person leaves alone.

She came in through the front with her mother, six years old as of that morning and carrying nothing, which was the new thing. The warm one came in beside her on its own four legs, clicking on the concrete, swinging its head to read the cold room for somewhere to put itself — and finding the radiator under the front windows before it had well cleared the door, and going to stand over it the way it stood over the stove vent at home, because Bart had built it a furnace, and a furnace burns hardest in the cold, and the warm could not wait on the meeting. Mira let it go and did not look back to see it settle, the way you stop watching a thing you have stopped having to carry. Last year she would have had it against her hip the whole way in. The year before that she would not have let it walk.

Inside it was warm and bright and smelled faintly of sugar. Lisa was at the camcorder end where she lived, the red light dark under her thumb and a bramble already going to water in its jar at her elbow, reading the battery charge once. Bart had the cigar box at his place and a pint of something green going flat beside it and both hands already busy in a sensor. Marge held the cold end with the kettle behind her and a beer at her elbow, and a white bakery box she had carried in herself and set where Bart could not reach it, the candles being her department and having been since the child was old enough to have a number on a cake. Burns crossed the floor to Jasmine, the cheek and the question about the drive, and she told him there was ice on the 201 and they'd come around the long way — true, and sized exactly to the asking.

Jasmine did not sit yet. She went to the back door instead and opened it on the cold, to look at the trellis on the patio — the only one of them who ever looked at it — and Mira went with her. The bramble had taken the cold the way the thing was built to take it, which is to say it had done nothing you would call thriving and had not died either: still gripping the slats it was given, dark and dormant, nothing showing. Jasmine got a hand on a cane and bent it and found it live under the bark and said so, more to the plant than to the girl. Mira gave it a grave hello. And then, because there was by now a bramble in a jar at Lisa's elbow and a bramble out on the radiator minding its own heat and a bramble on the trellis that needed no one to mind it, and the child kept them sorted, she told the one on the radiator to say hello to the one outside. The warm one read her ask and gave nothing back, having no way yet to say hello to anyone; and Mira, who took the not-answering for shyness because no one had told her it was anything else, let it be and came in out of the cold.

Mira bumped Bart and took the stool by Marge, and a glass of juice arrived at her elbow without either of the women who loved her seeming to have sent it — a glass now, not a box, a small promotion she had not asked for and did not mark, the box gone the way the carrying was gone, by attrition, nobody clocking the day it stopped.

There was a thing on the far bench she had clocked from the door, man-shaped and standing under a drop cloth, taller than she was, that had not been in the room last quarter. She gave it the long solemn look and asked nothing, and Lisa said *later* over the top of her head before the look was through — *it's a birthday thing; we'll keep it a birthday thing* — in the voice she kept for the quarters with a present hiding in them. Mira took the *later* and parked it, being a child who could park a thing, and went back to her juice. The cloth did not move all through the clade block, or the Bramble block, or the vote; the room had agreed, without a meeting on it, that the night would get to the bench when it was ready and not before.

When they had arranged themselves around the fact of the cake without quite admitting that was the thing they were circling, Lisa said the cake came at the end of the Bramble block, "and the birthday thing stays a birthday thing till the cake," which was as far as she would take it with the child two feet away — and pressed the light on.

<!--[4.1.b]-->
Jasmine had carried an errand through the whole arrival and held it until the light was on. The funder had sent it home from the bakery in January: the drawings were to go with the tape from here on. She set Mira's notebook on the table, the one from the lunch, and said as much and no more. Lisa took it the way she took a labeled cane or a charged cell, and stood it at the camera end where the record ran. Mira watched her own book change hands. "Good," she said, and told her not to bend the old ones, and went back to her juice.

<!--[4.1.c]-->
Burns was up before it had finished warming. He gave them the ambition plainly, as the lab's own and nobody's assignment: they had set out to seat the last knob in the last open slot, the fourth, the one Bart had spent a year certain he would outgrow the board before he filled and had not — and to make it the knob that set how much the thing minded the people it lived among — the Agreeable knob. With that one seated, he said, the brain line was full: four slots, four drives, a year's climb standing finished on the board in front of them, and the whole of a line theirs to walk at once for the first time — not a capacity at a time up out of the dark, but the finished set, laid end to end, while it lay across the room over the radiator with its forehead still going. He looked like a man who meant to take the long way through it. He told Bart to put up the card.

## Mentalizing
*2033-02-12 5:15pm*

<!--[4.2.a]-->
"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart had it out of the cigar box before the word was finished. "PRI-mate. *Now with opinions about the neighbors.*"

"Thank you," Marge said, into her beer.

"This is the one we almost are," Bart said, which was a degree off what he said at every clade, and meant it. "Hands that grab — front and back, the feet too, for the ones still up the tree. Eyes swung around to the front of the face so the two of them land on the one thing and the brain can do the math on how far. A skull going up like rent. Up on two legs, eventually, on the branch that's us. And the first thing the whole tree of life ever made *on purpose* and kept making — a rock knapped down to an edge, three million years back, by a hand that meant to." He tapped the middle of the card. "Burns has the animal, and he's been waiting on this one all year, so I'm getting out of his way the second I do the part I get."

The part he got was the old card. He turned the mammal one half over in his hand, the rat-in-the-dark. "Last clade, this thing ran the whole world in its head. The maze before it swam it, the bowl behind the table leg, the leash on the hook — best map-runner the tree ever grew, and I said so, I was proud of it. And you could take that map and aim it at another rat and get back a warm thing that moved. Furniture with a pulse. It ran a model of the *world* and never once ran a model of the *modeler.* The other animal in the room was weather — stuff that happened, not somebody it happened to."

"Not couldn't," Marge said, the beer going still on the table before the sentence she meant. "There was nowhere in it for the other one's insides to go. You can't keep track of what your neighbor is thinking in a room with no shelf for it. What's new at the primate isn't a sharper guess about the neighbor. It's that the neighbor's head became a thing there was finally somewhere to put."

<!--[4.2.b]-->
"Mentalizing," Lisa said, and put her finger on the word in the middle of the card. "The mammal modeled the world. This one models the *modeler* — it keeps a running model of another animal as a thing with its own aims, its own view, a picture of the world that isn't your picture. And then the part that's new" — palm flat, palm vertical, rotating — "it can model *that.* A model of another mind's model. It can hold what you can see that it can't, and what you believe that's wrong, and move on the gap." She kept her eyes on the room, not the card. "It's fresh hardware — the front of the brain gone fine-grained, wired back to a patch where the lobes meet that fires for other people in particular. And it's a *stack,* not a swap: it keeps everything it already had and lays one new move on top — the same model the mammal pointed at the world, pointed now at another animal. The top of the stack is reading minds."

She let that sit, and then did the thing she did when a clean story was about to get less clean. "And the standard why is that the brain got big *for exactly this* — not for tools, not for food, but for keeping track of each other. Bigger troop, more relationships to model, more load on the part that models. Brains for company." The nose-laugh almost arrived. "It's a good story and it's shakier than it sounds. Run the numbers carefully and the link barely holds — the math'll 'predict' a troop size somewhere between about two and five hundred, which is a long way of saying it predicts nothing, and what a primate *eats* calls its brain size about as well as how social it is. So sociality is *part* of why the brain's expensive. Not the whole bill. I'd rather hand you that than the bumper sticker."

<!--[4.2.c]-->
"Four ways the reading shows up," she went on, "and the framework's been pointing at this clade all year, because it's the cleanest — the four drop right out of two gauges crossed with the drives. Watch. The first is *standing.* A monkey doesn't only know it lost the fight; it knows the others *saw* it lose, and marks its own worth down by what the watchers think now. Reputation, before there's a word for it — a tally of yourself kept in other animals' eyes. The second is the *con.* A low chimp wants the food the boss is sitting on and can't take it — but it clocks that the boss can't see *behind the rock,* and it goes the long way round, into the blind spot. Not hiding by reflex. Hiding from a *specific* mind whose blind spot it has modeled." She moved her finger to the third region. "The third gives me chills, and it's the social form of the startle that's been in the line since the jellyfish. A chimp troop in Uganda split in two, and the split traced to one morning — the rising male took a submission grunt from the deposed one *out loud, in front of everyone.* Not a private fight. A witnessed one. And the troop reorganized around it, because every animal there registered not just *the rank changed* but *the others saw it change too* — and three years on, that fault line ran to killing. One signal, in public, mutually clocked, and a society cracked along it. That's minds reading minds reading the room. And the fourth we've had since the mammal — the dig-in, what makes a thing hard to push — except now it's social. Jasmine turned the water glass another quarter. "You're saying de Waal did the Noble Savage fallacy thing."

## Sixty Six Million
