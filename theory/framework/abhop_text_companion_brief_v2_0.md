# Companion Brief — Making the Text (v2.0)
## From trellis_framework to the book somebody wrote alone

*Companion to abhop_artifact_plan_v1_0.md. That plan governs the object, the record, and
the silence. This brief governs the text. Written to the Claude working author-side with
Brian. Supersedes v1.0, which misread the ask: the author identity is not published
apparatus — it is private craft apparatus, and the ambiguity is not aimed at real-world
detectives — it is raw material for the novel.*

---

## The correction that reframes everything

**You and Brian must know exactly who the author is.** Not a vibe — a person, fully
built, the way any novelist builds a character whose voice they have to hold for two
hundred pages. Without that person, every stylistic choice is a guess and the text
drifts back toward Brian's own voice or toward generic treatise-speak, which are the two
failure modes.

**The world never learns who the author is.** The identity lives in a private character
document and in the two heads that use it. Nothing external is ever published, claimed,
or hinted. This is the same discipline as any character backstory: 90% of it exists so
the 10% on the page rings true.

**The author is not trying to mislead anyone.** The strange passages are what naturally
happens when a solitary, unedited mind spends years expressing genuinely difficult ideas
with nobody to say "this part is unclear." The ambiguity is an *artifact of the
situation*, not a design aimed at readers. We engineer it only in the sense that we
choose, in character, which ideas come out weird — because the novel needs those
passages. The kids in Book One will quote them, fight over them, build theories on them.
The text is the B-plot's ore body, and we are placing the veins.

---

## 1. Build the author (the private character document)

Write this first, before touching a sentence of the manuscript. Decision fields:

**Formation.** Where did the rigor come from, if not an institution? The notation, the
statistical care, the invented terminology all have to grow from one biography.

**The room.** Where and how were the writing years spent? Solitude has flavors —
widowed, estranged, night-shift, rural, ill — and each produces different prose.

**The one domain.** Every autodidact has a home territory their metaphors come from.
This choice does more voicing work than any other single decision: it determines what
the weird passages sound like.

**Why this book.** What made a person with no audience spend years on a theory of
personality? The motive never appears in the text, but it pressurizes every page.

**Why it stops.** Death, decline, loss of nerve, loss of interest. The terminal edge
(§4) must be the natural consequence of this answer.

**A recommended candidate** — take it or build another, but decide:

> A bench technician. Thirty-odd years in a university physiology lab — prepping
> animals, maintaining instruments, running other people's experiments. Read
> everything; authored nothing; was thanked in two acknowledgments in three decades.
> Watched credentialed people be wrong slowly and carefully all their working life.
> Retired, alone, and wrote the book the professors never would — with the lab's
> discipline (the notation, the controls, the honest grading of claims) and none of
> the lab's manners (no citations, no hedging, no literature review, terms invented
> fresh because the inherited ones were never theirs to begin with).

This one biography simultaneously explains the rigor, the invented vocabulary, the
missing citations, the flashes of temper at academia, the obsessive worked examples
from animal behavior, and a plausible stop. It also sits nowhere near the novel's cast,
so the novel can never accidentally confirm it.

**Hard rule carried over:** the author is not a character in the novel, is never named
anywhere, and no external record of them is ever created. In the novel, authorship
stays permanently dark; the kids' three theories (crank / scientist / model) all remain
live because the text honestly supports all three.

## 2. Strip the system

Unchanged from v1.0. Remove version headers, authority rules, document pointers,
"movements," all project-management vocabulary, anything addressed to an operational
user. What survives is argument: the book explains, derives, defends. It never
coordinates. A lone author has no disputes to adjudicate.

## 3. The epistemic notes become the author's private discipline

Keep them — they are the best thing in the framework — but convert scattered status
tags into **one person's fixed grading notation**, introduced once, tersely, without
apology, then applied relentlessly. In the recommended biography it is lab discipline
turned inward: the author grades their own claims the way they once labeled specimens.
Idiosyncratic scale, nonstandard terms, an ordering no field uses. It is the text's
fingerprint in place of a name, and it reads as rigor and obsession simultaneously —
which is exactly the undecidability the novel's kids inherit.

## 4. Engineering the weird passages (the novel's ore)

The strangeness must be *in character* — each weird passage is what this specific
person, unedited, would naturally produce. Types, with their function in Book One:

- **Coinage before definition.** A private term used confidently for thirty pages
  before it is ever defined, because the author forgot a stranger wouldn't know it.
  *(The kids adopt it before they understand it; Marge notices the definition never
  comes.)*
- **The over-stretched metaphor.** The home domain pressed past its load limit — a
  paragraph where the analogy audibly creaks. *(Doctrinal fights: Bart takes it
  literally and builds it; the literal reading works, which is worse.)*
- **Sudden second person.** Twice, maybe three times in the book, the author briefly
  addresses "you" — then never again. Who is *you*? *(The kids argue: a student? a
  child? the machine? The answer, privately: nobody — a tired writer's slip. It is
  never resolved.)*
- **Unexplained arithmetic.** A calculation shown in full, its purpose assumed.
  *(The margin-arguer — Lisa's mother — checks it in pencil. It's right. This is the
  moment the annotator becomes real to the kids.)*
- **Misplaced precision.** Fanatical exactness about something trivial beside a grand
  claim waved through. *(Comic on first read; on reread, a window into what the author
  could and couldn't test alone.)*
- **The personal example, unexplained.** One worked example that is obviously from
  life — too specific, too felt — with zero context given. *(The single juiciest
  passage for the theories: the crank reading, the scientist reading, and the model
  reading all explain it differently.)*
- **The flash of temper.** Three at most, one sentence each, at academia or at an
  imagined objector. *(Kills the "model" theory for some kids, confirms it for
  others — a model trained on angry reviewers.)*

Discipline for all of the above: **each instance must be honest** — genuinely the kind
of thing this author would write, never a wink, never a planted falsehood, never a
puzzle that decodes. If a weirdness exists only to serve the novel, cut it; if it
serves the novel *because* it is exactly what a hermit would write, keep it.

## 5. The terminal edge

Choose the real edge the book dies on, in character. The framework's genuine forward
pointer — the planned second volume on *disorder* — remains the presumptive choice: the
book completes its account of how a personality holds together, arrives at how one
comes apart, begins, and stops. The stop must be the natural consequence of the
biography's "why it stops." Do not perform decay the draft doesn't have; if the late
material is genuinely thinner, let it stand. The printer's note (governed by the plan)
accounts for the ending in one flat sentence.

## 6. What the text must never do

Unchanged: no prophecy, no knowledge of anything in the novel, no addressing posterity,
no winking, no explanation of its own incompleteness inside the argument. And no
external apparatus of any kind, ever — the identity stays in the drawer.

## 7. Stylometry

Still real, now easier: a fully built character is the natural defense. The bench
technician's sentences are not Brian's sentences — different rhythm, different
connectives, different temper. Hold the character and the fingerprint diverges on its
own; audit once before print anyway.

## 8. Checklist before the degradation pass

- [ ] Private author document written and ruled final (formation, room, domain, motive,
      stop)
- [ ] All system/project apparatus stripped
- [ ] Grading notation designed, introduced once, applied throughout, consistent with
      the biography
- [ ] Weird-passage inventory placed — each instance in character, each one usable by
      the novel, none decodable
- [ ] The personal example written (and its private meaning known to you two alone)
- [ ] Terminal edge chosen as the biography's natural consequence
- [ ] Nothing predicts, nothing winks, nothing connects to the novel's cast
- [ ] Stylometry audit against Brian's natural prose
- [ ] Cold read by someone who knows nothing, for the only question that matters:
      *is it good?*
