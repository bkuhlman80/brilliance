## The arc

Marge tracks four persistent fallacies in personality science across the full book. Each chapter she presents a short fallacy section in the Trellis block of the retrospective. She does not arrive with a theory. She accumulates one across twelve quarters of working in the same room as Bramble.

The arc has three phases.

**Ch 1–4: naming.** One fallacy per chapter, paired to the cybernetic function Bramble is installing that chapter. Marge does not connect them. She is a psychologist noticing four things in her field that bother her, one at a time, as the lab's work gives her vocabulary to name them.

**Ch 5–10: riffing.** Marge senses a pattern she cannot yet articulate. Each chapter she works one angle. Structural rhymes. What each fallacy gets right. Why they persist. She hunts for a fifth and comes up empty. She starts to suspect each fallacy is held by people who are themselves a certain shape, but she does not yet trust the suspicion.

**Ch 11–12: synthesis.** Marge presents the unified theory. The four fallacies are not random. Each denies one of the four cybernetic functions. Each is held by people whose own shape lacks that function. The book's central anti-optimization claim arrives from her.

Marge is a lesbian.

At Ch 9 Marge floats her type hypothesis from the clinical register she works in: if a clinical pattern attaches to a shape, a fallacy may attach the same way. She does not work a specific case in the book — the hypothesis arrives as a clinician's pattern-recognition, not a documented instance. The fallacy arc closes on its own thesis at Ch 11–12. The clinical register itself is documented in the `Inheritance_Lines_symbolic` doc and is not rendered in this book.

## Per chapter

### Ch 1: Ghost in the Machine

**Bramble.** Effector. The vacuum is acting on its environment for the first time. Nothing regulates it. Nothing models it. The executive arm, raw and unqualified, making contact with the world.

**Marge's angle.** Names the Ghost in the Machine. The worldview that says: your traits are not destiny, they are a project. Therapy, psychedelics, habit stacking, neuroplasticity. The right intervention applied with enough consistency and you become the person this environment requires.

**The grand-theory announcement.** Before naming the first fallacy, Marge announces that she has a grand theory explaining why no one understands psychology, and that she will be teaching it to the team in bits across the book. She names the lab specifically as a site for working the theory out — these four people, in this room, watching this artifact, are the working conditions her theory needs. The team takes the announcement with affection. The grand-theory framing makes her fallacy section feel like a recurring topic from the outset rather than a one-off. She returns to the lab-as-site framing across the arc.

**Her stance.** Observational. She is a working psychologist with a clinical caseload. She sees clients arriving certain they can choose what to become. The willing self stands outside the system that produces it and rewrites the system from outside. The self-help industry has a name for this: reinvention. Marge is bothered without yet having the structural account.

**What she covers.** The self-help version: you are not your history, you can rewire yourself. The therapy-as-rebirth version. The psychedelic-reset version. She names the term (Pinker named it; she lifts it). She notes the absurdity: the willing self is itself a process in the model it claims to override. There is no homunculus standing outside the system. She does not yet have the full argument. She just names the thing.

**Continuity with Ch 0.** This is not a fresh start. Marge's fallacy work here is the next public articulation of a thread she has already been working in her fiction — the four-switches short story (introduced in Ch 0) that Burns recognized in the contest description, about a couple who each installed one switch per MBTI dimension and the myth of free will it dramatizes. The Ghost in the Machine she names at the lab is the same problem her story imagined.

**Connection to chapter.** Bramble has Effector and nothing else. The Ghost in the Machine worldview is what you get when you delete Effector from your model of personality: the embodied executive arm vanishes and a disembodied willing self appears in its place, choosing what to become. Bramble's Effector is the thing this view replaces with a ghost. Marge can make this point without seeing the larger pattern.

**Marge's "photoshop out" verb.** Marge's metaphor for the Ghost-in-the-Machine's deletion move. She uses it three times in Ch 1 — the worldview *photoshops out* the executive arm and pastes a willing self into the empty space. Available as a recurring Marge fingerprint when she discusses fallacies that work by leaving things out of a picture. Strongest fit for the Blank Slate (Ch 2) and the Noble Savage (Ch 3), where the structural move is identical (deleting one function from the model). Carries through naturally to Dealt Hand (Ch 4) and the Ch 11–12 synthesis.

**Marge's "frontal lobe" as Effector-by-analogy.** At Ch 1 Marge has no framework label for Effector. She substitutes *frontal lobe* with an explicit *by analogy* disclaimer — Bramble at the Protocell is, by analogy, the frontal lobe (the bit that does), and the Ghost worldview is what you get when you photoshop the frontal lobe out and put a disembodied willing self in its place. The substitution is locally clean but creates a forward problem: at Ch 2–4, when Marge names the Blank Slate / Noble Savage / Dealt Hand fallacies as deletions of Regulator / Modeler / Reviser, *frontal lobe* is no longer available as the substitute term (it has been mapped to Effector at Ch 1). Marge needs different folk-psych vocabulary for each.

Candidates for drafting:
- For the Regulator deletion (Blank Slate, Ch 2): *the program inside* or *the stored rule*.
- For the Modeler deletion (Noble Savage, Ch 3): *the inner picture* or *what you've built*.
- For the Reviser deletion (Dealt Hand, Ch 4): *the rewrite* or *the part that changes itself*.

Decide during Ch 2–4 drafting.

### Ch 2: Blank Slate

**Bramble.** Regulator. The vacuum now holds itself to a stored reference. The regulation is invisible from the outside.

**Marge's angle.** Names the Blank Slate. The worldview that says: personality is barely a thing. Context dominates behavior. Same person behaves differently with different people. So traits are a folk theory.

**Her stance.** Still observational, slightly more annoyed. She names Mischel's situationist critique. She gives an example from her work: the introverted girl who is loud with her friends. She points out that this is not "sometimes introverted, sometimes not." The girl is running a stable program that routes differently in different social contexts.

**What she covers.** Mischel. The Standard Social Science Model in the background. The popular version: "context matters, so personality is overrated." She names what the view gets wrong without yet having the structural account: it confuses behavioral flexibility with the absence of a stable program.

**Connection to chapter.** Bramble has Effector and now Regulator. The Blank Slate worldview is what you get when you delete Regulator from your model of personality: stored regulators vanish, and all you see is surface behavior varying by context. Bramble's Regulator is the thing this view denies exists.

### Ch 3: Noble Savage

**Bramble.** Modeler. The vacuum is now modeling its environment and itself.

**Marge's angle.** Names the Noble Savage. The worldview that says: your nature is good, the environment is the cage, fix the fit and the organism flourishes. Quit the draining job. Leave the relationship. Move to the city that matches your energy. The organism's nature is innocent; only circumstance distorts it.

**Her stance.** Now she is leaning forward. She has a clinical caseload of people who have done the fit-swapping. Some of them were right — a few cases really were environmental. Most of them carry the same internal landscape into the new context and are surprised to find it there. The landscape traveled. The new environment did not dissolve it.

**What she covers.** The fallacy as it appears in self-help, in popular career advice, in environment-swapping rhetoric. The Pinker source ("Noble Savage"). Rousseau as the genealogy if it lands without bogging down. She notes what the view gets wrong: the organism's modeling layer — the inner world it has built from its history, its attachments, its accumulated representations — is not separable from the organism. You cannot change the environment and leave the model behind. The model made the fit; the model will make the next fit too.

**Connection to chapter.** Bramble has Effector, Regulator, and now Modeler. The Noble Savage worldview is what you get when you delete Modeler from your model of personality: the modeling layer vanishes and the person is reduced to bare nature pressing against bare circumstance. If the inner model does not exist, the cage is the only explanation available. Bramble's Modeler is the thing this view denies exists.

---

### Ch 4: Dealt Hand

**Bramble.** Reviser. The vacuum is now revising its own regulators. The architecture is complete. The chassis is about to swap.

**Marge's angle.** Names the Dealt Hand. The worldview that says: some people got lucky, your traits are your verdict, the graph is not on your side. Heritability data taken as fate rather than as description.

**Her stance.** Now she is making a real claim. The Dealt Hand is the fallacy Pinker did not name. He named the other three. He missed this one. Marge thinks it is the most pernicious of the four because it carries the authority of empirical findings. The other three can be argued against on conceptual grounds. The Dealt Hand has numbers.

**What she covers.** The empirical case. Heritability findings are real. Conscientiousness does predict income and longevity. The fallacy is not in the data. The fallacy is in reading the correlation as a verdict instead of as a description of an organism in this environment. She does not yet have the structural argument. She just notes that the verdict reading is doing the wrong kind of work.

**Connection to chapter.** Bramble has all four functions now. The Dealt Hand worldview is what you get when you delete Reviser from your model of personality: revision vanishes and the trait number is treated as fate. Bramble's Reviser just came online. Marge can point out: Bramble's heritability is one hundred percent identical with the previous chassis. The hardware is the same. But Bramble revised itself this quarter. So either heritability does not mean what the Dealt Hand thinks it means, or revision is real anyway.

**End of phase one.** Marge has now named four fallacies across four quarters. She has not connected them. She sits down. The reader sees four parallel observations that all point at one architecture, and Marge does not yet see it. The architecture is complete. Her theory is not.

**Phase boundary beat (Ch 4-5).** As Marge finishes the Dealt Hand and begins to sit, Lisa or Jasmine notices, quietly, that Marge has more to say than usual. Marks the shift from phase one (naming) to phase two (processing). The beat foreshadows the Ch 5 first-rhyme without spoiling it.

## Marge's Venn

Marge developed an idiosyncratic Venn in grad school: a Jungian, MBTI-based mapping she built to organize personality the way she had been trained to see it. She brings it to the pitch. The team tries it briefly as a working framework on the office patio afterward and rejects it as too restrictive — prokaryotes do not have a thinking/feeling preference. The Venn does not return as a structural element in any chapter.

It survives in two other ways.

**As an authorial scaffold.** The Venn organizes the four characters, the four fallacies, and the four Pavlovian heuristics for the writer. It is a backstage instrument — a way to keep the mappings straight while drafting. It does not appear on the page after the pitch interlude.

**As a formal ancestor.** The team's later four-circle Venn for ABHOP keeps the geometric structure of Marge's Venn and replaces its content. Marge's instinct to think in a Venn was structurally right even when her Jungian content was wrong; the shape she reached for in grad school is the shape the framework needs, and only the labels change.

The mapping below is authorial reference, not in-book material. It records how Marge's grad-school Venn lines up with the framework — each cybernetic function taking one MBTI label directly and the four pair-syntheses taking the other four — so the writer can compose the characters, fallacies, and heuristics coherently. The convergence with standard MBTI is asymmetric, but the structural relationship is intact.

### The singles

The four cybernetic functions take MBTI labels, each grounded in one of the Pavlovian credit-assignment heuristics from the Ch 5 round:

- Effector = Intuition, built on eligibility trace (stimulus recency; spontaneity).
- Regulator = Introversion, built on blocking (familiarity; neophobia).
- Modeler = Thinking, built on overshadowing (stimulus potency).
- Reviser = Extroversion, built on latent inhibition (novelty; neophilia).

### The doubles

The four adjacent doubles take the remaining MBTI labels:

- Controller (Effector × Regulator) = Feeling
- Anticipator (Regulator × Modeler) = Judgment
- Adapter (Modeler × Reviser) = Sensation
- Explorer (Reviser × Effector) = Perception

### The dichotomies

The four MBTI dichotomies run between Venn regions:

- Energy-direction (E/I) runs between two singles: Reviser (E) and Regulator (I).
- Information (N/S) runs between a single and a double: Effector (N) and Adapter (S).
- Decisions (T/F) runs between a single and a double: Modeler (T) and Controller (F).
- Lifestyle (J/P) runs between two doubles: Explorer (P) and Anticipator (J).

Two dichotomies cross between regions of the same type; two cross between a single and a double. That is the asymmetry. It is the cost of giving each cybernetic function a direct MBTI label rather than mapping MBTI onto axes-and-pairs symmetrically. The fifteen-region Venn still places a person at a continuous position rather than sorting into one of sixteen types, and the borderline-score sorting is what produces MBTI's weak psychometric reputation.

### The labmates

Each labmate occupies one capacity. Three of their four MBTI letters are forced by the regions inside their triple. The fourth wobbles between two regions inside the triple. The wobble is a context-dependent shift, not a continuum point.

- Marge is Conservator-shaped. Forced: I, N, J. Wobble: T/F. Her Architect side (INTJ) is where she shines. Her Advocate side (INFJ) is more comfortable.
- Lisa is Planner-shaped. Forced: S, T, J. Wobble: E/I. Her Logistician side (ISTJ) is the class nerd. Her Executive side (ESTJ) is more agentic.
- Bart is Reactor-shaped. Forced: N, F, P. Wobble: E/I. His Campaigner side (ENFP) is the class clown. His Mediator side (INFP) is warmer.
- Burns is Pursuer-shaped. Forced: E, T, P. Wobble: S/N. His Entrepreneur side (ESTP) is the one he shows Cheryl. His Debater side (ENTP) shows up around the team.

Bart and Lisa wobble within a single MBTI category — Diplomat and Sentinel. Burns and Marge wobble across categories. The triples that include both Reviser (E) and Regulator (I) as singles produce E/I wobbles within one category. The triples that include only one of those produce wobbles in a different dichotomy and end up crossing categories.
