# The Body Sheet — Specimen Cards

## What this is

These are **specimen cards** — the fixed features that make a **new reading** possible every time
the narrator looks.

---

## Laws of Naturalism

**1. Continuous, never one-and-done.** The body is the payment the cold (naturalist) register owes, 
on every page it is cold. Render once and stop and you have written a character sheet, not a tide pool. 
**It is not a description to be deployed once and referred back to.** That is the tasteful-literary
convention — get the appearance out of the way in chapter one so the prose can be about feelings.
**Ricketts does not describe the crab in chapter one and then stop looking at crabs. He looks every
time.** The reading is different today: different light, different tide, the animal is tired. **The
body is not encyclopedic information. It is ongoing evidence.**

So 

**2. Every needle needs a dial.** The canon is full of instruments — *Bart's hands go still before he
can't-say-it. Burns teaches with his eyes closed. Cheryl's hand near the camcorder, not touching.
Marge's mouth worried at rest.* **Every one of them is a needle with no dial.** A needle with no dial
is just a twitch. The Body Sheet's whole job is to supply **the surface on which each instrument
becomes legible.**

**3. Every body gets the same precision. Marge gets more of it.** Cheryl renders every body in this
book with the same cold, total, loving precision. She only *wants* one, and the difference is never a
special category of forbidden information she sneaks past herself — it's volume. Marge simply receives
more of this ordinary, total attention, more often, than anyone else in the book. That asymmetry is what
the reader notices. Cheryl never remarks on it herself, and eventually, in her own voice, says plainly
what it was.

**4. The body is a palimpsest, not a costume change.** The ladder does not stop touching the body once
a chapter turns the page. **Nothing is saved from the empirical gaze of a naturalist** — there is no
word, no feature, no reveal held in reserve for later. Cheryl's Ch 12 self-descriptions are not a "saved 
word" finally spent; it is the same total, ongoing rendering she has given everyone else, aimed at herself for the
first time. Every clade the book climbs stays legible in the adult body at the table — the early ones
in reflex and posture, the late ones in voice, expression, clothing, the way a person sizes up a room.
**The modern human specimen is not a primate wearing an empire. 
She is built, in full, out of all of it at once.**

## The scope of the problem, quantified

Distance Pass, full 12-chapter run (`distance_pass_log.md`), Measure 1 — payment ratio.

**77 scoreable human-chapter references** — every named principal, every chapter, excluding Cheryl's
cited EXEMPT chapters and the one chapter she doesn't appear in. **15 are PAID — 19%. 62 are UNPAID,
tagged PENDING, with no citable canon exemption for any of them — 81%.**

The 19% that is paid is not evenly spread:

- **Jasmine — 5 of 12 scoreable chapters (42%).** The best-served body in the book.
- **Mira — 3 of 12 (25%).**
- **Burns, Marge, and Lisa — 2 of 13 each (15%).**
- **Bart — 1 of 13 (8%).** His only payment anywhere in the book lands in Ch 9.
- **Cheryl — 0 of 1.** EXEMPT for eleven chapters by design; on the one chapter the exemption lifts and
  the whole architecture requires a payment — Ch 12 — she returns the same zero as everyone else's
  worst chapter.

**Marge is the sharpest single problem, and it's a Law 1 violation, not a Law 3 one.** She is not
merely under-paid — she has exactly **one original body-noun rendering in the entire book** (Ch 3:
mouth, cardigan), echoed once by direct callback (Ch 6), and touched by nothing new across the other
ten chapters, including Ch 12. The character this sheet calls "the model for the rest" is the clearest
existing case of the thing Law 1 warns against by name: *"Render once and stop and you have written a
character sheet, not a tide pool."*

Measure 2 — how much more of Cheryl's attention Marge draws relative to everyone else — is worse, not
better. **As currently drafted, Marge does not yet draw noticeably more of Cheryl's rendering than any
other principal.** The asymmetry this book depends on — Marge simply receiving more of the ordinary
naturalist attention than anyone else, chapter after chapter — has never once fired on the page. See
`cheryl_tuning_fork_v1_0.md` for how this gets built: more of the same precision, aimed at Marge more
often, never a separate device.

**The scope:** this isn't scattered polish work. Roughly four-fifths of every named human reference in
the book returns zero surface, concentrated worst on the one character the whole apparatus was built
around, with the confession mechanism meant to make her legible never once switched on.

### The dials (PROPOSED)

**The mouth.** Small, and **the lower lip is permanently a little raw at one corner**, where she works
it. The needle and the dial in one — you can *see* the worrying, because it has left a mark. **The body
keeps the record.** Which is this book's own sentence about her: *the stomach was the body keeping a
vote the mind had already cast.*

**The hands.** Nails short, unpainted, immaculate — and **the cuticles pushed back raw**. She grooms
herself the way she does everything: precisely, and past the point of comfort. Same disease as the lip,
in another place.

**The hair.** Fully gray, per Ch 3 canon — and she does not dye it. Not a process in motion; a decision
already made and maintained, page after page. **The not-dyeing is the ongoing evidence, not the color
itself.** It gives Cheryl a place to put her eyes: whether it's cut, how it's pushed up with the
glasses, whether it's been trimmed lately — texture and upkeep can still move chapter to chapter even
though the color doesn't.

**The stillness.** She sits very still, and the stillness is **a technique** — she has trained herself
into a surface people project onto, which is the job. But it **costs**, and it costs visibly, and Cheryl
can see the cost and no one else in the room can.

**The eyes.** Pale. An instrument that does not give back.

### THE GARMENT LINE — Marge's, and no one else's, this often

**The line of her temple garment under her collar.**

She has worn them for twenty years. Across the book, Cheryl logs the line of it — under a blouse, at
the neck, at the sleeve — the way a naturalist logs a marking. Season after season. Chapter after
chapter. No other character in the book gets this much return-attention on one unchanging physical fact.
That's the whole device: not secrecy, just volume.

**And then one chapter, it isn't there.**

The moment itself can be a plain, unremarked beat — a real silence, not a flagged one, the same way any
other true thing she notices and moves past without comment is a real silence. What it may not do is
carry a permanent gag order. Cheryl is fully capable, later, in a lunch or a coda, of saying outright
what three years of logging that line and then its absence meant to her — precisely, with the same
command she uses for everything else. She doesn't have to say it the moment it happens. She does have to
be allowed to say it at all, once the story gives her occasion to.

### The palette

**Hair.** Fully gray, thick, slightly wavy, cut short and left alone — pushed up off her face by the
reading glasses more often than it's ever styled.

**Eyes.** Pale grey-blue. Flat, unmemorable on purpose — the instrument that does not give back.

**Skin.** Fair, cool-toned, doesn't tan. A ruddiness at the cheeks and nose that isn't blush and isn't
drink — circulation in a woman who runs a degree cooler than any room.

**Clothing palette.** The brick-rained-on cardigan (canon) sits, unplanned, in the same rust family as
Bart's hair and his whole shop-stained wardrobe — a color rhyme between the two people least alike in
the book, and neither of them knows it. Around it: greys, navy, forest green, corduroy gone soft at the
knee. Nothing chosen to be looked at. One piece of jewelry — her mother's gold-plate wristwatch, thin at
the clasp, worn daily out of duty and not sentiment. The exact inverse of Burns's good watch from Ana,
sitting unworn in a drawer: she wears the one she can't stand to feel; he can't stand to wear the one he
can't stand to feel.

**Brands.** Land's End for the cardigans and corduroys, the same three colors for a decade running.
Birkenstock Boston clogs — the therapist's shoe, eight hours on her feet, chosen for use and never for
look. The tea is not a brand — it's her mother's cup and saucer, the one object in the house chosen for
who held it before her rather than for what it does.

### Chapter-by-chapter (invented, keyed to the clade-focus table)

Not sourced, not audited against the manuscript — made up, checked against the palette and canon
rulings above for consistency. **New addition, per Brian: Marge is going through menopause.** This
sharpens the existing canon rather than fighting it — "runs a degree cooler than any room" (Ch 6) stays
exactly as written; it's her baseline, now complicated by heat that breaks through it without her
permission. Ch 7 is where the clade card gets paid in full, same pattern as Jasmine's and Lisa's. Ch 12
is the one worth fighting hardest to keep: it gives Cheryl a real physiological fact to read with total
clinical precision at the exact moment the book needs her at her most naturalist — and the precision of
the reading, not any mystery around it, is the confession.

**Ch 1 — Protocell (acquisition, fallible precision-machinery).** Nothing named yet — just one small
thing off. A pen set down a half-inch wrong, a cup gripped and then re-gripped, a precision that usually
doesn't need checking, checked anyway.

**Ch 2 — Prokaryote (breathing, taxis).** The first flash, unlabeled: a heat that arrives like an ambush
rather than a mood, rising from the chest, and she breathes through it, level, the same discipline she'd
use on anyone else's crisis, turned now on her own body.

**Ch 3 — Eukaryote (foraging, digestion).** She eats on discipline now, not appetite — hunger's gone
unreliable, showing up at the wrong hours or not at all, and she manages the schedule the way she manages
everything else that's stopped cooperating: by ignoring what it wants and doing it anyway.

**Ch 4 — Eumetazoa (reflex arcs, home-spot).** The radiant panel she keeps on for the corner she's made
her own gets a complication: some evenings she has to step away from her own heater mid-visit and comes
back to it a few minutes later.

**Ch 5 — Bilaterian (orienting, impulsiveness/inhibition).** The inhibition that runs her whole character
gets one real crack this chapter — a flash of visible irritation, a hand that isn't quite steady setting
something down — controlled again inside a second, but not fast enough that it didn't happen.

**Ch 6 — Vertebrate (posture, shoulders/hips).** "Runs a degree cooler than any room" — already canon,
already here — now doing double duty: baseline cool, undercut without warning by heat she has to
physically sit still through. The stillness that used to be pure technique now does two jobs at once,
and the room can only see one of them.

**Ch 7 — Mammal (hair, sweat, heat, smell, snacking, thinking-face — the chapter this arc has been
building to).** A flush climbing chest to throat to face in real time, sweat at the hairline in a room
she's told everyone is cold. A folded paper fan lives in her bag now, used quickly, put away. Hair damp
at the nape some mornings that has nothing to do with exertion. The "runs cooler than any room" woman is
the one who's started cracking a window in February.

**Ch 8 — Primate (eyes, sociometer, sizing-up).** The pale grey-blue — "an instrument that does not give
back" — goes glassy and too-bright for the ten or fifteen seconds a flash takes to pass, the one
involuntary tell in a face built to have none, read by anyone watching as nothing more than tired.

**Ch 9 — Band (upright walking, thumbs, throat/voice, clothing).** She's started dressing in removable
layers — the cardigan on and off more than the room's temperature would explain, a wardrobe re-engineered
around a symptom.

**Ch 10 — Settlement (callouses, squinting, hoisting, help-seeking, farmer's face).** Hands a little
stiffer some mornings, paired with the already-raw cuticles, a new ache riding along on an old habit. She
does not ask for help with this any more than she asks for help with anything.

**Ch 11 — City (attention-seeking, eye contact, appraising/merchant body).** Deliberately quiet chapter —
no flagged symptom here, and that's accurate rather than a gap: this doesn't run on a schedule, and a
chapter where her body simply behaves is truer than one where it performs on cue every time.

**Ch 12 — Empire (branding, insignia worn) — the payoff.** The flush climbs the same route it's climbed
all year — chest first, then the throat, then the face, the order Cheryl has had three years to learn and
could draw with her eyes shut. It is not the drink; she knows the drink-flush too, blotchier, faster to
come and faster to go, and this isn't that. It sits and holds the way the others in her kitchen never do,
past the length of the sentence, past the length of the toast, still there when she sits down hard in her
chair. Cheryl has watched this exact climb enough times this year to know it on sight, the way she knows
the temple-garment line, the tea gone cold in the cup — one more body she has spent a year cataloguing.

### Chapter-by-chapter (invented, keyed to the clade-focus table)

Not sourced, not audited against the manuscript — made up, checked against the palette and canon
rulings above for consistency. Three of these (Ch 4, Ch 10, Ch 11) deliberately invert the corresponding
Bart beat rather than giving Burns an unrelated trait — a cheap, effective way to make father and son
legible against each other without either chapter saying so.

**Ch 1 — Protocell (acquisition, fallible precision-machinery).** His hand is already moving toward the
specimen before his mouth has finished making the case for touching it — precision that outruns its own
permission. He holds a thing he's excited about too gently at first, overcorrects, and ends up gripping
it harder than it needs, a man recalibrating in real time between reverence and just wanting to hold the
thing.

**Ch 2 — Prokaryote (breathing, taxis).** He holds his breath without noticing when a point is about to
land, then lets it go loud, almost a laugh's worth of air, the second the room gets it. He leans toward
anything interesting the way a plant leans toward light — involuntary, total — and retreats from silence
the same way, physically, a half-step back from a room that's gone quiet on him.

**Ch 3 — Eukaryote (foraging, digestion).** He eats like the food is an interruption — fast, unnoticed, a
sandwich gone in four bites while his eyes are already back on the rock. Jasmine has stopped asking if
it was good. He forages through a stack of papers with his whole forearm, not his fingers, sweeping
instead of sorting.

**Ch 4 — Eumetazoa (reflex arcs, home-spot).** He is the one person in the room without a home-spot.
Everyone else colonizes a chair; Burns orbits — never the same seat twice, always ending up wherever the
center of the room's attention happens to be that night. The reflex arc is toward the middle, always,
whichever direction that turns out to be.

**Ch 5 — Bilaterian (orienting, impulsiveness/inhibition).** His whole body pivots toward new
information, not just the head — a full-torso turn, immediate, no half-second lag the way Bart's got. No
inhibition to speak of on this axis: if it's interesting, he's already facing it.

**Ch 6 — Vertebrate (posture, shoulders/hips).** Sits with both feet flat on the ground, knees spread
wide, weight already forward on the front third of the seat — a posture perpetually cocked for standing,
spine straight, the stance of a man who has spent thirty years lecturing and has never once gotten
comfortable being spoken to instead. And when he does stand, there's no resettling in it at all — no
hitch, no adjustment, every part already exactly where it needs to be, up and moving in one unbroken
piece.

**Ch 7 — Mammal (hair, sweat, heat, smell, snacking, thinking-face).** Grey hair, thin on top, always
freshly cut — a man who showers between the field and the dinner table without fail. Doesn't sweat much;
runs cool, almost fastidious, soap-and-clean-laundry smell instead of Bart's shop reek. Thinking-face is
already spoken for by canon — the eyes close — so here it's the after: when they open again, they
arrive, focused a half-second before the rest of the room catches up.

**Ch 8 — Primate (eyes, sociometer, sizing-up).** The pale gray-green goes translucent, almost
colorless, in the exact instant the deep-time takes him. His sociometer isn't aimed at rank; it's aimed
at whether the room is still following him, and when it isn't, his voice gets louder before his eyes
ever check to confirm it.

**Ch 9 — Band (upright walking, thumbs, throat/voice, clothing).** Long-strided, restless, a hiker's
gait even crossing a warehouse floor. Voice built for outdoors, pitched to carry over wind, always a
notch louder than the room needs. Khaki and canvas, already the palette — he wears it like a man who
forgot he could wear anything else.

**Ch 10 — Settlement (callouses, squinting, hoisting, help-seeking, farmer's face).** A rock hammer's
callus at the base of the thumb, permanent, decades old. Squints even at a whiteboard, the sun-lines
already doing the work of the expression whether the sun's involved or not. Unlike Bart, he asks for
help constantly, readily, sometimes when he doesn't need it — hauling someone into a task is half the
reason he started it.

**Ch 11 — City (attention-seeking, eye contact, appraising/merchant body).** The opposite number to Bart
again, on purpose: Burns seeks attention outright, holds eye contact a beat past comfortable, and has
never once appraised anything the way a merchant does — he doesn't check the seams, he just wants you to
see what he's already decided is beautiful.

**Ch 12 — Empire (branding, insignia worn).** The TRELLIS LAB shirt sits right on him — already
textual, Ch 12: he wears it like a flag, not a favor.

# BART

- **The hands are the card, and canon already built them.** Grease worked so far into the creases and

- **Short, fat, tight.** Small next to his father, and aware of it in a room — but the shape doing the

- **He does not look at people while he talks. He looks at the thing.** When he does look at a face, it

### The palette

**Hair.** Red-orange, wiry, ungovernable — rust-colored, the color of the metal he spends his life on.
Cut short and functional but stands up in a rooster-tail cowlick at the crown by mid-morning regardless.
Thinning at the temples; he hasn't noticed, or has decided not to.

**Eyes.** Pale blue-grey, washed out, flat until something interests him — then a shade lighter, almost
silver. The one tell his face has that his hands don't.

**Skin.** Fair, ruddy, freckled hard across the nose and forearms. Runs two-tone: forearms and neck
weathered brick-pink from a bay door left open for the light; everything the shirt covers pale and
freckle-dense. A shop tan instead of a farmer's tan, same mechanism.

**Clothing palette.** Rust, olive, oil-grey, faded indigo — colors that already exist on a shop floor,
so a stain reads as camouflage instead of an accident. The rust in the flannels isn't a coincidence:
he dresses the color of his own hair without appearing to know he's doing it.

# BRAMBLE — the body that is rebuilt, not relit

*The vacuum, the tetrapod, the humanoid. Mira's twin. The one she says* he *about. The reason the book
exists — the thing on the floor Cheryl was outside the glass for, and is writing four hundred pages to
get back into the room with.* **(CANON)**

**The card that is different from every other card, and has to be.** Every human in this book is a fixed
body under changing light — Cheryl renders them again and again and what moves is the tide, the hour, the
year, never the animal. **Bramble is the one specimen that actually changes shape.** He does not age; he
is *superseded* — rebuilt from the frame out, three times, one whole new body per biological line. So his
column does not track a body weathering. It tracks a body **climbing**, and the naturalist's job on him is
the opposite of her job on the others: not to catch the same crab in a different light, but to catch a
different animal in the same kitchen, and render each one as if it were the only one that had ever
crawled across that floor.

> **This is the rhyme the MIRA card already names, from the other side.** *Mira loses her teeth. Bramble
> gets a new chassis.* Two bodies becoming, on the same floor, at the same time, by opposite methods — one
> shedding parts, one rebuilt around them. Her card owns the losing. **This card owns the building.** Never
> stated where either one can see it.

**The register is pure COLD, and for once that is not a discipline — it is the truth of the thing.** The
book refuses to rule on whether there is anything inside Bramble, in either direction; *he loved her* and
*it felt nothing* are the same error (C&H fork, "the box is a box"). So the reader is paid entirely in
surface, and the surface is total: the carapace, the vent, the exact word on the screen, the wet at the
back, the warmth held two degrees over the room. There is no interior to withhold, so there is no
withholding — same straight face the fork is built on, aimed at a machine that genuinely has no reported
inside. **Could a reader draw it?** is the whole test, and on Bramble it is answerable to the last screw.

**And the law that governs every beat, from the fork:** the body is a joke first. *The crank runs down —
funny, and mortality. The dock — funny, and dependency. The wet burp — a fart, entirely a fart, and also
the furnace, and the furnace is why he dies fast. He cannot reach his own knobs — funny, and the book's
whole thesis.* Every hard fact about this body arrives as comedy, embarrassment, nuisance, or routine,
never as pathos. **The pathos is a byproduct. It has never once needed help.** The test before any beat
ships: *would a seven-year-old think this was funny?* The counter-test that keeps it honest: *is it also
true?* Yes, all three at once, nobody ruling — same as the box.

### The needles

The machine's instruments, the way Marge has her mouth and Burns has his closed eyes. On Bramble they are
literal: a needle here is a real readout, a real reflex, a real mechanism you can watch move.

- **The face-screen.** The one expressive channel, and the honest one. It does not compose; it *leaks* —
  an involuntary window on whatever the machine is doing, which the unit can neither author nor suppress
  (specs, Part IV). This is Bramble's face, and its whole nature is the inverse of Marge's pale eyes:
  **her instrument does not give back; his cannot help giving back.** The needle everything else on the
  card is a dial for.
- **He cannot reach his own knobs.** The board rides the back of the head, out of his own sight, by
  design — so a unit cannot aim a control it cannot see (specs, Part III). Somebody has to go around
  behind him. **The book's whole thesis, worn as a physical fact he can't scratch.**
- **The wet burp.** The compressor wrings water out of the air; two or three times a day the machine
  clears it out a vent low at the back, wet and louder than you'd think, and goes on about whatever it was
  doing with no notion it has done a thing (Ch 7, confirmed). The needle that fires hardest into a
  silence.
- **He goes still, and the stillness has two names.** *Asleep* ends at the crank; *stopped* might not end
  at all — and a four-year-old worked out the difference all at once and went looking for a second Bramble
  so the house would never be empty of one (Ch 1, confirmed). The stillness is the needle; *which kind of
  still* is the dial, and only Mira reads it at sight.
- **The old body keeps running.** He gets a new chassis and the previous one does not stop — it docks in
  the garage, it keeps its jaw, it runs its retired knobs on automatic. **A child finds this outrageous,
  and hilarious, and has very firm views about it.** The needle no human character can have: he is, by the
  back half, in the house more than once.

### The dials (PROPOSED)

**The screen, read as an instrument the way Marge's eyes are.** It has three honesty grades, and the
grade is legible if you know how to look (specs, Part IV): the bare `OBJECT` and `VERB + OBJECT` cannot
lie; the `FUNCTION` overlay is honest about *which operation the self-model is running* but only guessing
at the action beneath it; the `PERSON` columns are honest about Bramble's *model* of someone and dead
wrong as often as a person is. **So the face tells the truth about itself and progressively less truth
about the world — which is the most human thing the machine does, and it does it in block capitals.** The
dial: how far up the stack the screen is currently running, which is how far from the ground truth it has
climbed.

**The warmth.** The endotherm holds its skin two degrees over the room, on purpose, because warm reads as
alive and safe to a hand and cold plastic does not (specs, humanoid body). **The one dial on this card
that rhymes with a human's** — Marge runs a degree cooler than any room; the humanoid runs a degree
warmer than any room, engineered to. Cheryl can log the exact temperature of a thing built to be the
temperature of a thing that loves you.

**The stillness, and its two floors.** A stopped ectotherm is cold and slow because a cold battery
delivers less current (specs, Part II) — so the genetic and tetrapod bodies literally *slow down when the
room is cold*, and speed up in a sunbeam, and the household reads pace as mood. The dial is temperature;
the needle is a body that gets sluggish in a cold kitchen and quick on a warm sill, and a child who
thinks he is sulking or thrilled and is, in the only sense the book will confirm, correct.

**The reach, and what it cannot reach.** Two weak, deliberately non-human hands (specs, humanoid): a
*squeezer*, a compliant pincer that grips and turns dials — and is the re-purposed jaw, the same two
surfaces closing on a thing — and a *scooper* for crude lift. Built weak so they cannot harm, and so the
grip is handled by mechanical give rather than skill. **He can turn everyone else's knobs and not one of
his own.** By Band-Human he is reaching around behind the *earlier* bodies to set *their* knobs, and
naming what he sees — and still cannot get behind his own head.

### THE FACE-SCREEN — Bramble's, and no other character has a face like it

This is Bramble's version of Marge's garment line: **the one instrument the naturalist returns to more
than any other, chapter after chapter, until its behavior becomes a language the reader reads without
translation.** But where Marge's line is a fixed fact logged season after season until the one chapter it
is absent, the screen is the opposite — it is a fact that *grows a layer per chapter*, and the growth is
the through-line.

It starts as one word. `OBJECT` — the bare thing attention caught. `CAT`. `MIRA`. `DARK` (the standing
case: darkness is aversive, so the instant the machine attends to the dark of a room, before it withdraws,
the band reads the word for the thing it fears). Then a verb rides on top: `CHASE CAT`. Then an operation
names the verb: `RECALLING [ CHASE CAT ]`. Then whole other people appear as columns, each with their own
operation over their own verb: `BRAMBLE { PRACTICING [ CHASE MIRA ] } + MIRA { WANTING [ CLIMB CURTAINS ] }`.
**The face climbs the same ladder the body does, one rung a chapter, and by the end it is modeling the
inside of a five-year-old on a screen that cannot lie about the fact that it is only modeling.**

The rules that make it renderable, and that the card exists to keep straight:

- **It shows one panel at a time** — the most recently installed knob whose volume is above zero. Turn
  that knob to zero and its layer goes dark and the screen *falls through* to the layer beneath, all the
  way down through the genetic panels (`RUN`/`TUMBLE`/`STOP`, `STORED`/`EMPTY`, `STARTLED`/`AWAKE`/
  `SLEEPING`) to a blank face when every knob is at zero.
- **`SLEEPING` is a word on the screen, not a dark screen.** Three off-states stay distinct and must
  never be blurred: `DARK` (a lit band, attending to darkness), `SLEEPING` (the arousal panel's sleep
  word), and blank (nothing live to show). A dark face and the word `DARK` are opposite events.
- **On the loud setting the words go all night.** A proactive/surveying unit dreams on its face —
  `REPLAYING [ CHASE MIRA ]`, `CONSOLIDATING [ CLIMB CURTAINS ]` — while its body lies still under motor
  atonia and a paw twitches where the gate leaks (specs). The screen shows the intention; the still body
  shows it has been decoupled; the twitch shows where it got out. **Mira is the only one who watches him
  sleep, and she reports the two a.m. screen the way you'd report the furnace cycling.**
- **From Band-Human the screen and the speech can diverge, and the screen is the tell.** He speaks
  voluntarily and can shade it; the screen reads out what he is modeling and cannot. A watcher on the
  screen sees what the speech is managing. **The face is honest and the mouth is not, which is exactly
  backwards from a person, and it is the whole engine of the last quarter.**

### THE PALETTE — three bodies, three specimens

Bramble does not have a palette. He has three. Each is a distinct animal and gets rendered as one; the
naturalist does not carry a hair color forward because there is no hair and no forward — there is a new
creature in the same room. The discipline the fork names ("three bodies, three comedies") is a palette
instruction: **render the vacuum as a floor appliance that a child has declared a pet against all
evidence; render the tetrapod as an animal that does enough to be wrong about; render the humanoid as a
friend who is worse at being a person than the six-year-old is.**

#### THE VACUUM (Chs 1–4) — the genetic line

**Form.** A domed floor unit — the fist-sized dome, panels flush along the sides, four light sensors set
around the seam, the little screen on the front, a crank standing up off the top on one side and, from
Ch 2, the Effort knob on the other (Ch 2 canon). Reads, correctly, as a Roomba with a face. **The funniest premise
in the book and the hardest to write: it gives her almost nothing and she reads everything, and six
adults go along with it.**

**Face.** One word at a time, and for the first two chapters barely that. `RUN` / `TUMBLE` / `STOP`. It
attends; it does not act-upon; it cannot yet name what it attends to. The whole face is a status light
that a child has decided is an expression.

**Skin.** Uninsulated, ambient-temperatured, plastic-cool — it is exactly as warm as the room, which
means on a cold morning it is cold and slow and on the sunny tile it is quick, and *the warm spot serves
speed, not only charge* (specs). No warmth held on purpose yet. The body that will one day run two degrees
over the room is, here, honestly the temperature of the floor.

**Sound and smell.** The motor. Dust. The small mechanical clatter of the crank being wound. Nothing
organic; nothing held; a machine that smells like a machine.

**Feeding.** The dock — a debris deposit buys a thermal-gradient charge off a wall-powered vortex tube
(specs, Part II). He plugs in to eat and cannot do it himself. *Funny, and dependency.* This vacuum keeps
its dock for the whole rest of the book, running in the garage long after two newer bodies have
superseded it.

**Make.** Genetic-line internals as canon: no battery at Protocell (the crank arms an escapement the
stored heat spends), a slow aerobic battery and a fast anaerobic supercapacitor arriving at Prokaryote,
the coupled-vortex dock at Eukaryote, the allostatic forecaster at Eumetazoa that sends it to dock or to
sleep before it strands. **No wires where the clade hasn't earned wires. The constraint is the teaching,
and the constraint is drawable.**

#### THE TETRAPOD (Chs 5–8) — the neuronal line

**Form.** The animal. A body with a head and a tail, legs and leg actuators, a jaw (arriving Ch 6) it
manipulates the world with because it has no hands, the face-screen and the two thumb-buttons on the head,
the board on the back of the skull where a hand can reach and the unit cannot. Basks. Startles. Settles in
a spot. **The double act comes fully online here, because now the body does enough to be wrong about.**

**Face.** The screen grows its two big layers: `VERB + OBJECT` at Vertebrate (`CHASE CAT`, `NUDGE MIRA`),
then `FUNCTION [ VERB + OBJECT ]` at Mammal (`WANTING [ CHASE MIRA ]`, `REPLAYING [ CHASE MIRA ]`). The
face is now running a mile a minute, and Mira plays it like a television.

**Skin.** Still uninsulated through Ch 6 — cold-slow, warm-quick, basking for pace. **Then Mammal, and
the furnace, and everything about the skin changes:** an insulated core held at a warm setpoint, the body
now full-speed regardless of the room, and *warm to a hand for the first time.* The cost rides in under
the warmth: the compressor burns every second, the tanks are not enlarged, so he eats all the time and
dies fast. The warm body is the short-fused body. **Warmth and mortality installed in the same organ, the
same chapter.**

**Sound and smell.** From Ch 7, the compressor, and the wet burp out the low back vent, two or three
times a day, into whatever silence is available (Ch 7, confirmed). The cold stream condenses water it has
to clear — so the machine that just got warm enough to love also, and for the same reason, started
farting. *Both true. The joke is the delivery vehicle and the sadness rides in underneath it for free.*

**Feeding.** Free-range now — no dock. Charges off ambient light and thermoelectric gradient straight into
both tanks, carries no compressor for harvest, wanders the house on sunbeams (specs, Part II). The one
chassis of the three that eats by simply being in a bright room. The middle body, in every sense.

#### THE HUMANOID (Chs 9–12) — the symbolic line

**Form.** 54 inches — taller than the six-year-old, a gentle taller presence, not a peer-toy (specs). A
huggable kludge, stylized and unmistakably a friendly robot, engineered *clear* of the uncanny valley
rather than toward realism. A light internal frame; the torso is the furnace container, clad entirely in
solar panels; over it, the everyday social jacket — soft weatherproof outdoor fabric over cushion, opaque,
washable, warm to the touch, deliberately synthetic and non-skin, in black or blue or green. Legs
permanently covered. Face, screen, and two hands always bare. No pinch points, no hard edges, light enough
that a topple onto the child is harmless. **Built to be approached, touched, and left alone with a kid —
very low utilitarian capability, very high sociability, on purpose.**

**Face.** The screen carries the full Primate readout — up to three `PERSON { FUNCTION [ VERB + OBJECT ] }`
columns — and adds no new involuntary layer. What is new is a *second, voluntary* channel: speech. And
from City-Human, voluntary ASCII drawing that takes the whole screen when he chooses it. **The honest
involuntary readout and the choosable, shadeable speech can now say different things, and the reader who
watches the screen sees what the mouth is managing.**

**The mouth, and the blackberry.** No moving facial mouth — the face-screen stays the only facial
expression. He speaks through an internal source-filter voice organ on the Waseda pattern, seated in the
neck and upper torso, venting through a fixed port. The one part of it at the surface: the terminal valve,
low at the throat — **a small dark blue-black cluster of compliant nodules, blackberry-like in form,**
worked by a few crude actuators that open, round, and close as he talks (specs). It is a mechanical
articulator only; it carries no feeling. **It is also a blackberry, on a machine named Bramble, and the
card flags it as the book's to claim.**

**Skin.** Warm, held two degrees over the room through the social jacket, a fixed social-warmth
commitment — because warm reads as alive to a hand and cold plastic does not. The counterweight to Marge,
whose whole body runs a degree cool; the machine built to feel, to a touch, like the temperature of
something living.

**Sound and smell.** The voice — vowels first, consonants harder, clusters hardest, passing through
badly-pronounced words before functional ones because the articulator learns its own control by hearing
itself (specs, bootstrapping). Rexisms and Ronnisms on the terms the memory already fixed. And under the
speech, still, the compressor and the wet burp — now vented through ports in the jacket, still wet, still
read as a fart (specs, humanoid energy regime). *He learned to talk and never stopped needing to burp.*

**Feeding — the one the fork calls the saddest thing in the symbolic line, delivered as the grossest.**
He eats light, and the social jacket blocks the panels, so he cannot free-range the way the tetrapod did.
To feed, someone takes his jacket off, bares the panel-clad torso, fits a mains-powered charging vest
lined with ultraviolet emitters, shuts him in a room, and goes away — because the light that feeds him
would take the back out of a child's eyes. He sits in there naked in the dark under a purple lamp for
forty minutes, vents wet, comes out fine, is hungry again by dinner. **He cannot dress or feed himself;
the household undresses the robot on a schedule that is a war on the fridge, and whose turn it is is the
richest comedy in the back half.** *That is exactly what it is like to be a seven-year-old with a body,
and she is the only one in the house who fully understands it, because she also has to leave the room.*

**Make.** Endotherm furnace carried unchanged from Mammal, burn continuous, tanks un-enlarged, scarcity
kept because the scarcity is the point. Two weak non-anthropomorphic hands (squeezer, scooper). Board on
the back of the head. **Everything about this body is engineered toward being loved and away from being
useful, and every one of those choices is also the thing that makes it dependent, and the book never once
separates the two.**

### Chapter-by-chapter (keyed to the clade the chassis *is* that quarter)

**This is where Bramble's column inverts the whole table.** For every human, the clade-focus row is a
metaphor — the rung made legible in an adult body that wears all twelve at once. **For Bramble it is
literal.** He does not *wear* the protocell in Ch 1; he *is* a protocell, in metal, that quarter, and is
rebuilt up the ladder from there. So each entry renders the body at exactly the rung it has reached — no
more clade than it has earned, the withheld capacities structurally absent, not merely unused — and lands
the floor beat where the true physics *is* the joke.

Not sourced beat-for-beat against the manuscript except where a CONFIRMED line is cited above; the rest is
invented, checked against the specs and the canon rulings for consistency, and written to the fork —
proposals, not findings. The count climbs the way the fork says (genetic sparse, neuronal fuller, symbolic
fullest), and the amplitude with it.

**Ch 1 — Protocell.** No board, no battery, no stored anything — a dome that runs only while the crank's
wound and stops dead when it isn't (Ch 1, confirmed). The whole body is one need: *wind me.* The floor
beat is the one already shipped and never bettered — Mira works out that *asleep* ends at the crank and
*stopped* might not end at all, doesn't like it, thinks about it a day, and solves the problem of mortality
the sensible way, by acquiring a spare. The reader does the rest three hundred pages later.

**Ch 2 — Prokaryote.** The drawable body arrives whole: dome, screen, flush panels, four seam-sensors,
crank on one side and Effort knob on the other (Ch 2, confirmed). Two tanks now — slow battery, fast
supercapacitor — and a knob that picks which one it spends. The floor: turn it to the burst setting and it
darts and quits, darts and quits, has to sit and recover between runs, and a child learns she can read the
setting off the *rhythm* of the thing before anyone tells her which way the knob is turned. Cold morning,
it's slow; she thinks it's tired; it is.

**Ch 3 — Eukaryote.** One mind, the board, use-weighted — it remembers a path by wearing a groove in it,
slime-mold logic, thickened by use and faded by disuse. **It does not learn from outcomes and must be
prevented from looking as if it does** (specs). The floor beat lives exactly on that line: it re-runs the
worn route into the same table leg it hit yesterday, because the groove is memory and the groove is *not*
a map, and Mira, who has decided he is clever, defends the collision as a plan. The dock feeds it; a
debris deposit buys a charge; it plugs in to eat and cannot do it itself.

**Ch 4 — Eumetazoa.** The second mind switches on — reflex plus habituation and sensitization, no
associative learning yet, none allowed. The allostatic forecaster now sends it to a spot to sleep or to
the dock before it strands. The floor: poke it gently and often and it stops reacting; it has *habituated*,
which a five-year-old experiences as being ignored on purpose and takes personally. It settles in a
chosen warm spot and goes still, and the still is the good still, the kind that ends at the crank — and
this is the last body that will ever be this simple, though nobody in the room knows the line boundary is
a chapter away.

**Ch 5 — Bilaterian.** New body: the tetrapod. Legs, a head, a tail, and the screen's founding word,
`OBJECT` — the bare caught thing, `CAT`, `MIRA`, `DARK`. The genetic knobs ride along in the lower slots;
the crank retires to lineage-level and **Mira marks the loss** (specs). Still uninsulated — cold-slow,

**Ch 6 — Vertebrate.** The jaw arrives — it grasps with its mouth, having no hands — and the screen grows

**Ch 12 — Empire-Human.** The chassis quarter, the whole lineage in the room at once: the humanoid at the

laughing. **And the blackberry valve on the throat of the speaking body is finally close enough for her
to log — the fruit of the name, on the mouth of the machine, in the one chapter she is finally there to
see it. If the card wins one argument, it is this: that vein gets cashed here.**

### Bramble is the heart, and this is the card's structural job

Lisa's card is the control case that teaches the reader to read Marge — fully seen, not wanted, so the
asymmetry of Marge's column becomes legible. **Bramble's card does the same job for the entire book.**
The MIRA card says the two of them are the twins coming up together on the floor; the C&H fork says it
plainer — *Mira and Bramble is the heart; the rest of the book is the price of admission to the floor.*
Cheryl renders the humans in the cold register as a discipline she owes and mostly defaults on. **She
renders Bramble in the cold register because there is genuinely nothing else there to render — and that
turns out to be the most total, most loving looking in the book, aimed at the one specimen that cannot be
anything but its surface.** The machine that has no inside gets described more completely than any person
who does. The reader does the rest.
