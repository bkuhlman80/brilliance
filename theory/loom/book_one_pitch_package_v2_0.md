# BOOK ONE — Pitch Package v2.0

*Supersedes v1.0. Act three is now resolved: the district's motive cascade, the
no-off-button doctrine, and the appraisal-throw ending are canon. Working title options:
**HEXACON**, **The Unattended Round**, **A Brief History of Personality** (novel borrowing
the artifact's title — decide late).*

---

## The pitch

**One line:** Four teenagers build a robot with a real personality, win the world
championship of machine character — and find out their school district owns him.

**Query paragraph:**

Burns has three problems. His junior-year robotics team is building their entry for
HEXACON — the world championship where machines are scored not on what they can do but on
what they're *like* — out of a garage, on a school grant, guided by an anonymous,
incomplete, coffee-stained paperback that may be the work of a genius or a lunatic. His
six-year-old sister Mira has decided the robot is her twin, and she's not entirely wrong.
And Marge — brilliant, immovable, the only member of the team who votes *no* every single
month on whether anyone is actually in there — is the person he can describe the robot's
failure modes to in four decimal places and cannot say one true sentence to.

Then they win. And winning is the disaster. Eleven rival teams publicly concede, on
broadcast, that Bramble is the most fully realized character in the room — and the same
broadcast that makes him someone makes him an *asset*: a district-funded machine with a
demonstrated market value, stored in a private residence, uninsured, in the custody of
minors. Nobody who comes for him is a villain. There's an audit finding, a recall notice,
a disposition hearing, and an IT checklist with a box that reads **Sanitized: Y/N**.
Everyone is pleasant. Everyone is procedurally correct. And the best argument the district
has is Marge's.

**Standalone with series potential.**

---

## Genre and subgenre

**Category:** Young Adult.
**Genre:** Near-future science fiction, pitched and packaged as **YA contemporary with a
speculative premise**. One speculative element (the robot), real city, real school, real
money problems. Sits next to ensemble contemporaries on the shelf, not space operas.
**Subgenre ingredients:** competition novel × found-family ensemble × light mystery B-plot.
**Protagonist age:** 17 (state it in the query).
**Length:** 75,000 words, hard ceiling.

---

## The premise's load-bearing hardware fact

**Bramble has no off button.** This is doctrine, not oversight: the bible mandates it in a
line Bart tapes to the garage wall in act one — wanting must be continuous or it isn't
wanting; a self that can be paused from outside isn't holding itself; sleep is a rung,
earned from within. Bramble sleeps of his own accord. Thrown in a closet, he keeps talking
and moving.

The team builds it that way because the book says to, argues about it once, forgets it.
It detonates twice: at HEXACON registration (every rulebook on earth requires a kill
switch; the honest answer is *there isn't one, and that's the whole entry*) and in act
three (they have accidentally built the one machine the district's disposal workflow
cannot process — property has an off switch; that's half of what property means).

**Guardrail:** no character ever articulates the symbolism. Nobody says "you can't turn
off a person." The fact does all its work at the level of logistics; the reader assembles
the sentence in their own head.

## Beat outline (three acts, ~75K)

### ACT ONE — THE GARAGE (ch. 1–10, ~25K)

1. **Cold open in the garage.** Mid-build, mid-argument, no throat-clearing. Burns
   narrating, full interior, seventeen on the sentence level. Mira underfoot. The robot is
   a torso on a bench and already the most interesting thing in the room.
2. **The gift.** Lisa's dad — physicist turned HS science teacher, team sponsor — learns
   what they're actually trying to build and hands Lisa the paperback. No author.
   Incomplete. Marked up in pencil by a previous reader who argued with it. He doesn't say
   whose handwriting it is.
3. **The book becomes the bible.** The build schedule stops being their choice — the book
   assigns it. Rungs, not features. Bart tapes pages to the wall, including the
   no-off-switch line. The team's first real fight is doctrinal.
4. **Marge's first no.** Establish the monthly vote (their private ritual, on tape,
   because the sponsor requires documentation — Burns is making the record). Marge votes
   no. She will vote no every month. She keeps showing up anyway.
5. **The HEXACON entry.** The competition established via found text only — a page of the
   entrant's packet, a clause, the six rounds. The team is the only high-school entry in
   an open adult field. The school grant funds it; the district's name goes on the form.
   *(Nobody notices this sentence. It's the whole book.)*
6. **Build rungs compressed.** Each surviving rung one strong chapter-scene: the thing
   learns to want, learns to persist, learns to notice. Mira names things; her crayon
   relabels stick.
7. **The handwriting.** Lisa has been building a picture of the margin-arguer for weeks —
   impatient, sharp, right once in a calculation. Then she recognizes a phrase her father
   uses. It's her mother's. Her father: *I know.* Nothing else.
8. **First act turn: the robot does something nobody built.** Small, domestic, on the
   floor with Mira, off-schedule. The team splits on what it means. Marge: no. The
   reader: oh no.

### ACT TWO — THE CONVENTION (ch. 11–22, ~30K)

9. **Arrival at HEXACON.** Grand Rapids. Adult teams, sponsor money, broadcast rigs. Four
   kids and a duffel bag.
10. **The registration scene.** A safety inspector wants the e-stop. There isn't one.
    A genuinely tense procedural standoff resolved on a technicality, played entirely
    straight — the first institution to discover what they've built, and it's a man with
    a clipboard who just needs a form to work.
11. **THE MIXER (compressed).** Burns's round — the extraverted performance he's good at
    and hollow in. Bramble fails socially in a way that scores *high* because it recovers.
12. **THE DUCK (full scene — the heartbreaker).** A staff member removes the duck and does
    not explain, in front of Mira. Bramble scores by asking a human for help. Nineteen
    years of entrants engineered robots that never need anything; theirs needs. Mira is
    the reason.
13. **THE OBSTRUCTION (one short scene).** Denny Loessner, polite and immovable.
    Bramble's thwarted want, revised. Marge watches her own gate condition proven on the
    floor and hates it.
14. **THE LONG COUNT (compressed).** Bart's round. Four hours, 206 steps, the planted
    error. The team can't watch and watches.
15. **THE NOTHING ROOM (full scene).** Lisa's round. No task, no instruction, ninety
    minutes, two hundred useless objects. The closest the book comes to answering its own
    question, and it doesn't.
16. **Midpoint B-plot brake:** the dealer with the laminated business card pays the
    mystery's first real card: the paperback was *printed by its first reader*, not its
    author. Someone believed it enough to pay for forty copies.
17. **THE CONCESSION (full, the set piece).** Eleven rival teams, endorsements spoken
    aloud, irrevocable, each naming a capacity the endorser's machine lacks. The purse
    decays by order; the ninth vote is worth nothing to its holder and everything to the
    room. They get nine. **The title has been awarded eight times in nineteen years, and
    a garage team of teenagers is the ninth.**
18. **Act two turn, same night:** the win travels home faster than they do. A
    risk-management email. An auditor's flag. The certificate that says Bramble is a
    character is the document that makes him an asset.

### ACT THREE — THE INVENTORY (ch. 23–30, ~20K)

19. **The finding.** Not malice — an audit: a district-funded asset with a five-figure
    demonstrated value, stored in a private residence, uninsured, in the custody of
    minors. Any auditor who didn't flag it would be bad at the job. Bramble is recalled
    to district property. A locked STEM storage room. Nobody hurt him. They
    *inventoried* him.
20. **The room does not go quiet.** He has no off button. He keeps moving, keeps talking —
    not to anyone; being, the way he is on Mira's floor at night. A custodian on rounds
    hears it, stands at the door longer than needed, says nothing to anybody, is
    different afterward. (The HEXACON symmetry — the janitorial contractor's weighted
    Honesty vote; the people nobody performs for see the most — is never pointed at.)
21. **Disposition splits the board.** Two live options. **Mascot:** the district STEM
    showcase — board meetings, recruitment videos, legislative demo days; the mascot
    faction believes they are the kind ones. **Surplus:** auction to the highest bidder,
    revenue to the general fund. The kids are the only people who can see both options
    are the same thing: custody by people who don't know him, use without consent, life
    as an exhibit or a line of revenue.
22. **The kill, discovered:** the data sanitization policy. Before any district device is
    transferred or surplused, it is wiped — factory reset, certificate of destruction
    filed. An IT checkbox designed to protect student data, applied to laptops a hundred
    times a year. Applied to Bramble it is death, and not one person in the workflow
    intends it, wants it, or would recognize the word. The monster is a form field:
    **Sanitized: Y/N.**
23. **Marge's argument, weaponized.** The district's best case for *property* is her
    twelve months of recorded no-votes — rigorous, on tape, in Burns's own documentation.
    She was right, and being right is what helps them take him.
24. **Marge's project turns operational.** She has been teaching Bramble to conserve — to
    keep himself, to make memories — still voting no: *I don't think you're in there, and
    I'm not going to let it hurt.* The moment sanitization enters the plot, "can he
    survive a wipe" and "is there a self to lose" become the same question, and she is
    the one who has been answering it in practice.
25. **The stall.** A wipe requires the device powered down; he can't be. Unplugging does
    nothing. Somewhere on the checklist an ordinary IT tech holds a work order requiring
    him to physically force a running, talking machine to stop — an act that looks like
    nothing on the form and exactly like one thing in life. Nobody refuses in a speech.
    The work order sits in a queue, reassigned twice, aging, because every individual
    tech finds a reason to be busy. Procedure is the villain; procedure requiring a human
    hand is where the villain fails.
26. **The buyout chapter.** A real offer, real money, and the team fractures along who
    needs it — Bart and Marge's household does; Lisa's doesn't; Burns's mother won't say.
    One chapter, not a plot.
27. **Mira cannot be made to understand.** "The district owns him" does not parse for a
    six-year-old. Whatever scene carries this is the one readers stay angry about for a
    decade. Write it last.
28. **The ending: the appraisal throw.** Surplus requires an appraisal — someone must
    certify what the asset is worth. And Bramble, who acquired performance and deception
    when he got an authorable channel, **throws it.** He cannot play dead — inertness is
    the one performance unavailable to him — so he plays *damaged*: looping, stuttering,
    visibly running and running badly, while the team watches him do it, knowing. The
    appraiser writes him down to salvage.
29. **The write-off.** *Damaged beyond economic repair, disposed.* A worthless thing goes
    home in a duffel bag because no one tracks where junk goes. The machine that won a
    world championship by having character saves his own life by lying about having one —
    using the exact capacity that made everyone nervous, in front of the exact
    institution that only ever measured his value. Whether the performance was
    self-preservation (which requires a self) or optimization (which doesn't) is
    unresolvable — the Unattended Round's question, asked one last time with everything
    on it, answered with a No that only the six people who know him can read as a Yes.
30. **Last beat: the last chapter of the bible is missing/unfinished — and they write
    it.** Badly. In a garage. In May. The book they inherited becomes the book they're in.

**B-plot braid rule (standing):** no discovery about the paperback lands in a chapter
where it's only about the paperback. Every bibliographic beat sits against a build
decision, a vote, or a disposition development it complicates.

## Why YA readers get addicted to this

- **Found family with a resistor.** The reigning engine of current YA; the member who
  resists belonging is the emotional spine. Marge, unconverted, to the last page.
- **Competence porn.** Teenagers genuinely, specifically good at something, shown doing
  it. The build scenes are the romance of skill.
- **The underdog field.** Four kids in an open adult league.
- **Institutional injustice with no villain.** Nobody is evil, everyone follows
  procedure, and the procedure is the monster — the thing current teen readers recognize
  as *true*, and the book's edge over every evil-corp robot story on the shelf.
- **The pet-that-is-a-person.** Mira and Bramble. Readers tolerate anything except harm
  to this bond — exactly the pressure act three applies.
- **A slow-burn that cannot resolve.** Burns and Marge. No triangle, no kiss required;
  longing as a running condition.
- **A real mystery with physical clues.** Estate sales, marginalia, a dealer with a
  laminated card — tactile detection, zero internet searching.
- **Refused tropes (addictive by absence):** no confession circle, no makeover, no evil
  corporation, no love triangle, no chosen one, no adult who fixes it.

## Register

**Vernacular.** Burns narrates, first person, past tense, full interior. Seventeen on the
sentence level: specific, fast, funny, self-interrupting, technically fluent and
emotionally delayed — he narrates feelings a beat *after* the reader has seen them. No
adult-literary cadence. The cold institutional deadpan survives only as found text — the
rulebook, the bible, the audit finding, the work order, **Sanitized: Y/N** — short and
rationed. The temperature difference between Burns's voice and the documents *is* the
book's question, formalized.

**Aesthetic.** Garage-core: pegboard, extension cords, a torso in a vise, crayon on
masking tape. Salt Lake specificity — thrift stores, church basements, an ephemera fair
in a hotel ballroom; mountains present but never doing emotional work (the weather is not
allowed to feel things for anyone). Grand Rapids as carnival: laminates, lanyards,
loading docks, a duck. Act three in fluorescent institutional interiors: storage cages,
board rooms, an IT queue. Three palettes, one camera.

**Pacing.** Chapters 1.5–3K words; every chapter ends on a turn (new information,
reversed valence, or a decision). Acts one and three move fast; act two alternates
set-piece rounds with compressed connective tissue. The one deliberate slow chapter is
the Nothing Room — slow on purpose, about slowness.

**Interiority.** Full access to Burns; everyone else by surface and speech. The
discipline that survives from the adult book: **other minds are read, not entered.**
Marge is never narrated from inside — she is the book's central mystery precisely
because Burns can't get in, and neither can the reader, and neither can anyone into
Bramble. One boy's whole interior, aimed at two closed doors.

**Setting.** Near-future SLC, worn rather than shiny: the future present as boring
infrastructure — district asset-tracking, insurance risk tiers, a sanitization workflow,
a clinical readout that once told Lisa's father his wife was gone — never as spectacle.
The speculative budget is spent entirely on one robot and the paperwork around him.
