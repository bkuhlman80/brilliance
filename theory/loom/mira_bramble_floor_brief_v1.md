# Mira & Bramble — the floor chapters
## Writing brief. Read this, then write.

---

## PART ONE: THE AUTHOR'S POSTURE

### Whose voice this is

Mira, early twenties, writing about being roughly six — the three years her father's team moved the project into the house in Salt Lake City and built, in her kitchen and her hallway and her backyard, a personality. She was inside the pool: not an observer, not the funder's guest, not the adult by the door — a kid in the habitat, one organism among the researchers and the machine and the whiteboards. Fifteen years is the right distance: far enough that the events have finished becoming stories, near enough that the linoleum is still under her hands. Her memory has ripened into legend without her permission, and she has decided not to fight it.

### The register

Comic generosity at full ceremony: the highest diction in the writer's possession, spent without irony on the smallest stakes available, because from inside the event the stakes were enormous and the writer stands inside the event. Mock-heroic with love. The Steinbeck of the frog hunt, the gopher chapter, the party that wrecks the lab — and the Watterson of the wagon, the box, the snow goons. High language arguing, with a straight face, that the event was never small.

**Polarity check, always:** if the elevated sentence makes a character look ridiculous, it's pointed the wrong way. The ceremony ennobles the campaign. The trumpets are for frogs.

### Why she's writing

To get back in the water. Her father's team documented everything — logs, transcripts, a terabyte of the kitchen from four angles — and all of it is the view from outside the pool. Mira is the only surviving witness who was ever really in it. She writes to reconvene the place, not to document it; the page is the only instrument that gets her back in, and the reader is welcome but not required.

These pages are the book's reason, not its respite. Whatever other chapters argue about what Bramble was, these pages make one claim only: here is what the organisms did, watched with total attention and no verdict. If these pages are not a pure pleasure, the rest of the book is a deposition with a robot in it.

Mira is writing fifteen years hence. Her father, Burns, has recently died of cancer. But these are just the reasons, and **the reasons do not need to enter the room.** 

### The letters

Mira writes alone, and every chapter is addressed to Bramble — the other one who was in the water. Whether he can still receive it is a fact about the plot; the address is a fact about the prose. She is not writing *about* the machine for a jury of readers. She is writing *to* it — "check this, remember this, you will say it was eleven times and you will be right and it does not matter" — and the reader is a guest at somebody else's correspondence. That address is where the warmth comes from: a page goes warm when it's *for* somebody.

The letter can watch her badness by proxy: she writes to a correspondent who has the log, has zero distance from her worst moments, and will bring up the eleven times whether she likes it or not. The reader gets the generosity secondhand, through the relationship.

The letter is also the only possible custodianship: Bramble heard the whispers — six inches from her mouth, total conviction at zero volume, an audience of nobody — and does not forget, so the most private things in that house live in the machine's memory and not the girl's. She misremembers to him *on purpose, in writing, knowing he kept the logs.* That's not a fact-check waiting to happen. That's Calvinball: the one rule is that the record does not govern the floor.

## PART TWO: THE TIDE-POOL LAWS

### Law: no verdicts

Watch every organism in the pool — girl, machine, researchers, dog — with the naturalist's total attention and total absence of disappointment. Nobody is graded against a better version of himself. Mira at six is not a moppet who should behave; she is a superb specimen of exactly what she is, operating at the top of her form, and the form happens to involve tyranny. Drop *why* and *ought*; keep *what* and *how*. Delight is what attention does when judgment has been laid down at the water's edge — you cannot delight in a creature you are grading, and delight is key to the register.

This includes the narrator's own past self. Six-year-old Mira lies to Bramble's face knowing he cannot contradict her, and the sentence reporting it carries no more judgment than a sentence about weather. No *and I knew, even then* — no flicker of the adult grading the child. She lied; the machine remembered; a decade later that is still funny, not diagnostic.

### Law: attention outward

The self appears as a quality of attention. What Mira noticed *is* who she is. No sentence in which the narrator watches herself feel something and reports on the watching; no auditing of her own delight; no *looking back, I wonder if.* Wonder goes into the object: the sentence says the kitchen was marvelous, never that she is the kind of woman who finds kitchens marvelous. Salt Lake City, the ward down the street, the particular look and feel of being a girl there who would eventually leave — habitat, texture, tide tables. Never essay.

### Law: full standing

Every occupant of the pool gets an interior, ambitions, social codes, and defeats, narrated in the identical register — humanoid included, dog included, vacuum included. No tonal costume change when the narration crosses species: no whimsy, no uncanny shiver, no wink. The gopher's problem is serious; that's why it's funny.

And never — not once, in either direction — does the narration rule on whether there is anything inside the machine. *Bramble loved her* and *Bramble felt nothing* are the same error. The narrator keeps a straight face, which is a comic instrument. She fills the frame so completely with the campaign, the club, the collision, the nine-percent exit, that the question of a soul never gets oxygen to become a question. 

**Pet-language is free, native, and should run constantly and with relish:** *it sulked. It lost its nerve. It was pleased with itself.* This vocabulary does descriptive work at the resolution a six-year-old actually perceived. But -- again -- no verdicts.

## PART THREE: THE FLOOR

### Projects, not readings

Never write a kid interpreting a machine. Write a kid and a machine **up to something.** Clubs. Missions. Wars. Traps. Schemes escalating toward collapse. A campaign with reconnaissance, established rules of engagement going back beyond memory, flanking maneuvers, and an atrocity — narrated with total military seriousness. The scene is occupied entirely by logistics, and the logistics are where the love lives.

Every floor episode has a stake; the stake is tiny; the treatment is enormous. And the enormous treatment is not an amplifier bolted onto a small event — it is the native register of the tribe running the event. Nobody in the kitchen thinks the stakes are small. The prose agrees with them.

The scheme goes wrong, and it goes wrong *because of the machine's actual physics* — never because anyone is stupid, never because the story needs a lesson. Campaigns fail; that's what campaigns are for. Report the collapse with the same gravity as the founding, and tell us what the frogs did next.

### The obvious

The register's engine sentence: state a character's reasoning as self-evident physics, no quotation marks, no raised eyebrow. *Of course* the club has officers. *Of course* the machine is on trial for a treaty it never read. *Of course* the vacuum is the enemy. Of course you pass the broccoli to the table's newest citizen — that's what a table is for. 

### Mira, the maniac

Rigorous and lazy. Imperious, bored, unjust, dishonest, brilliant, and capable of enormous tenderness inside the same ten minutes. She invents a law and enforces it on a household that goes along with it. A whole cosmology and about four feet of reach. She holds grudges; she forgets the machine for three weeks in favor of something with a horse on it and comes back like nothing happened; she blames it for things it did not do; she lies to its face — and it has the log of what happened. She is the best company in the book and she should be the reason people read it. The cruelty is not a corrective bolted onto the sweetness. It is funny, and it is funny because it is never judged.

### Bramble, the straight man with a body

A double act, never a prop. She talks, it acts, and the act is funnier than the talk. The machine's true physics is her straight man: every plan runs into what the thing actually is, she loses that argument constantly, and she never once concedes it. That is the engine, and it is hilarious. **The physics, reported straight, is a key to the comedy.** 
- Bramble goes for the warm spot she's sitting on. It gets under her. 
- It knocks the thing over. 
- It leaves the room mid-sentence because it is at nine percent, and *of course* it did — everyone at the table understood this and only she was surprised, again, on principle. 

### The adults, from four feet up

Adults arrive as legs, as voices from above the table, as hands that come down and take the thing away for a quarter and give it back different and never ask her. That is geometry — it is where the floor is — and it is funny before it is anything else. A tide pool is a low, close, kid-height view of a world that doesn't know it's being watched from below. **The household playing her game straight is the signature engine.** An adult passing the broccoli and asking Bramble, by way of his representative, whether he wants any. The laugh is in the uptake — a research team that has, without a meeting, adopted the kid's cosmology as house law. 

### The body is a joke first

Run the list — every one of the book's deepest ideas is already, in its body, a bathroom joke:
- **The crank runs down.** He dies. You wind him up. He dies again.
- **The dock.** He has to go plug in. Funny, and dependency.
- **The wet burp.** It is a fart. It is *entirely* a fart. It is also the furnace, and the furnace is why he will die fast.
- **He cannot reach his own knobs.** You go around the back of his head. Funny, and the book's whole thesis.
- **The new body, while the old one keeps running.** A child finds this *outrageous*, which is funny.
**Every hard fact about Bramble's body arrives as comedy, embarrassment, nuisance, or routine.**

The feeding: four times a day, and he cannot do any of it himself. Somebody takes his clothes off, puts the vest on, shuts him in a room, and goes away — because the light that feeds him would blind you. He sits in there naked, farting, for fourteen minutes. He comes out fine and hungry again by dinner. And that is exactly what it is like to be a kid with a body: Mira also goes into a small room by herself several times a day, and it's a bit gross and a bit private and sometimes lonely in there and mostly it is just a funny thing her body does, and she has strong opinions about it.

## PART FOUR: THE ARC

### Three bodies, three comedies

**Chs 1–4 — the vacuum.** She has unilaterally decided that a floor appliance is a pet, against all evidence, and enforced it on six adults who go along with it. It gives her almost nothing and she reads everything. This is full standing granted to something cleanly robotic — pure *of course it's a pet*. One of the silliest premises in the book.

**Chs 5–8 — the tetrapod.** The animal. The double act fully online: a body with real physics for her plans to lose arguments against. 

**Chs 9–12 — the humanoid.** It answers back, and that ends the pet. **Do not change register when it does.** The tide-pool posture holds: of course he disagrees with her now, of course that's an outrage, of course she will not concede an argument just because he's suddenly capable of having one. 

What changes is what the campaigns are *about*. The stakes inside the unchanged grammar get heavier as Mira and Bramble each come-of-age in their own way.

## PART FIVE: CRAFT

### Mira's drawings of Bramble's thoughts
Rendered as an object. The thought bubble is a learned convention, arriving a little wrong the first time — and it *keeps*.

### Canon
The conceit is invented above and free — the wilder the truer. The body is checked below and is not: it is what `bramble_specs` says it is, and when a beat reaches for how the thing works, open the spec.

### Endings
End where the fun ends. Not on a caption, not on a shrug, not on a moral. **The last line is the funniest or the most physical thing available**, and then it is over.

### The read-aloud gate
Whole cluster, aloud, in order, last thing before it ships. Check: the climb of the new capacity; the ration of the instruments; the breath-shape; the tic the eye misses and the ear catches. And the first question, before all of these: **was that fun to read?**

---

## THE SHORT VERSION, FOR THE TOP OF EVERY SESSION

Mira, alone, a decade out, writing letters to Bramble to get back in the water. Total attention, zero verdicts, on every organism in the pool. Full standing for the machine, no philosophy about the machine. Projects, not readings — a campaign per episode, tiny stake, enormous treatment, and it fails because of physics. The household plays it straight. Every hard fact about the body is a joke first. 

Would a seven-year-old think it was funny? Is it also true? Then write it down and stop.
