## Purpose

Reference material for the *Environment and tendencies* segment of the clade block (Book_Frame.md, Clade block §2). Burns presents the deep-time and environmental context for each clade as Lisa presents the clade's tendencies. This doc gives him the substrate to draw from: where the clade most likely emerged, when, under what climate and atmospheric conditions, against what evidentiary record. The doc is neutral reference. Burns transforms it into his own voice at draft time.

The biological clades (Protocell through Primate, Ch 1–8) use one category set. The symbolic-line clades (Band-Human through Empire-Human, Ch 9–12) use a category set adapted to a timescale where plate tectonics is essentially static and where geography, climate stability, and ecology carry more weight than tectonic and atmospheric variables.

Dates carry implicit uncertainty bars; "approximately" and "contested" are used where the science is actively debated.

## Symbolic-line clades (Ch 9–12)

Plate tectonics is essentially static on this timescale. Climate, regional geography, and ecology carry the load.

Category set:

- **Time period.** Calendar dating with cultural milestone anchors.
- **Region of emergence.** Where the clade first appears; whether origins are single or multiple.
- **Climate.** Climatic regime and variability across the clade window.
- **Regional geography.** The landscapes that shape the clade.
- **Key human-relevant events.** Discrete events that shape the clade's window.
- **Domesticable species and ecology.** What's available to eat, ride, or live alongside.
- **Key archaeological sites.** Where Burns can anchor specifics.
- **Signature evidentiary signal.** The marker that anchors the transition.

### Band-Human (Ch 9)

**Time period.** Anatomically modern *Homo sapiens* from approximately 300 ka (Jebel Irhoud). Behavioral-modernity timing contested — between approximately 70 ka (Recent African Origin view) and approximately 300 ka (gradualist view). Clade window runs through the end of the Pleistocene at approximately 12 ka.

**Region of emergence.** Africa, likely with multiple regional contributions rather than a single point of origin. Major out-of-Africa dispersals from approximately 60 ka onward. Australia reached by approximately 50 ka; the Americas by approximately 16–20 ka.

**Climate.** Pleistocene ice ages. Last Glacial Maximum approximately 26.5–19 ka — cold, dry, expanded ice sheets across northern Europe and North America, lower sea levels exposing land bridges (Beringia, Sahul).

**Regional geography.** African Rift Valley as a long-term hominin landscape. Coastal Africa important for resource diversity. Expansion across all continents except Antarctica by the end of the Pleistocene. Mosaic environments — savanna, forest, coast, mountain, tundra.

**Key human-relevant events.** Toba super-eruption (~74 ka; bottleneck contested). Last Glacial Maximum. Bering land bridge open. African Humid Periods, including the late-Pleistocene Green Sahara. First unambiguous symbolic artifacts — Blombos ochre, Diepkloof ostrich-eggshell engravings.

**Domesticable species and ecology.** No domestication yet. Late Pleistocene megafauna present — mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths, giant marsupials, *Glyptodon*, cave bears. Late-Pleistocene megafauna extinctions begin coincident with human arrival on each continent.

**Key archaeological sites.** Jebel Irhoud (Morocco, ~315 ka); Omo Kibish (Ethiopia, ~195 ka); Blombos Cave (S. Africa, ~75 ka); Sibudu (S. Africa); Lake Mungo (Australia, ~42 ka); Sungir (Russia, ~30 ka); Chauvet and Lascaux (France, ~17–32 ka); Monte Verde (Chile, ~14.5 ka).

**Signature evidentiary signal.** The behavioral-modernity package — symbolic art, personal ornaments, long-distance trade in raw materials, complex burials, projectile weapons, microlithic technology.

### Settlement-Human (Ch 10)

**Time period.** From the end of the Younger Dryas at approximately 11.7 ka through the emergence of the first cities at approximately 5.5 ka. The Neolithic transition.

**Region of emergence.** Multiple independent centers of agricultural origin — Fertile Crescent (wheat, barley, sheep, goats, ~10–9 ka); Yangtze (rice, ~9–8 ka) and Yellow River (millet); New Guinea (taro, banana); Mesoamerica (maize, beans, squash, ~9–6 ka); Andes (potato, quinoa, llama); Eastern Woodlands of North America; West Africa.

**Climate.** End of Pleistocene; entry into Holocene climatic stability. Younger Dryas cold snap (~12.9–11.7 ka) just before the warm Holocene. African Humid Period — the Green Sahara — running from approximately 14.5 ka to approximately 5 ka. The 8.2 ka event is a brief sharp cooling. Holocene climate is overall warm and remarkably stable, making predictable agriculture possible.

**Regional geography.** Rivers and floodplains as agricultural cores — Tigris-Euphrates, Nile, Indus, Yellow, Yangtze. Coastal and lacustrine wetlands. Mediterranean and monsoon zones. The Fertile Crescent's mix of high-quality domesticable plants and animals gives it an early lead.

**Key human-relevant events.** Younger Dryas. Holocene onset. Agricultural origins in multiple regions independently. First pottery. First permanent villages — Jericho (~10 ka), Çatalhöyük (~9 ka). Göbekli Tepe (~11 ka) — monumental architecture that precedes farming, a key challenge to settled-life-causes-monuments framings.

**Domesticable species and ecology.** Sheep, goats, cattle, pigs, dogs in SW Asia and Europe. Rice, millet, pigs, chickens in East Asia. Maize, beans, squash, turkey in Mesoamerica. Potato, llama, alpaca, quinoa in the Andes. Eurasia's east-west axis carries a domesticable-species advantage (Diamond's thesis); Africa and the Americas have north-south axes that slow diffusion.

**Key archaeological sites.** Göbekli Tepe (Turkey, ~11 ka); Jericho (~10 ka); Çatalhöyük (Turkey, ~9 ka); Jiahu (China, ~9 ka); Banpo (China); Mehrgarh (Pakistan); Caral / Norte Chico (Peru, ~5 ka).

**Signature evidentiary signal.** Sedentism, pottery, polished stone tools, domestication signatures in plant and animal remains, storage architecture, megalithic monuments.

### City-Human (Ch 11)

**Time period.** From the Uruk period and the first cities at approximately 5.5 ka (3500 BCE) through to the emergence of large multi-region empires at approximately 2300 BCE (Akkad). Roughly the Bronze Age and into the Iron Age across regions.

**Region of emergence.** Mesopotamia first — Uruk (~4000 BCE). Egypt — predynastic Naqada into the Old Kingdom (~3100 BCE). Indus Valley — Harappa and Mohenjo-daro (~2600 BCE). China — Erlitou (~1900 BCE). Mesoamerica — Olmec (~1500 BCE), then Maya. Andes — Caral / Norte Chico (~3000 BCE). Aegean — Minoan, then Mycenaean.

**Climate.** Mid-to-late Holocene. The 4.2 ka event — a regional drought — coincides with the Akkadian collapse and Old Kingdom Egyptian decline, often invoked as a contributing factor. African Humid Period winding down. Generally cooler and drier than the early Holocene optimum.

**Regional geography.** Floodplain civilizations on Tigris-Euphrates, Nile, Indus, and Yellow rivers — the "river-valley civilizations" framing. Secondary cities along trade routes. Mediterranean as an inland sea linking cultures. Trade beginning to span hundreds of kilometers in obsidian, tin, copper, lapis lazuli.

**Key human-relevant events.** Bronze metallurgy from approximately 3300 BCE. Cuneiform writing from approximately 3200 BCE (Uruk). Hieroglyphics from approximately 3200 BCE (Egypt). Wheel and wheeled transport. The 4.2 ka event and its collapses. Late Bronze Age Collapse approximately 1200 BCE. Spread of iron metallurgy through the early first millennium BCE.

**Domesticable species and ecology.** Cattle, sheep, goats, donkeys integrated into agricultural systems. Horses domesticated by approximately 3500 BCE on the Pontic-Caspian steppe. Irrigation agriculture transforming floodplains. Deforestation around cities. Soil salinization in southern Mesopotamia.

**Key archaeological sites.** Uruk, Ur, early Babylon (Mesopotamia); Memphis, Thebes (Egypt); Mohenjo-daro, Harappa (Indus); Anyang (Shang China); Knossos (Crete); Mycenae, Troy; Caral (Peru); San Lorenzo (Olmec).

**Signature evidentiary signal.** Monumental urban architecture — ziggurats, pyramids; writing systems; specialized craft production; urban planning; social stratification visible in differentiated burials; palace and temple complexes; mass-produced standardized pottery such as the Uruk bevel-rim bowl.

### Empire-Human (Ch 12)

**Time period.** From the Old Babylonian Empire (~1800 BCE) — with Akkad (~2300 BCE) as imperial precursor — through to the onset of the Industrial Revolution at approximately 1750 CE. Roughly 3,500 years.

**Region of emergence.** Mesopotamia for the earliest large multi-region empires (Akkad, Old Babylonian, Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, Achaemenid Persian). Then Mediterranean (Hellenistic, Roman). South Asia (Mauryan, Gupta, Mughal). East Asia (Qin, Han, Tang, Song, Ming, Qing). Mesoamerica (Teotihuacan, Maya, Aztec). Andes (Wari, Inca). West Africa (Mali, Songhai). Eurasian steppe (Scythian, Hunnic, Mongol). Maritime European empires from approximately 1500 CE onward.

**Climate.** Late Holocene. Medieval Warm Period approximately 950–1250 CE. Little Ice Age approximately 1300–1850 CE, overlapping the back end of the clade window. Overall Holocene neoglacial cooling trend.

**Regional geography.** Empires connecting previously distinct hearths through roads, sea routes, and overland caravans. Mediterranean and Silk Roads as primary connective tissue across Afro-Eurasia. Oceanic trade emerging after approximately 1500 CE.

**Key human-relevant events.** Alphabetic writing from approximately 1200 BCE (Phoenician). Coined money from approximately 600 BCE (Lydia). Axial Age religious and philosophical traditions approximately 800–200 BCE. Silk Roads active by Han / Roman era. Black Death approximately 1346–1353 CE. Columbian Exchange from 1492 CE. Printing press in Europe mid-15th century (printing earlier in East Asia). Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment. Onset of the coal-and-steam Industrial Revolution approximately 1750 CE.

**Domesticable species and ecology.** Full integration of horse, camel, mule, and ox as long-distance transport and military assets. Agricultural specialization by region. Large-scale deforestation across Eurasia. Introduction of Old World crops, animals, and diseases to the Americas after 1492 — catastrophic for Indigenous populations. Megafauna long gone.

**Key archaeological and historical sites.** Babylon, Persepolis, Athens, Rome, Constantinople, Xi'an, Chang'an, Beijing, Teotihuacan, Tenochtitlan, Cusco, Timbuktu, Angkor, Great Zimbabwe, Cahokia.

**Signature evidentiary signal.** Monumental imperial architecture; standardized weights, measures, and coinage; legal codes (Hammurabi's, Roman, Tang); bureaucratic records; road and post systems; large-scale irrigation works; religious universalism; the written historical record itself becomes a primary evidence stream alongside archaeology.
