# Ch 0: Bots

*Brian Kuhlman. draft v1.0. June 2026.*

---

***February 12, 2031***

The bots came up the driveway in a foam-lined case about the size of a hardcover book, five of them, numbered one through five on the underside in black marker someone had put there in a hurry and that would not all the way come off. It was a Saturday in February, cold and clear, the peaks white and the valley locked in under an inversion that would sit there for days, and the warmest thing for a mile in any direction was the slate inside the Burns kitchen, which Lisa was standing on the doorstep of, holding the case out to Jasmine the way you hold out a thing you are not sure you ought to be handing over. Bart waited in the van with it running. Burns was out on the step, explaining the mechanism to Jasmine — already mid-sentence, the way Burns is always already mid-sentence — because Burns could not let a new thing into the house without explaining it first, and would sooner do it on a cold step than not do it at all.

This was the winter before the rest of it. Before the lab had held a single retrospective, before the warehouse had its name on the outside, before the four people who would give the next three years to this had let themselves understand what they had agreed to be the ones to do. They had been at the bots for weeks. They did not have a robot. They had five small things that twitched, and a hunch about why the twitching was worth three years that none of them could have said out loud yet without feeling foolish, and a four-year-old's birthday to bring it all to.

Mira took the case to the kitchen floor. The slate had radiant heat under it — Jasmine's one condition when they built the house, run through the cold months and shut off when the spring warmed, and it had paid itself back across four winters of it. Mira sat down on the warm stone the way a child sits when she has decided she will be where she is for a while, cross-legged, the case beside her, and took the bots out one at a time and set them in a row.

They were small, about the size of a fist, low and domed, the kind of thing you cannot look at without your hand wanting to go to the top of it. Each had a key standing up out of its dome. Each had two small patches set on its body in slightly different places, and Mira, who was four, did not know what they were for and did not ask. What Burns was telling her was that the patches drew heat up off the floor and let it go into the air, and that the difference between the warm stone and the cooler room was the whole of what made the things move — that they ran, in other words, on the floor being warmer than the air above it, and would go still when they had spent that difference down. She let his explanation pass over her the way a woman lets pass the explanations of a man she has loved a long time — not from inattention but because she had decided to come to know the thing some other way — and kept herself for the part she wanted, which was the child meeting the thing.

Mira wound the first one with both hands on the key, the way she had been taught to wind her music box, and set it down. It sat. Then it twitched, and twitched again, and started off in a small busy circle, and stopped, and went the other way. She watched it. After a while she wound the second and set it beside the first, and the second did a different thing — fewer moves, longer waits between them, a lean to the right the first one did not have. She wound the third, and the fourth, and the fifth, in order, because she had a sense even then that the order was part of it. By the time all five were going, the slate around her had become a small busy field of differently-shaped twitching — five fixed little natures spending themselves down at five different rates on a warm floor — and the child in the middle of them had gone quiet, and stayed quiet, a long time.

What she said, when she finally said anything, was hello.

She said it to the third one. The third was the least of the movers, the most held back; it would do one small thing and stop, and a long while later do one more small thing and stop, and it was the one that, by the end of the morning, would still have a little tension in its spring when the rest had gone slack. Mira did not know that yet. She had wound them all at once and the running-down was still ahead of her. She knew only, in whatever it is a four-year-old knows things in, that this was the one — the one she would set apart from the others by the next morning, and carry through the house in one fist by spring, and that the four in the warehouse would settle on by the May retrospective as the one worth building forward, and that would by then have a name, a word she had lifted off something Lisa drank out of a jar. But the name was a long way off. The floor was warm. The child had said hello to a thing that did not know it had been said hello to, and could not have been made to know it. The bot, in the way of bots, went on twitching.

---

How they had gotten there — the bots, the van, Lisa at the door, Mira on the warm slate — is a story that starts some months earlier with Burns at his desk in his Westminster office reading a contest description on the internet. 

***November 11, 2030***

It would have been late afternoon, the kind of fall light Burns loved and that nobody else in his department got to see because they were all in their offices. He was supposed to be writing a paper on a new tiktaalik vertebra and was instead doing what he always did when he was supposed to be writing, which was reading something else.

The contest had been posted by Cheryl on a mailing list Burns had been on for twenty years and that he would have said, if asked, he no longer paid much attention to. The mailing list was the kind that occasionally produced something interesting and more often produced fundraising appeals. The contest had been at the top of his inbox that morning. He had ignored it. By late afternoon, he was reading it for the third time.

The contest had two parts. The first was the prize, which was a lot. The second was the rules, which were unusual: build twelve robots, one per provided evolutionary snapshot, deliver them over three years at quarterly retrospectives, and — this was the part that made Burns stop and read it again — each robotic demonstration had to include a knob that manipulated the robot's personality. A user-accessible parameter. Something you could turn.

He read this and sat back in his chair. He had not been thinking about personality in a long time. He had been thinking, professionally, about the deep-time architecture of how an animal becomes able to live on land — the bones, the lungs, the new way of moving — and the personality requirement was at the wrong end of his mind for the work he had been doing. But it lit something up at a different end. Some months earlier, his colleague Marge had published a short story about a couple who, after years of therapy, had installed four small switches on the back of each other's heads, one per typological dimension, and who had used the switches over the course of the story to make themselves into the people the other one wanted to be married to, and who had, at the end of the story, not known who they were anymore. Burns had liked the story. He had told Marge it was the best thing she had written. He had also, privately, thought that the four switches were the most useful piece of speculative apparatus he had encountered in some time.

He read the contest a fourth time. Then he picked up his phone and called Marge.

---

Marge made him say it twice. He said it twice. She said she would meet him for coffee the next morning at the place across from the library, where she always wanted to go and he always pretended to like, and that she would bring her notes.

***November 12, 2030***

She did not bring notes. She brought, instead, the face of a person who had already decided the answer was no and was there as a favor. Marge was forty-one then, a year and a half into a slow exit from a religion she had been in since she was born, and the exit had given her — among other things — a wariness about projects that asked their participants to be smarter than they were. She had known Burns for fifteen years. She thought he would die before he learned not to grab at things. She had come to coffee anyway.

"This is not us," she said.

"It could be."

"We are not technologists, Burns."

"We do not need to be. We need to recruit them."

"You have someone in mind."

"Two."

Marge looked at the screen again. She looked at her coffee. She looked at Burns. She had been having approximately this conversation with him for fifteen years, and the conversation had occasionally produced a paper they had each been glad of and had more often produced a long lunch and a parking-lot good-bye in which Burns had said *think about it* and Marge had said *I will* and had not, particularly. This time was different and she could feel it being different. The four switches were her. She had written the four switches.

"Who are the two," she said.

"My son and Lisa."

"Your son does not work at this university."

"He works in a garage four miles from here and he can build a chassis with his eyes closed."

"Lisa is in computer science."

"Yes."

"Lisa is, specifically, the person at this university who would understand what a knob is supposed to do."

"She is. I could go on about her for an hour." He didn't.

Marge drank her coffee. The conversation had landed somewhere it had not landed in fifteen years. She had said *who* and not *no.* Burns, who was not a stupid man, knew not to push. He sat with his coffee. He waited.

"When are we sitting down with Bart," she said.

---

***November 14, 2030***

Bart's garage was in Millcreek, four miles south of Westminster, and was the half of a duplex he shared with two friends who were also engineers and who had between them never once cleaned the bathroom in the back. He used the front bay as a workshop. The back bay held an Audi he was rebuilding for a customer and a refrigerator that contained only beer. Burns and Marge came down on a Tuesday after work. Bart had cleared the kitchen table.

He was thirty then, and he listened to Burns the way he listened to all of Burns's projects, which was with the patience of a son who had heard a great many ideas pitched at his kitchen table and who had noticed that the good ones tended to involve other people doing the talking. Burns explained the contest. Marge explained why she had not, at first, wanted to do it. Bart listened. He looked at the printout of the contest description Burns had brought. He read the personality-knob requirement. He read it again. He looked up.

"You are saying twelve robots."

"Yes."

"In three years."

"Yes."

"With a knob in each one that does — what."

"Different personalities."

Bart looked at the printout again. He set it down. He looked at his father, then at Marge, then back at his father.

"This is the kind of thing I would do," he said.

"Yes," said Burns.

"This is the kind of thing where in six months I would still be doing it because I had already started and I do not stop."

"Yes."

"You have funding."

"Not yet. We pitch in January."

Bart absorbed this. He drank some of his beer. He looked across the table at Marge, who had not said much in the last several minutes and who had been watching him in the way she watched a person whose mind she was already starting to map. He had known her for years — they had been at four or five of his father's department parties together — and he had never quite looked at her face the way he looked at it now, in the bad overhead light of his kitchen with the contest description between them. He did not say what he was thinking. What he said was, "Okay. I am in."

---

***November 15, 2030***

Lisa was harder. Lisa was harder because Lisa was the one who had been looking for this without knowing she was looking for it, and the looking-without-knowing had built up a structural skepticism in her — the skepticism of a person who had said yes to two or three things that had turned out to be what someone else thought she was looking for, and who had learned, over a decade in computer science, to make a thing prove itself before she joined it.

She had been working on an Evolutionary Algorithm since her senior year of college. She had carried it through her PhD and into her first faculty job and into her second; she had never published it, because the part of it she had wanted to publish had never quite worked, and the part of it that did work had not yet, in her hands, generalized; and at thirty-five she had reached the point where she could no longer say with confidence whether the algorithm was a piece of her work or a piece of her self, the line having blurred in the way that lines tend to blur when a person has been carrying something for half their life.

The three of them came to her office on a Thursday. She had been told that morning, by both Marge and Bart separately, that Burns was bringing something and that she should clear an hour. She had cleared an hour. She had not cleared more than an hour, on principle.

Burns did the talking. He laid out the contest. He laid out the team — Marge on personality, Bart on hardware. Then he stopped, and he said, "Lisa, the reason I am here, the reason all of us are here, is that this contest cannot be done without your algorithm."

Lisa, who had been listening with the kind of patience she had learned in her thirties, took a long beat. She looked at Burns. She looked at Marge. She looked at Bart. Bart was watching her in the way he watched her when he was about to say something he had been waiting to say.

"You have not heard the rest of it," she said.

"I have heard enough of it. Twelve robots. Twelve clades. Each one needs an inheritance mechanism that crosses the line from the last one to the next one. Your algorithm is the inheritance mechanism. Without it we cannot answer the contest."

Lisa did not say anything for a moment. Then she said, "You are going to need it to do something it has never done."

"Yes. Which is why it has to be you — anyone else would tell me it can't, and you've spent your career finding out it almost can."

"You are going to need it to generalize across substrates."

"It will. And if it doesn't, it'll fail on the most interesting problem anyone's ever handed it."

"This is the only application I would say yes to."

"I know. I didn't bring it to anyone else."

Lisa looked at Bart. Bart, in the way of Bart, had the expression of a person watching a thing happen that he had predicted with some precision. She looked at Marge. Marge had her hands folded the way she folded them when she was choosing not to talk.

"Okay," Lisa said. "I am in."

Burns sat back. Bart sat forward. Marge did not change posture. They were, the four of them, a thing.

It was Bart who named the thing. He was looking around Lisa's office — Lisa's office was on the third floor of the science building, with a window that looked out at the parking lot and the foothills behind it — and he was looking at the three of them, and he said, in the voice he used when he had located a piece of a system, "Team Smithers."

"What did you call us," said Marge.

"Team Smithers. We are Team Smithers. There is no Smithers, so we are Team Smithers. Smithers is the place. Smithers is the lab. Smithers is where we work."

"We do not have a place yet."

"Smithers will be the place when we have it."

Burns laughed. Then he said: "Okay. We are Team Smithers. We pitch in three weeks."

---

***December 1, 2030***

I had bought the warehouse eighteen months before I needed it and kept it empty the whole time. It was in the Granary district, on the corner of two of the streets the locals named the way they named streets in Salt Lake — eighth south, fourth west — and it had a fenced patio out back with a real wood trellis already standing on it and nothing growing up the trellis. On the morning of the Pitch I got there first. I let myself in and set a stool in the middle of the floor and put my thermos on a folding chair beside it and waited.

They came in two cars. I heard the doors. They brought everything in through the side door and across the concrete with their arms full — a Roomba, a stack of printed resumes, a laptop, a foam-core board with something taped to it, a cardboard tray of coffees, and a bound printout that had the look of a thing that had been carried around for two months. They set it all on the long table at the back. I did not get up. Burns crossed the floor and put out his hand and I took it, once, and it was the only time I would touch any of them for three years. Then I said, "I am Cheryl. Let's begin."

Bart opened. He set the Roomba on the table and the resumes beside it and started in on who they were and why they had assembled, building toward something that was going to be a joke, and four sentences in I put up one finger.

I did not say anything. I held the finger up and looked at the back wall, where there was a metal door and a fuse box and the corner of a window and nothing else to look at. Bart stopped mid-sentence. Lisa, on his left, went stiller than she already was. I held it about ten seconds, which is longer than it sounds, and then I put the finger down and said, "Keep going."

He kept going. The joke did not land, because they had all reset, and Bart did not push it, which told me something about Bart. He finished and handed off to Lisa.

Lisa walked me through her algorithm without once using a word that would have made it sound like software, which is a harder thing to do than it looks and which I understood, watching her do it, that she had spent the morning learning how to do. I listened with my hands in my lap and did not say anything when she finished.

Burns took the deep time. He stood up to do it and used his hands. He laid out his guess at the twelve — twelve turns in deep time, each one built on the last — and his sequence ended with us, with people, because he did not yet know that the last cards in the portfolio said cities and empires. He was good at it. He was the kind of man who warms when a room turns toward him, and the room turned toward him, and I let it, and watched him without moving my head.

Marge went last. She set a foam-core board on an easel, and there was a Venn diagram on it she had clearly been carrying a long time. Four circles. Extroversion, Intuition, Introversion, Thinking. The four places they overlapped two by two — Perception, Feeling, Sensation, Judgment. The four fallacies in the three-way overlaps, named where the literature had named them: Noble Savage, Blank Slate, Ghost in the Machine, Dealt Hand. And the center, where all four met, which she called Personality Theory.

I looked at it for a long time. It was worth looking at. I looked at it long enough that Marge, who did not strike me as a fidgeter, fidgeted, and long enough that Burns started to open his mouth and Bart put a hand on his father's wrist. Then I said, "That is a beautiful diagram."

"Thank you," she said.

"I am not going to comment on it. I want to be clear about that. I am not going to comment on any of what you have shown me. I am going to give you three things and then I am going to go. The next time you see me as a team will be at your last retrospective, three years from now. I will see each of you before that, one at a time, and I will tell you when. In between you will record the retrospectives on a camcorder and give me the tapes, and I will watch the tapes."

I lifted the portfolio from beside the stool and set it on the table.

"There are twelve cards in here. Each one has a clade on it. They are not all of the clades, and they are in no order except the one I have decided on, which you will work out for yourselves. You will build one robot per card across twelve quarterly retrospectives, and each robot will have a knob on it that controls something about its personality. You will work out what the knob does. You will work out what the personality is. You will work out everything I have not told you, which is nearly all of it. I am paying you to work it out. I am not paying you to be right."

I took the check out of the satchel and set it on top of the portfolio.

"That is the first installment. There are three more across the three years. The warehouse is yours for the duration. You will not pay rent and you will not pay utilities. You will pay for what you build."

I stood up. I had been on the stool an hour and I stood up without the small adjustments people make after sitting that long, because I had decided beforehand that I would. "I am going outside," I said. "Look at what is in the portfolio." And I went out the back door to the patio.

The patio was cold. I sat under the trellis at the metal table with my thermos and did not need to see the table inside to know what was happening at it, because I had made the cards myself and laid them in the order I wanted, and I could see them the way you see a room you have left. Protocell. Prokaryote. Eukaryote. Eumetazoa. Bilaterian. Vertebrate. Mammal. Primate. Bands. Settlements. Cities. Empires. Heavy matte stock, one word a card, the backs blank. They would be turning them over now and finding the backs blank. Twelve words I had carried up and down the canyon for a year and a half, and I had just handed them to four people I had decided were the ones who were going to do it, and I sat in the cold and let them have it.

They came out after a while. Burns had one card in his hand, the first one, Protocell.

"We have a question," he said.

"You have many questions. Pick one."

"Why these twelve."

"I am not going to tell you."

"Why not."

"Because if I tell you, you will build the robots I would have built, and I want the robots you build."

"You are seeding us."

"I am seeding you."

Marge sat down across from me and set the foam-core against the leg of the chair. "May I try the Venn against the clades," she said.

"You may try anything you want against the clades. That is what you are paid for."

She held the diagram up against the Protocell card. She looked at the four circles and the four functions and the four fallacies, and then she set it down. "This does not work," she said. "A protocell has no thinking-or-feeling preference. It has no self. The diagram assumes a self — it assumes a subject doing the typing. None of these things are subjects yet." She set the board down beside her chair with the diagram facing the trellis, the way you set down a thing you are done arguing with.

"It is a good diagram," I said. "It is a different diagram from what you are going to need."

"I know."

"You will keep it for the back of the room."

"I will keep it for the back of the room."

Bart had been watching the board go down. "We should call this place something," he said.

"Smithers," said Lisa.

"Smithers is the team. Smithers is us. We cannot also call the place Smithers."

"We can. We have been calling it Smithers for two months."

"We need a name for other people."

Marge was looking up at the trellis, the bare wood of it with nothing climbing it yet. "Trellis," she said. "The Trellis Lab. Smithers is what we call it. Trellis is what we say to everyone else."

They all looked at her. Burns looked up at the trellis and then at her and laughed from the chest and said, "Trellis. Okay."

The name was settled and the men had moved on to the rest of it, and I did not move on. I was watching Marge. She had found the word looking up at a bare frame, and she had let it go the way you let go of a thing that has left your hands cleanly, and her face when she did it was at rest for the first time all morning, and I watched her face be at rest. There was nothing left on the table to be evaluating. The diagram was down. I watched her sit back in the metal chair and let the others finish deciding, and I went on watching her after there was any reason to, and Lisa saw me do it. I felt Lisa see me. Lisa, who saw everything, saw me watch Marge a beat past the end of the meeting, and I looked at my thermos and gave her nothing else to see, and Lisa said nothing.

I stood and picked up my thermos and my satchel. The check was on the portfolio inside, where I had left it. I walked back through the warehouse, past the long table with the cards on it, and out the front door, and I did not say goodbye.

Outside it was the kind of clear cold that makes the mountains look closer than they are. I got in the car and drove out of the Granary and onto the road that goes up the canyon, and the warehouse got small behind me and then the city did. I had shaken one hand. I would not be in a room with the four of them again for three years. The tapes would come up the canyon to me, and I would sit with them in the winter dark and watch four people do the thing I had set going and would now only ever see later, at one remove, on a screen. I had wanted it that way. I drove up into the trees and left them to it.

## Reflection: What is Learned?

No dog ever learned, across its own life, that the woods had bears in them. The line of dogs learned it — across the long line of woods, across the long line of dogs that did not come back — and handed each dog after them a flinch it had done nothing to earn. My doodle Maggie, asleep on my kitchen floor as I write this, has never met a bear. She carries the bear anyway, folded down into the part of her that startles before she has decided to, and she may some day hand the same bear forward to her own pups without once being able to say what it is they ought to be afraid of.

That is not a figure for the thing. It is the thing. Evolution and learning are the same — not alike, which was the long and honest sixty-year mistake, because to call two things alike is to have agreed already that they are two. Watson and Szathmáry wrote it out plainly: selection is computation. A line learns across its generations the way a learning system learns across its runs, keeping a little of what it tried and letting the rest go dark. The proofs are other people's and run two ways, depending on whether the line recombines, and I have turned them over often enough to recite them and won't, because the reciting is the part that always gets in the way. Different goo, same algorithm. The result had been lying on an open page for fifteen years — the biologists not reading it, the computer scientists never told — and I carried it up off that beach the way you pocket a stone a hundred people have stepped over.

It is never written in the dog. It is written one rung under the dog, in the wiring that builds the next one — which genes speak, and when. The individual does not update; the individual dies. What learns is the line, and the page it writes the lesson on is the very stuff the next body is made from, so the flinch comes already installed, already warm, older than the animal it runs in and with nothing remembered attached to it.

I understood all of this on the road up the canyon, and did not understand, until somewhere in the dark of the trees, that I had been describing myself the whole way. I keep to one remove. I shook a single hand and would not stand in a room with those four again for three years; I built an apparatus out of distance — the tape, the canyon, the winter dark at the top of it — and called it a design, and was proud of how clean it was. It is not a design. It is a flinch. Someone taught me, early and well, to stand off from the people I work beside, taught it so far down that I pull back from the warm room before I have decided to, and I have never been able to say what I think is in there waiting. I did not earn the distance. It was handed to me. I have carried the bear my whole life and never once met it.
