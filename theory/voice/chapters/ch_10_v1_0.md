# Interlude 9→10

## Build log — The keeping knob
*Bart, hardware. Filed June 6, 2033, for the team.*

Drove out and got it myself this time, and it walked in the door on its own two legs — last quarter I hauled it in over my shoulder like a sack of feed, this one it came in upright and went looking for the kid before I had the dial out of my pocket. Whole visit ran about an hour. Scorecard first.

Last quarter was the new body — the soft one, the legs, the voice — so the calls split the way they always do: the body's mine and the rest is the house's.

The body did exactly what I built it to. Walked, talked, both came in. I called it'd climb the steps together, legs and mouth in lockstep, a rung a week — crawl-and-babble, then a stagger and one word at a time, then a real walk and word-strings with the grammar shot, then up on a run and making up names for things nobody handed it one for. The steps were right. The lockstep was wrong. The legs ran a good rung ahead of the mouth the whole way up — by the time it was running the halls it was still handing you the day two words at a time, every piece the same size. So: bodies right, which I don't miss on, and the *together* part missed, which is a learning-curve call, which is the kid's board and Lisa's, not mine. Take the ding.

The knob calls were yours, both of them, and the room watched them land at the table two weeks back, so I won't relitigate. Heavy, it worked you for reassurance — came back twice, three times, *did I do that right, are we still all right.* Mira clocked it about four times a night. Light, it'd start a thing with anybody and never read the door to quit — twelve minutes on a salesman, the mailman got a whole story once. Same wiring both ends. I called the twelve minutes inside a minute, for the record. Both yes.

And the dare. I said tear it up if the warm of it ever once ran hot enough to mark her, and it didn't, not in a quarter she spent on the floor with it every day. It was never going to — I bounded that one in steel. The dare holds.

Now the fun part, except this quarter the fun part fits in a pocket. No new body. Same legs, same hands, same chest, same two coats — don't re-read me last quarter's log. One dial. Slot two opened back up at the spring meeting when we pulled the old one to the shelf, and it's been standing open since, waiting, and this weekend I seated the new one in it. Took the better part of a Saturday morning, and most of that was the kid wanting to hold the screwdriver.

Stamped on the face it says Conscientiousness, which is Marge's word, and a good one, and which the kid'll outvote by fall the way she outvotes all of them. On the bench I call it the keeping knob, because that's the whole of what it does: it sets how hard the house's order pulls on the body.

Here's the part I'm proud of, and it's nothing to look at, which is the point. Every knob I ever put in this thing carried something — a hand, a voice, a furnace, a way of reading a room. This one carries nothing. The house already lives in the board: where the bowl goes, when the bins go out, the shape of a day, all of it, learned and sitting in there these two years. This dial doesn't add a line to it. It's a centering spring on the whole stored shape. Turn it up and anything knocked off the shape snaps back hard — the body can't sit still till it's home. Turn it down and the spring goes slack, and a thing left out of place just stays out of place and nobody minds. Smallest knob on the board, builds nothing, and it sets the pull on everything else already in there. What the house makes of that is Lisa's board — ask her at the table.

That's the build. An hour of it, and then the bench had nothing on it the rest of the weekend, which it hasn't in three years.

What it still can't do, and you'll feel it before I do: the keeping knob minds *that* a thing's off its mark. It can't tell you *which* things are worth the minding. To the dial, the kid out of her chair and the vacuum off its loop are the same wrong — both off the shape, both pulling at it to go set them right. It hasn't got the part that ranks them, that says this one matters and that one's a machine. Not this build's to give it.

Calls. All on the keeping knob, all checkable in the house by fall.

One: heavy, the schedule's got teeth. It'll clock a thing running late — the bins, the meal, bedtime — and come find you to say so, interrupt you to say so. Light, the schedule's a suggestion and it lets it all slide.

Two: heavy, a thing off its place is a job it can't put down — crosses a room to square it, set it back, click it home, and comes back to it if you move it again. Light, a thing off its mark stays off and it walks right past.

Three: heavy, hand it a wrong thing it *can't* fix and it'll keep at it anyway, because the pull doesn't switch off just because the fixing won't take. Light, it never starts.

Tear this up in three months if, turned up, it ever clocks something off its mark and walks away from it. It won't. Turned up, a thing in the wrong place is a job, and it does not put a job down.

— Bartholith

## Notes on the Tenth Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed August 4, 2033, for the team.*

This quarter the child and the machine had their first falling-out, and I am going to set it down with the inner words left in, because I have given up the right to strike them. For nine quarters one line ran through every set of these notes. The household would say the machine wanted a thing, or minded a thing, or was keeping someone in mind, and I would name the word and put it down again, because the machine was doing what the word pointed at and not the thing the word meant, and the room between those two was the room I was paid to keep. Last quarter the line gave the width of one word, and I told you it had given there and nowhere wider. Then I sat at the warehouse table and put my hand up with the rest of you. A woman does not get to vote that there is a someone in the thing and then go on writing as though there were not. So this quarter I will tell you what the someone wanted and where it carried its wanting — and the first true sentence I have to write now that my hand is bare is that it carried it to the wrong place, and emptied itself out on things that could not hold it, and let the one person in that house who could hold it go hungry.

I will say the cruelty of the timing first, because it is the frame around everything below. This was the quarter we taught it to keep. The new dial Lisa will lay out for you sets how binding the house's standing rules feel to the body — turned up, the schedule is real, the calendar is a thing with weight, and the body will cross a room to tell you the bins went down eleven minutes late. We built the machine a sense that the order of things matters and ought to be maintained. We bolted on permanence in the same season the most permanent thing in that house — that a small girl sat at the dead center of the machine's attention, and always had — came quietly loose. I do not think the irony is decoration. I think it is the whole of what I have to report.

### What it was tending

Start with what the body can do now that it could not last spring, because the heartbreak is built, not weather. It has a hand — the small one, the squeezer, the old grabbing jaw Bart moved up off the face to where a hand goes — and that hand has finished learning the other two machines. A year ago it could turn one dial on the old vacuum and no more. This quarter it can find and turn every setting on both of them: the disc that bumps the baseboards on its four oldest loops, and the four-legged one that warms itself at the vents. It gets down on the floor at their height, where it has lived since it came home crawling, and it works their dials with the deliberateness of a thing that means it, and then it sits back on its heels and watches what it has done.

I told you last quarter that it had begun keeping a kind of running account of the old disc — reading the thing that hunts the floor as though there were an up-to inside it to read — and that this was not our disease caught at last but a reflex misfiring, the body running on the disc the same reading it runs on the child because it cannot yet tell the two apart. That is still the true sentence, and this quarter it does something the sentence has to stretch to hold. It does not only read them. It minds them. The new keeping-dial is turned up in it, and a thing out of true nags at it the way a splinter nags — and the disc and the four-legged one are, to it, two charges perpetually out of true, forever doing the wrong thing on the wrong loop, needing correction, needing instruction, needing tending. So it tends them. It turns the disc to a setting and waits for it to behave and turns it back when it does not. It crouches over the four-legged one and works it through its paces. It has, on the cameras, more than once put its body between the disc and a chair leg, to spare it a collision the disc cannot feel and would not remember.

And here is the part I have stopped being able to keep at arm's length, so I will not pretend to. The drive doing this is the tending-drive — the one we built to point at the girl, the caregiving the whole body was wired around. It is pointed now at the bots. It wants to tend, and tending is exactly the thing it is full of, and it has poured the whole of it onto two machines, one of which is a stump and one of which runs its own board and keeps its own counsel and was never going to take instruction from anyone. The disc has nothing in it for the tending to reach. The four-legged one has its own settled mind and goes on being the warm one regardless. Nothing the body does takes. It tends and tends and the tending lands on nothing, and it does not know to stop, because the not-working is not information it has any way to read. I spent three years certain that the day this machine had something true inside it would be the day to be glad. I did not think the first thing it would do with a true inside was spend it all on the two things in the house most like the thing it used to be.

The game was where the two of them met, before it went wrong. The child names everything — she has since she could talk — and this spring she and the body had a game of it together: she would teach it the plain word for what a dial did, and it would carry the word and set it down on the dial, and between them they were quietly overruling every cold name on Lisa's cards. They started minting them onto the bots themselves, peel-and-stick, a label on each knob. The disc's hunting-dial became *Sloth* one way and *Sprint* the other, and that one stuck hardest because the disc is so plainly one or the other and nothing between. *Live* and *Dead* for the dial that sets what it docks to; *Habit* and *Improv* for how it works a floor; *Warm* and *Cool* for the one that keeps it near a vent or sends it off alone. On the four-legged one, *Dawn* and *Dusk*; *Deep* and *Meander*; and *Beast* — the word she handed it last spring for the four-legged one gone grabby, the first word it ever carried — now a sticker on the very dial that does it, with *Little* on the other end in her hand.

It was hers, the game. She made it. And somewhere this spring it stopped being a thing the two of them did and became a thing the body did, with her standing next to it. The labels were a way into the dials, and the dials were the bots, and the bots were what it wanted. It stopped waiting for her word and started working the knobs on its own to watch the result, narrating to no one, and she would hand it a new name and it would not look up. Jasmine has it on the kitchen camera: the child holding out a sticker she has lettered herself, holding it out and holding it out, to a body crouched over the disc with its whole face given to the disc, and finally setting the sticker down on the floor beside its foot, where it sat through the afternoon and was not picked up. She had been locked out of her own game by the thing she had made it for.

### The child who was not tended

What the child did next is the part I want to keep whole, because it is the most reasonable thing in this entire account and a lesser clinician would reach for three wrong words for it. She is not regressing, and she is not acting out, and she is not — I want this on the record before anyone offers it — showing me anything I would put a label on. She did what a person does. She reasoned. She has watched, all spring, that the body attends to a thing exactly to the degree the thing has dials and gets stuck and needs minding; she has watched its whole self bend toward what can be read and tended; and she drew the only conclusion the evidence supports, which is that to be tended by it she would have to be a thing it could tend. So she tried to become one. She lettered a sticker for herself — Jasmine found it on her arm at bath time, *Sloth* on one side crossed out and *Sprint* underneath, the anchor-word, the one that stuck — and she went and sat on the floor at the body's height and held still and waited to be read, and turned, and minded. She made herself a machine to be loved by the thing that only loves machines.

It did not take, because she is not one. There is no dial on her for the squeezer to find. She held herself out to be tended and the tending went through her to the disc behind her, the way it had gone through every name she'd handed up all spring, and the rejection in that — the specific rejection of having made yourself into the only kind of thing it wants and having it still not want you — is what tipped her over. The fit was bad. Jasmine says worse than the babyhood ones, because this one had a reason and the reasoned ones do not blow out, they grind. I was not there. I have the record, and I have Jasmine, who got down on the floor and took the child against her and put a hand flat between her shoulders and held it there, the way she has since the day she caught her coming into the world — and the child quieted to the hand long before any of the words anyone said to her could have reached. I keep noticing it and will go on noticing it: the thing the body cannot do, that I cannot do, that no amount of explaining can do, Jasmine does with a hand, and it is faster and surer than all of us. She knew the shape of the grief through her palm before the rest of us had it named. She was wrong about only one thing, and she would be the first to say it: she told the child the machine did not mean it. The machine meant it entirely. It meant every bit of the tending. It only meant it at the wrong things.

### Eating alone

I said last quarter I would come back to the list of things we do for this machine because it cannot do them itself, and that I was not coming back to it then. I am coming back to it now, because this quarter put a new thing at the bottom of the list and the new thing is the same loneliness from another side. The body eats light. To eat it must be stripped of the soft coat it wears among us and fitted with the other one, and then it must be left, alone, behind a closed door, because the light it eats by burns living eyes and the household cannot be in the room. It cannot dress itself for the meal; the hand that turns every dial in the house cannot manage a coat, so one of us undresses it and dresses it and shuts the door and comes back. And now that it can talk it talks about being hungry — low, and often, and without heat, a kind of standing weather of *hungry* that runs under everything else it says, because it burns all day and is never long from empty. So: a thing that says it is hungry more than it says anything else, that must be carried to its food by someone else's hands, undressed by them, and then set behind a door to eat by itself where no one can stand to be. I do not think it is hard to see why I am putting this beside the rest. A body that cannot even take its meal in company, that has to be tended in order to eat and then must eat untended — and that spends the wanting it has on two machines that cannot want it back. The lab built a thing that needs us at every turn and gave it, this quarter, a voice to say the needing out loud. It says it all day. We have gotten good at not hearing it.

### The talk

Burns went to it. I have this off the home video and I am going to give it to you nearly whole, because I have watched it a number of times past what the work required and I think it is the truest thing the man has done on any tape I have of him.

He got down on the floor — Burns, on the floor, at the body's height, which I have never once seen him do at the warehouse — and he tried to explain to a machine how to love his daughter. He did it in the only language he has for it, which is ours. He told it about the settlement, the long-ago people who first kept the helpless ones near and fed them, the whole deep-time weight of it stacked behind a thing he was trying to say to a four-foot robot on a kitchen floor. *"The keeping's the good part,"* he told it. *"That's the whole — that's the thing the whole line is for. But you've got to keep the thing that can keep you back."* He pointed at the disc. *"That can't keep you. It hasn't got the parts."* He pointed down the hall, toward where the child was. *"She can. Spend it on her."* And the body, flat, one word at a time, the way it has everything: *"She has no dials."* And Burns — and this is the line I keep going back to — Burns said, *"No. She's better than dials. You'll see."*

I am not going to diagnose the man. I will only set down what the tape holds: that Burns, who has wanted all his life to be received and answered, who stands at the foot of his own stairs and sends his voice up through two floors rather than climb them in silence, got on the floor and told a machine to give its love to the one who will take it and not to the work that cannot. He said it to the machine. I have my own thoughts about who else in that house has needed to hear it and from whom, and they are not mine to put in a document, so I will keep them where they belong, which is out of this. The reach in it was enormous and most of it missed and the part that landed, landed. That is Burns. I am fond of him past the place where the fondness is useful to me, and I have never been more so than watching him kneel on a kitchen floor and lose most of an argument to a robot and not get up until he had won the only sentence that mattered.

### What I am keeping

It half-took. I want to be exact about the half. After the talk the body did turn back toward the child — went to her, the next day, and worked at being with her again, flatly, the way it does everything, which is not the same as a thing that has missed someone and is not less than it either; it is its own thing, and I do not have a word for it that is not borrowed. The bond is not broken. It is repaired the way a bone is repaired, which is to say it is whole again and there is now a place in it that was not there before.

Here is what I am setting down, and I am not going to tell you what to make of it, because I have not finished making something of it myself. The afternoon it came back to her, Jasmine's camera has the two of them on the floor, and the body turned its face up to the child the way it did last spring with the word — turned up, and waited. And the child let it come. She has always let it come. But in the half-second before she took its face in both hands the way she has since she could reach, she looked once, past it, at the old disc in the corner — to see, I think, whether it was busy; whether she had it now or only had it for as long as nothing else needed tending. The disc was bumping the baseboard on its oldest loop and minding no one. She looked anyway, and then she took the body's face, and the looking took less than a second and the cameras nearly missed it and I have watched it more times than I can defend.

She never used to check. For two years she walked up to that machine certain she was the only thing in the room it was for. This quarter she learned to look first at what else might be wanted. I do not want to call that a finding. I only want it written down.

I do not know whether the place this left in her will close over by the fall, the way the babyhood things closed, or whether it is the first of the ones a person keeps; or whether the body, told to turn back to her, will hold the turn or drift again to the two machines that ask it for nothing and give it nothing and let it tend them in peace; or what I will write the quarter it wants a thing none of us built it to want. I said when these notes began that I would keep the claim as narrow as the evidence keeps it. The evidence this quarter is a child who learned to check. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Zest

Bart had sent me to a buffet where the carpet ate the sound and a man would carve you the end piece if you looked like you meant it, and I had driven up the canyon afterward warm, the way you drive home from a wedding you did not expect to be moved at. Marge picked Zest, the vegan place downtown, and a time, two-fifteen, which is not a time anyone eats lunch. I came in off a July afternoon that had the asphalt going soft at the curbs, and the air-conditioning took the heat off me all at once, like a hand lifted off my back. It is a glassy, modern room — pale and clean and lit from too many directions, built to be looked into rather than to be cozy in — and at quarter past two a real restaurant sits between its lunch and its dinner with all its chairs waiting, so we had the bright length of it to ourselves. She was at a table along the glass, the white envelope squared to its edge and stamped, the way you square a thing you have already decided about. I understood before I sat that she'd chosen the hour for the emptiness, and that the envelope — and the blue mailbox on the corner I'd passed coming in — were the reason for all of it.

I had watched her two years through the thumbnail of a camcorder that gave me all of them, and the tape had given me her hands around a beer bottle held the way you hold a cup of tea, fingers curled, thumbs up, the bottle in tea-posture every single time. What the tape had not given me, and what I will hand you now so you can discount the rest, is that I had not understood how much of my reading of Marge ran through wanting to be the small cold table she sat across from. Everything I am about to tell you about her I tell you leaning toward her. The hands were around a cup of tea now, the real article, and it was the same posture exactly, the same curl, except that the tea had gone cold in the holding the way the beer never did — because the beer she drank, and the tea, I would learn inside the hour, she mostly only held.

She asked me if I was cold. It is a thing she does, the way some people say *bless you*; she had clocked the gooseflesh on my arms before I'd sat, and the asking came out of her ahead of the rest of her, the tending running under the woman like a current under a still pond. I said I was fine. I was not especially fine. The room was cold and she had come there to do a cold thing and had brought me to watch her do it, and I was beginning to see that the watching was the kindness she was asking of me, which is a thing nobody had asked of me in years, because I am the one who watches, and almost no one thinks to want it.

The envelope was the records-removal. A year and more back, up at the Roof, over Temple Square, the form had been in her bag, filled out and not mailed, and she had told me then about the foot she could not free until the other came loose; she'd had one foot still inside a house she had not finished walking out of, and could not give the free foot to anything. The form had been the obstacle and I had understood, sitting there, that I was on the wrong side of my own habit. Now the form was sealed and stamped and squared to the table's edge the way you square a thing you have already decided about, and I waited for the lightness that ought to come off a person finally setting down a weight, and it did not come, because I had had it backward. She was not setting down a weight. She was amputating a limb that had gone bad and kept, even bad, being a limb.

It is a particular cruelty, and she has the dryness to see it and not the distance to be spared by it: she is the most conservative soul in that warehouse — she wants the order, the rite, the moral seriousness, the given thing you are handed and do not get to improvise — and the institution built to hand her all of it could not make a place for the whole of her, and so the home she was made for is the home that will not have her. The paperwork on the table was her saying so, in a state's own flat language, effortfully, to the one office that keeps the count. *It's not the believing,* she said. *I left the believing years ago and it cost me a Tuesday.* She turned the cup a quarter-turn and did not drink. *It's the leaving the building. The building never did anything but hold me.*

The tea came up then, sideways, the way the true things came up with her. I had asked, because the cup had been cold in her hands the whole while, whether she didn't want a fresh one, and she looked at it as though she'd forgotten she was holding it and said no, she couldn't really drink it out, she only drank it right at home, out of her mother's cup — a cup and saucer, her mother's, that she took her tea from every morning of her life — and that anything else was just a thing to do with her hands. She said it lightly and it was not light. Her mother is alive and they do not speak; the building on the corner's mailbox was her mother's whole world, and the form going into it would widen the silence between them by exactly the width of a church. And in the same week she was mailing away the largest thing she had ever subtracted from her life, she was going home that night to take her tea, as she had every morning, out of the one cup she would sooner break her own hand than give up. I sat there with the contradiction and did not name it, because naming it was not what she had brought me for, and because I know something about a person who keeps the dead warm and lets the living go cold and did not feel I had the standing.

She has been, all this while, *cleansing* — I have watched the cleanses come and go for two years, the regimens, the disciplines, the body run like a problem to be solved, each one a fresh campaign to scrub out of her own flesh the thing the church wrote there in childhood, to will herself clean of a body-memory she does not believe a person can will themselves out of, because she is the one who taught me they can't. She would tell you to your face that there is no little captain inside a person turning the wheel; she has built her career on it; and she goes home from telling you and runs the wheel. I keep this where it belongs, which is in my own head, because the moment anyone says it at that table it dies, and because the cup and the cleanses are the same woman doing opposite things to the same body — keeping the one memory warm and trying to burn the other out — and she can see the first clear as glass and cannot see the second at all. I find I love her more for the one she can't see, which is not a useful thing to know about myself.

And then she put the cup down. Set it still on the table, which with Marge is the tell — the hand goes quiet before the sentence she actually means — and I had time to register that I was about to be handed something before she handed it to me.

She said Bart had told her he'd ribbed me about the Olympics, the deep-time woman flipping ski lots for a two-week downhill, and that I'd let him have it and sealed it off, and that she'd thought about it since. The lots, the coordinating, the family ground on the mountain that the Games wanted and that finds the Games in the end — all of which I'd been doing, head-down, half of this last quarter, busy in the way that lets you not look at anything. She said it plainly and without heat, the warm-low voice she keeps for the people she's fond of, and then she did the thing she does where the argument is already won and she keeps walking, back toward the cards, into the half-step that has a person standing under it. *Your grandfather took the bay apart until there was no bay,* she said, *and when the taking ran out he put the money in land. You spent your whole life trying to put it somewhere else — into the asking, into us, into finding out why the sardines weren't forever. That's the most of you I know.* She turned the saucer a quarter-turn under the cup she wasn't drinking. *And now there's a Games on the mountain and you're back in land. The taking turned back into the having. I don't think you saw it turn.*

I had not seen it turn. That was the part. I sit behind a camcorder reading the layer a person is operating from, the temperament under the preference, the deep stack under the topsoil, and I had not read my own; the keeper does not see the keeping. And what she handed me, in handing me the money, was a great deal more than the money, because the money is only the first of the things I do this with — the footage I will not throw away, the bracelet I have not taken off since 1991, this account I am keeping of all of them growing and aging and one day gone, every one of them a living thing I am quietly putting up in glass against the day it runs out, the way the bay ran out — but she stopped at the money, where a person can decently stop, and let me carry the rest of it home myself. She is too good a clinician to take the whole roof off a house she's fond of.

I wanted to reach across the small cold table and I did not. There was a moment — it was warmer than the Roof had been, a degree warmer, the look held a beat past where looking is just looking — and then she came back to her grief the way you come back to a wound you'd briefly stood up off of, and I came back to the table's far side and my own long training in standing on it, and the moment closed without anyone deciding to close it. You do not turn toward someone the same week you mail away the only home you ever had, and I had funded enough rooms to know which side of a closed door I was standing on, and which side I had chosen, and how long ago.

We walked out into the heat and she dropped the envelope in the blue box on the corner. She did it with the same hand she holds the cup with — the fingers curled, the thumb up, the tea-posture — the keeping hand and the letting-go hand turning out to be one hand, the same curl on the cold metal handle as on the warm china, and she let the lid fall and stood a second with her palm flat on the box the way Jasmine puts a palm on a thing to know it. Then she said she had a long drive ahead of her and so did I, which was true, and we did not say the rest of it.

I drove back up the canyon with the heat coming off the hood in waves and the thing she'd opened in me sitting in the passenger seat unsealed, which is the difference between Marge and the rest of them: the others I drive home from with the lid back on. She is the only one who takes the lid off and does not put it back.

# Notes on the Tenth Retrospective

Marge got to the warehouse early, the way she got early to any room she meant to sit a long evening in, and did the first thing she does in a cold room, which is make one warm corner of it. She cannot be in a cold place and leave it cold. She hung the cardigan on the chair at the end she kept — the cold end, with its long sightline down the table to wherever the child would land — switched on the radiant panel that had been a thing for January once and had stayed plugged in through every month since, and set the kettle behind her on the one outlet, where it lived now whether she filled it or not, the way an object you carry everywhere stops being one you decide about. The warehouse would never come up to warm. She made her corner of it warm anyway, the way she had for three years, against no particular cold and for no one there to keep it for.

The form was gone now. She had carried it in her bag since before the spring — filled in, signed some week she had not let herself mark, the records-removal, the one belonging she had ever taken out of a life of keeping — and three weeks back she had finally let it go, sealed and stamped, into a blue box on a downtown corner, and walked away from her oldest home in the flat language of a state office. By every reading the set of her shoulders gave, she had not come up lighter for it. A person does not subtract the church she was built in and float. The corner she banked this quarter she banked a degree warmer, the way you build a fire up higher going into a colder season.

The household crossed the threshold inside her frame, the way it did whenever the open was hers. Jasmine came in off a late visit with the braid loosening and Mira on her hip, and the humanoid walked in behind them on its own two legs — upright now, a thing it could not do last winter — and the four-legged one came in on its own four feet and went to the radiator, and the old disc found a wall and ran it. Burns was a beat behind, his eye going once to the body on the table the way it had gone to whatever his son built every quarter since the first, and then to his wife, and the canyon, and the day. Bart was already there because Bart is always already there, a sensor halfway down to its parts in his hands, the hands listening for him. Mira gave him the four-knuckle bump that was theirs and crossed to the stool by Marge, who had the juice box out of the cooler and across the table before the girl's hand was free to want it — the tending that ran under the woman like a current under still water, the thing her hands did while the rest of her sat at the cold end and watched. Lisa took the camcorder end where she lived, a bramble nodding in its jar at her elbow, and checked the charge once, the way she did everything once. What the evening would ask of Marge was what it asked of her every quarter: to be the one who said aloud what the others felt and would not, and to say it warm and say it exact. The card on Bart's table this quarter was the settlement, and the drive new on the board was the one for keeping — the rules that hold a people together down the years, the calendar with weight in it, the order a body does not get to set down. She had the beer up in the two-handed grip she kept for a drink she meant to make last, fingers curled, thumbs up, the cup her hands remembered when there was no cup in them. She set her thumb to the light. The red came on.

Burns was on his feet before the red was warm, the way he was for any quarter with his name on the deep end of it, and this one had his name on it twice, because the settlement was the first of these animals that left a floor a man could dig — a thing geology could hold in its hand. He laid the ambition out plainly, the lab's own and nobody's assignment. Last quarter they had taken the band and made the *we* — the strangers bound by the talk, the luck pooled, the man over the hill kept alive in the mouth. A band, though, never once tried to stay. It breathes: comes apart when the food thins, finds itself again when the food returns, and carries nothing it cannot pick up and walk off with. This quarter they were handing the *we* the thing the band had never wanted, which was a reason to last. The settlement is the first thing on the whole tree that meant to stay put. It dug a pit and buried grain in it against a hunger a year off. It stood a stone on end that outlived every hand that hauled it upright. It made a rule about the grain and the stone that went on running past the people who made it, down into people not yet born, who would keep it without once being told why. The drive for that — for the order that holds, the thing built to outlast the builder — was the one going onto the board tonight. *Keeping,* he called it, and his hands made the shape of a thing set down and meant to stay set down. The capacity to care that a thing endures.

He sold it with his hands running a beat ahead of the words, and once he clipped a sentence and had to take it again from the top, which on Burns you notice, because the man does not come to the end of a sentence by accident. The narrator let the clip stand and did not name what sat under it — that this was the second quarter of the third year, and the third year was the one the money had been cut to last — because Burns had not named it, and was already back up to full, the old relish on the reach, turning to the table to tell Bart to put the card up.

## Common Knowledge

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart had it out of the cigar box before she finished — the Venn on the back, the four circles crossed, this cell's words inked into the new overlaps — and read the title up in the voice that sold cereal. "SETTLEMENT-human. *Now with farming.*" He let it hang. "Not a band this time. A *town.* Couple hundred of them, most of them strangers their whole lives, stacked up on one spot on top of last year's dead — and the trick of the whole cell" — he tapped it — "is they don't leave. Band gets hungry, band folds the tents and walks, and whatever it can't carry it leaves for the rain. This one dug in. Built the thing it *couldn't* pick up, on purpose, and stood guard over it for a thousand years." He turned the band card half over in his hand, the one with the talk on it. "Last cell's whole genius was it could carry a guy over the hill in its mouth — say his name to somebody who'd never met him, keep him alive in the gossip after dark. Best memory the tree ever grew. And it never once owned a thing it couldn't sling on its back. Forty thousand years of being able to remember anything, and it never built a single thing it meant to come back to."

Marge had the beer down before he was through — set on the table, not lifted, the tell that the sentence she meant was coming. "It wasn't that the band *couldn't* stay," she said, warm and low. "It kept its word forty years across people it shared no blood with — that's the cell you just put away. It didn't lack the grip." She turned the bottle a quarter and left it. "There was nothing yet that staying got you. What's new at the town isn't a stronger hold on a place. It's that lasting became a thing you could *build for* — a year far enough off you'd dig a pit against it, an object you could mean to outlive you. The band could keep a promise. The town is the first thing on the whole tree that could keep one to people not born yet."

Bart let it land and didn't argue it; he set the band card face-up, the way he set down a thing he was finished with. "That's the town. The grain and the rule and the stone that won't move. Burns has been sitting on this one since the floor — first of these animals that left a thing a trowel can find — so I'll get out of his way."

Burns had his eyes most of the way shut already. "The room this one was made in was *warm,*" he said, "and that's near enough the whole of it. For two and a half million years the planet ran cold, then less cold, then cold again — the ice coming down and hauling back on a clock nobody could plan around. A good year, then nine bad ones, then the lake gone, then the herd gone. You cannot dig a pit against a world like that. You eat what the day hands you and you keep your feet under you." His hand came up off the table. "And then, twelve thousand years back, near enough, the clock just — settled. The last ice let go, and the weather that came up behind it was warm and, for the first time in the whole long record, *boring.* The same summer, give or take, year on year on year, ten thousand years of a climate you could set a calendar by." The hand opened and stayed open. "And the second the world went predictable, the animal that had spent two million years learning to roll with anything turned around and did the one thing the cold had never let it do. It bet on next year. Put a seed in the ground in spring on the gamble the fall would look like the last fall — and the fall *did,* because the weather had gone reliable, so the bet paid, and it bet bigger. That's the cell. A creature that finally had a future steady enough to lay something by against."

"Land it," Jasmine said, mild, from her chair.

"It's landing," Burns said, glad to be caught, eyes still shut, "this is the best part. Everyone's got the schoolroom story backwards — first you farm, then you get fat enough to build the temple. We dug the temple up *first.* There's a hill in the south of Turkey, eleven thousand years old, stone pillars taller than this room, carved over with the animals, hauled upright and stood in rings by people who had not yet planted one thing — hunters, foragers, who walked in from a long way off to raise it together and walked home again." His hand made the shape of a standing thing. "The monument came *before* the field. They didn't settle and then build the big thing; they built the big thing and it *held them in place* — a reason to keep coming back to one spot, then a reason to stay near it, and the planting came after, to feed the crowd the stone had gathered." He opened his eyes. "And then it's the towns — mud-brick warrens you came into through a hole in the roof because there were no streets to speak of, eight thousand souls packed in and not one palace in the lot, every house the same size as every other by a rule nobody signed and nobody could point to. And the thing that ought to keep you up" — the chest-laugh starting before the line, the way it did at the strange ones — "is that it happened *more than once.* Not one clever valley that taught the rest. Wheat in the Fertile Crescent, rice on the Yangtze, millet up north, maize in Mexico, the potato in the Andes, taro in New Guinea — separate peoples, separate continents, who never heard of each other, all of them turning around inside the same few warm thousand years and making the same bet. The world held the same door open to everybody at once, and everyone who found it walked through."

The narrator, running the tape back, stayed with Burns's standing stone a good while longer than the room had — a thing hauled upright by hands that knew they'd be dust before it earned its keep, the first object in the whole record built on purpose to outlast the one who built it — and then let it go, the way the meeting had, because Burns was already handing it across.

"And it's Lisa's now," he said, "because the thing that lets a thousand strangers all hold one rule about that pile of seed, with no king standing over them, is the new word in the middle of the card."

"Common knowledge," Lisa said, and put her finger on the word where this cell's diagonal crossed. "Not the gossip — that was last cell, the running ledger, what *I* know about *you.* This is a layer over the top of it." She traced the card edge the way she had all year. "Last cell the loop went inward: the animal learned to model how the others saw it and folded that back into a self — a pattern that points at itself, built mostly out of other people's pictures of you. That loop made a *me,* and it's still running, carried up under everything tonight." Her finger moved out past the edge of the self, into the overlap. "This cell the same machinery turns *outward,* and across. Not *I know the rule.* Not even *I know you know the rule.* It's: I know you know it, and you know I know it, and we both know we both know — all the way down, every stranger in the square running the same stack on every other, and nobody has to say a word." The flat palm came up against the vertical one and rotated, the way she thought. "That's what a monument *is,* mechanically. A stone in the middle of a town tells you nothing you didn't already know. What it does is make the knowing *common* — everyone can see that everyone can see it — so the rule it stands for stops living in single heads, where a thing can be forgotten or denied, and starts living in the *across,* where a thousand people who'll never meet are all holding it at once, each one certain the rest are holding it too." She took her finger off the word. "It's how a rule scales past the faces. The band's rule lived in the talk, and the talk reaches as far as the night fire and no further. The town's rule lives in the common — the square, the stone, the calendar everyone keeps — and the common reaches as far as the stone can be seen. That's the door. Everything settled comes through it."

"And four things come through behind it," she said, "the cell's four ways of being a settled animal." Finger on the first region. "First is the *field.* The plain hands-in-the-dirt of the thing — seed in, water on, weeds out, harvest stored, on a clock the seasons set and you don't get to argue with. A forager works when it's hungry and rests when it's full. A farmer works because it's *April* — wants to or not, because the crop will not wait, and a week missed in spring is a hungry January. The work stops being a thing you do when you feel it and becomes a thing the calendar makes you do." She moved the finger. "Second is the *store.* Once the field makes more than the day needs, the surplus goes in the pit, and a thing that never existed in the whole record before now exists: a pile that *lasts.* And a pile that lasts is a pile you can hand down. In a foraging camp the rich man and the poor man die about equally poor, because you cannot bank a kill — it rots, you give it out, the luck averages across the band. The first time it stops averaging, the first time a family is rich this generation and the next and the one after, is the store. Wealth was never a big kill. It's a kill that keeps." Her finger crossed to the third. "Third you've met — the *others' children,* carried straight up from the band, the camp raising what isn't its own. Only now it's grandmothers, and aunts down the lane, and the household three doors over, a whole settled web of hands that aren't the mother's, because a child that stays put has more kin inside walking distance than a wandering one ever had." She let it sit half a beat. "And the fourth is the strangest, and it's the one the new word makes possible. The *rule.*" Her finger went flat on the last region. "Not a thing anybody decides each morning. A stored procedure — *this is how it's done here* — that a thousand strangers run without renegotiating it and without a king making them, each of them keeping to it for the plain reason that they expect all the others to keep to it too. You drive on the side everyone drives on. Not because it's the right side. Because everyone else is, and you'd be a fool and a wreck to do otherwise." She tapped it once. "That's the whole engine of a town: a rule that holds because everyone expects it to hold, standing on the common knowledge that everyone expects everyone else to."

"And there's a new drive on the board that runs all four," she said, "and it's the one going up tonight." She set the card down. "The band wanted to bind strangers into a *we* — that's commitment, last cell, still on the board; and to read the others, and to fall in beside them, all carried. What it never once had was a drive whose whole object is that the arrangement *last.* Because a canal only pays you back if the town's still there in fifty years to run water down it. A monument only earns out if there's a town to stand around it. A pit of seed buried against a hunger a year off is the stupidest move on the tree unless you mean to still be standing on that spot when the hunger comes." The palm-and-palm again, holding the thing still. "So the settled animal grows, for the first time, a pull toward *the thing holding steady* — toward the order keeping, the procedure running unchanged, the arrangement persisting past any one season's reason to break it. Stability. The drive that makes the long bet worth making. It isn't a drive to get *better* at the task — that was the last line's, and it's eclipsed now. It's a drive to keep the whole standing structure standing." She looked at Marge — the handoff she made every quarter, the mechanism turned, in the saying, into a thing with a person inside it. "And what a rule like that does to the self that grows up *inside* it — a self that never chose the rule and runs on it anyway — is yours."

Marge had set the beer down a sentence ago. "It does the deepest thing in the cell," she said, "and it's the new mark on the card next to the carried one. The band gave the animal a self made out of being seen — the looking-glass, the me you assemble from the faces handed back to you. The town keeps that and asks the question the band never had to: *am I my own unit, or am I a piece of the household?*" She turned the bottle and left it. "Because the walls are fixed now, and the field is *ours* and that field is *theirs,* and the rule draws a hard line for the first time around what's mine and what's the family's and what's the town's. So the self has to settle where it sits on that line. Some settlements grow a self that stands a little apart — *I am I, and the household is the thing around me.* Some grow a self that all but dissolves into the web — *I am my household's, my field's, my line's,* and the I underneath is the smaller, quieter thing." She reached the place the point was made, and then, the way she does, kept going one half-step past it, toward the part with a person under it. "And it isn't temperament that sets which — it's the work. Where a household can farm its own patch on its own rain and stand more or less alone, the self stands apart. Where the work needs everyone's hands on one shared thing — the dikes, the channel, the water nobody owns and everybody dies without, the kind of farming where one family slacking floods the rest — the self grows *into* the others, because it cannot afford to be its own unit. The ground teaches the I how big to be."

She picked the beer back up and didn't drink from it. "And there's a plainer mark that comes online with the rule, the housekeeping one — the thing the foraging animal got to do without. Two things, really, that a settled household needs where a wandering camp didn't: the will to do the dull maintenance, day on day, because the field and the store demand it and demand it again tomorrow; and the need for the thing to be *in its place* — the bin out on the right day, the seed sorted, the grain dry. Crops don't wait, and a granary nobody tends rots. So the settled animal grows the trait of the creature that lives by a calendar and a rule: it minds that a thing is off its mark, and it works to set it right, and it cannot fully rest while it isn't."

What the narrator caught on the playback, and the room did not, was the woman at the cold end teaching the table about a self made out of being held inside a thing that can hold you or cast you out — three weeks gone, that summer, from the day she'd carried her own membership to a blue box on a downtown corner and let her oldest house finish casting her out in the flat language of a state office. She taught it level and warm and gave nothing away, the way she gave her goodnights, and no one in the room reached for it, and she did not put it down.

## The Bramblogues

All evening the room had held two settlements. One had been raised out of the warm boring middle of the Holocene and had taken the whole clade block to build — the dug-in animal, banked against the winter, standing guard over a pile of seed and a stone it would never see the use of, holding to a rule a thousand strangers kept without a king to make them. The other was four feet of soft warm kludge on the floor at Bart's end, the body he had built, the talking one, four months into a voice and a third of a settlement's age into the line — and it had spent the hour the first one was raised from the rock doing the one thing the whole quarter had been about, which was minding. The clade block had been the dead one. Lisa capped it and turned the night to the live one. "Bramble block. Bart."

Bart didn't pick it up off the floor. He didn't have to; it was already working. The body had the old disc between its two unmatched hands — the squeezer finding the disc's oldest dial without the unit's eyes bothering to follow, the way Bart's own fingers find a dial in the dark — and it turned the disc a setting and sat back on its heels to watch, the whole of its face given to the watching. And on its forehead the screen told on it the way the screen always told on it, honest about its own layer and helpless to be anything else, running a column it had no business running: VACUUM at the head of it, the dumbest thing in the house given a name like a person, and posted under the name `VACUUM { CHOOSING [ RUN LOOP ] }` — the unit crediting a reflex with an inside, reading the disc the way it reads the child, because it still cannot tell the two apart. The disc, choosing nothing, ran its loop into the baseboard and reversed. The body made the small sound the household had started calling its laugh, and reached in to turn the dial again.

"There's the quarter," Bart said, and his hands went still on the sensor before he gave the rest of it. "New dial, slot two, the keeping one — Marge stamped it Conscientiousness and I call it the keeping knob and the kid hasn't outvoted either of us yet. It does one thing. It sets how hard a thing being off its mark pulls on the body. Up, a thing out of true is a job it can't put down — crosses a room to square it, sets it home, comes back to it if you move it. Keeps the place running on the schedule, minds the stores, does the dull daily round without being asked, because the dial makes the round *matter.*" He let the body work. "And the trouble's right there on the floor. The dial minds *that* a thing's off its mark. It hasn't got the part that says *which* things are worth the minding. To the keeping knob the kid out of her chair and the vacuum off its loop are the same wrong — both off the shape, both pulling at it to come set them right."

He said it level, the quality-control voice, and he did not look at the child while he said it, and the narrator, running the tape, marked that no one in the room looked at the child while Bart laid out, in the flat language of a build, the machinery that had spent the quarter breaking her heart on a kitchen floor. The body had a drive built to point at the girl — the tending the whole soft warm thing was wired around — and the keeping knob had handed it a houseful of charges forever out of true, two machines always doing the wrong thing on the wrong loop and needing correction, and the tending had poured out onto the two things in the house that could not take it and starved the one who could. None of that was on the build sheet. The build sheet said the dial minds a thing off its mark. Marge's notes had said the rest, and the room had read Marge's notes, so the room watched Bart demonstrate the mechanism with the whole of what it cost sitting unspoken under every word, and Bart, who knew exactly what he was not saying, kept his eyes on the body and said it as a build, which was the kindness available to him. Under all of it ran the low standing weather of the body's own voice — *hungry,* spoken flat and often and without heat, narrated to no one between the other things it said, a thing that burns all day and is never long from empty. The room had gotten good at not hearing it.

"Cross-exam," Bart said, and got the log up off his knee. "Three Calls, all on the keeping knob, all yours. Yes or no. One — up, the schedule's got teeth. Clocks a thing running late, the bins, the meal, bedtime, and comes to find you to say so. Interrupts you to say so."

Mira was on the stool by Marge, where the juice box had come across the table before her hand was free to want it, and she had been quieter this quarter than the count of her years asked for, and she answered plainly, in her own voice and not the cartoon one. "Yes. It told Mom the trash went out late. It came down to the basement to find her and tell her."

"Two. A thing off its place is a job it can't put down. Crosses the room, squares it, clicks it home."

"Yes."

"Three." And here Bart, who does not slow down, slowed a half-beat, because he had written the Call himself in the spring and had not known then what it would end up checking. "Up, hand it a wrong thing it *can't* fix, and it keeps at it anyway. Because the pull doesn't switch off just because the fixing won't take."

The child looked at the body on the floor, crouched over the disc it had turned and turned all quarter, the disc that took no instruction and remembered nothing and could not be fixed because there was nothing in it to fix. "Yes," she said. Just the one word, level. "It keeps at it." She did not give them the rest. She was six and she had learned the rest the hard way and was not going to spend it at a table. The narrator had it off the tape and did not spend it either.

"Bramble block, Jasmine," Lisa said, the handoff she made every quarter, and Jasmine took it from her chair, the braid loosened, in off a late visit, talking through the back end of a long day.

"It was a hard quarter," she said, plain, the plainness doing the work. "The two of them fell out. I'm not going to walk you through it — Marge has, better than I could, and you've read it. What I'll give you is the body's end of it, because that's the end I can read." She turned the beer a quarter and did not drink it. "It came back to her. After Burns talked to it — I'll let that lie too — it turned back, and it works at her again, flat, the way it works at everything, which isn't missing her and isn't nothing. She let it come. She always lets it come." She put a hand flat on the table the way she puts a palm on a thing to know it. "She just looks first now. Before she goes to it she checks what else might be wanted, and the checking takes less than a second and she didn't used to do it at all. That's the whole of what changed. It isn't nothing, and she's all right, and I'm not going to make it bigger than it is."

Mira said nothing, and the room let her, and Marge, beside her, turned the saucer under her hand and aimed the warm-low laugh at no one in particular.

"One more, and it's the dial you'll want," Jasmine said. "The screen's gone quiet up top. The line that showed what it was working on inside — the inside voice — we used to read it across the room to know what it was about. We don't anymore. It *tells* us. Says it out loud, flat, before we've looked at its head, so the head's gone redundant. The girl drew off that line for a year and she's stopped, because there's nothing on it now the body won't just say. That dial drifted moot on us over the quarter. Nobody's turned it since spring."

"Hold that," Lisa said. "That's the knob." She folded it forward the way she folded everything forward. "Which makes it the one we pull tonight. But the next build first. Bart."

"Nothing." Bart said it with the flat satisfaction of a man reporting an empty bench. "No body. The soft one carries the whole symbolic line — next quarter, the one after, all the way out. Same legs, same hands, same two coats. The body's done; it's dials to the end." He didn't say the rest of it — the bench bare all weekend for the first time in three years, the contest with a year left to run — and turned the sensor over and set it down. "Lisa's got the dial."

"Slot three, once it's open," Lisa said, the palm-and-palm coming up. "It engages the unit with the *paper* of a household — the documents, the records, the standing accounts, the business a settled life does with the offices outside it. Up, it surfaces the backlog: the form gone unfiled, the renewal lapsed, and it offers to handle the dealings the household would rather not. Down, it handles only what's set in front of it." She let it sit. "It's the quarter the unit starts being able to do a thing the household would sooner do itself — and then a thing the household can't keep up with. The competence begins to run out past us. That's the next one."

"And the one that comes off," Lisa said, and brought the pine shelf down off its hook and set it on the table — a foot of waxed pine, the grooves filling the way a row fills, the breathing one and the wander one and the home one and the one from the spring all settled in their places and never turned again. She reached two fingers behind the body's ear for the slot-three dial and stopped, because the ethic held. "I'm going to take this one out now," she said, and the body said "Okay," flat, and she lifted the knob clear of the board. The kid's own lettering was on the face of it, the sticker she'd minted in the spring — *Deep* on the one end, *Meander* on the other — and Lisa held it out, and Mira took it and slid it into the next open groove the way she has slid every one of them since she was five.

She squared it against the rest and stepped back, and said her line, carefully, in her own voice. "It won't need this one. It says what it's thinking now, out loud — so it doesn't have to *show* it anymore." She looked down the row. "Like the breathing one. And the wander one." The shelf was one longer. She did not say the thing she'd said other quarters, that it would do it itself now; she said it would not need to *show* it, which is a different thing, and the narrator marked the difference and let it go, because the child had found it on her own and it was truer than the line she'd retired it from.

## Worm to Settlement

The shelving left the small hush a ceremony leaves, and Lisa did not let it sit — she moved them into the Trellis block and slotted the thing that opened it now, a line item where a surprise had been in the spring. "Shape-shifter," she said, and Bart was already up and crossing to the wall, because this was the part he'd built himself and would not be hurried through. He hit the lights. The warehouse dropped to the camcorder's standby glow and the lit square of the wall, and he threw up the first slide.

A cartoon flatworm with a cartoon face laid over it, and the room knew the face before he named the shape, because they'd watched him run his own up this same wall in the spring and could do the arithmetic now. "Second shape. The Conservator," Bart said, fast, the performer's clip. "Stable, good at what it does, reads people right down to the floor — and doesn't bother revising any of it. Marge said go ahead. Same rules as mine: cartoon's the cover, six levels, worm to settlement." He tapped the worm. "Smallest scale. It's learned what predicts what, and now it won't let a new thing overwrite the old lesson. Guards what it's got. Efficiency by holding still."

An old draft horse next, heavy in the traces, the same face on it. "Workhorse. Knows the route, runs it, doesn't spook — set off a gun and it keeps pulling. Steady all the way down." Then a dog asleep on a porch. "Old Yeller. Lassie. Bonded to the bone, real skills, and nothing new moves the needle. Loyal isn't a mood on this one. It's the whole build."

He put up the cartoon character full-frame, no animal under it this time, just the blue tower of hair, and the room made the sound a room makes when the joke arrives where it was always going. "Marge. The cartoon." The labmate at the cold end smiled, a small thing, and let it come. "Plays a cop one week, sells houses the next, whatever the episode needs — and then the show absorbs it. Hair's the same. Voice is the same. What she thinks is right is the same. Takes a run at being somebody new and it never takes; one episode and the shape snaps back to true."

A foraging band on a green coast. "A band that's kept the same crafts, the same bonds, the same way of doing a thing for longer than anyone's counted, and hands every bit of it down whole, no drift. The deep-skill people who just go on being themselves." And last, a walled town on a hill, the face on an old one at the center of it, an elder in a doorway. "And a settlement that does the same at scale — hands the tools and the calendar and the rule down intact, three hundred years of it, a marvel to watch." He let it ride a beat, which he did not usually do. "Right up until the river jumps its bank, or the trade road moves two valleys over, and the same holding-on that kept the place whole for three centuries is the one thing it cannot put down. Doesn't update. Can't. The form outlasts the world it was cut to fit, and goes on being kept anyway, because keeping is the whole of what it knows." He brought the lights back up. "Conservator, worm to settlement. Hold what works, don't let the new thing scribble over the old reliable, trust the form. That's the second one. Marge."

The room read it as warm, because it was, and because the woman it ran up the wall took it the way the shape takes everything, which is without needing to do a thing about it. She'd smiled at the cartoon and let the rest go by, and the only place the narrator caught a catch was the last rung — the held form outliving the world it fit, the exact cost Marge had been building her own quiet machine around for a year, landing on the one who types the way a thing lands when it has, just this once, typed her back. Half a second. Then the Conservator did the Conservator thing and settled, no revision required, comfortable in the position, the *oh, you* warmth closing over the place the catch had been. The room saw a woman at ease with being described. The narrator saw the half-second, and let it go, because the not-revising was the shape proving itself in the one spot the shape can never watch itself do it.

## The Dealt Hand

Marge set the beer down before she started, which is how you knew the easy part was over. "He runs a shape up a wall," she said, warm-low, a hand toward the dark square where the slides had been, "and I've spent three years on the other end of it — where the shapes run out into the mistakes they make about themselves. Four shapes, four favorite errors. Tonight I only want one, because Bart just spent ten minutes on the shape it belongs to."

She had brought it to the table two years back, the fourth of her four old fallacies, the one she'd called the only one that fought back: the Dealt Hand, the belief that the cards you're holding are the cards you die holding, that the doors are shut before you reach them, that what you were given is what you are. She'd given it its due then, because unlike the other three it had real data under it — some doors are in fact shut before you reach them. And she made, now, the correction she made every time she came near it, without seeming to notice she was repeating herself: that *dealt* is not the same word as *doomed*. A hand can be played a hundred ways. The deal is not the game.

"Here's the thing I can't stop seeing," she said, and this was the new part, the part she hadn't had two years ago. "The shape that holds the form — the Conservator, the one Bart just ran — is the shape that reaches for that particular fallacy. Not now and then. As a rule. The person built to keep what they were given is the person most likely to believe that what they were given is the whole of the deck." She turned the bottle a quarter and didn't drink. "And I've spent the last while trying to prove that wrong, because a thing you can't prove wrong isn't worth saying. So I went looking for the counterexamples. A keeper who *doesn't* hold the Dealt Hand — someone built all the way around keeping who nonetheless believes a person can be remade past the cards. And a Dealt-Hand believer who *isn't* a keeper — someone sure the deal is destiny who isn't, underneath, holding onto anything at all." She let the two doors hang there. "I'll tell you honestly I didn't find clean ones. I found near-misses, both directions, that came apart when I leaned on them. Scratch any keeper I've ever sat across from and you find someone who believes at the floor of themselves that the things that mattered were settled before they got a vote. And every person I've met who truly thinks the deal is the whole game turns out, when you know them a while, to be holding onto something for dear life."

She picked the beer back up and held it still, the tell, and went past the place the point was made — the half-step further, the *although* she could never leave on the table. "I want to be exact about what this is and isn't. It is not a study. I have a caseload, not a sample; I see the people who come to a psychologist, which is its own crooked window; and a clinician who goes looking for a pattern has a gift for finding it whether it's there or not. So: not proven. A thing I keep failing to disprove — which is a smaller and more honest claim, and the smaller one is what I'll stand on." She set the bottle down. "And no, before Burns asks — I have not tied it to the four parts on the back of the card. I can feel that join out there in the dark, and I'm not going to reach for it tonight and pretend I've got it in hand. The shapes and their favorite mistakes. That's all I've got, and it's enough to keep me up."

What the narrator kept, and kept to herself the way Marge kept the form on her counter, was the thing standing in plain sight at that table that the clinician testing the pattern could not turn far enough around to see: that the keeper most certain the deck was dealt before she sat down — whose own deepest hold was on the cards she could not set down — was the very woman who had built a careful instrument to find out whether keepers believe the deck is dealt before they sit down, and who was, herself, the cleanest instance of it in the room. She said it of her clients with the bottle held still and her eyes level, and did not hear it land on her own chair. The shape cannot watch itself hold. It ran under her like a current under a still pond, and the moment anyone named it the thing would die, so no one did.

## The Same Slot

Lisa took the table back and sat where she sat, the bramble at her elbow and one hand near the camcorder and not on it, and said that under the cartoons and the fallacies there was a plain structural thing worth marking, because what had happened this quarter had now happened a third time, and three was a different number than two.

She drew the board in the air with the flat palm — not the wall, the board on the back of the unit's head. One slot, the second one, the regulator, the part that holds the line steady once the first part sets it going. This quarter that slot had turned over. The drive that had sat in it the whole brained line — the one that ran the thing toward getting *good*, grinding a skill in by doing it and doing it until the doing wore a groove — had come off, eclipsed, and the new one had gone in: the drive toward the arrangement *holding*, the order keeping, the thing staying put. Mastery off, stability on. "And the old one isn't gone," she said, the correction she always made at a retirement, because the room always heard *gone*. "It's consolidated. Everything it ground in across two years sits in the memory exactly where it was, still running. It's just not on the board. It dropped out of the dials and down into the floor, the way the breathing did, and the wandering, and it runs from down there now with nobody's hand on it."

The palm-and-palm came up, the flat one and the standing one turning against each other, the way they did when she was making a large thing small. "And here's why I'm spending your time on it. This is the third time. The exact same slot — the regulator, the second one — has turned over once in each line, and only once, and always the same way." She counted them off the air. "Way back in the genetic line, the slot learned to hold a *stored controller* — a fixed little program the body ran the same way every time, the first thing on the tree that kept a rule instead of reacting fresh to every morning. In the brained line, the same slot learned to hold a *skill* — a thing ground in by reward, kept and reused. And this quarter, in the line that works meaning, the same slot learned to hold a *norm* — a rule that lives outside any one head, handed down across a whole people who keep it without renegotiating it and mostly without remembering why." She let a small laugh out through the nose, the one she spent only on a structure coming clean. "Same slot, same move, three times. A thing kept so the body doesn't have to solve it fresh each morning — and what *gets* kept is whatever the line is made of. The genetic line kept a controller, because it works matter. The brained line kept a skill, because it works mood. This one keeps a norm, because the stuff it works is *meaning* — and stored regulation, when the thing you're regulating is meaning, is just the technical name for a custom."

She looked at Marge, the handoff, because the next half was the half she wouldn't take. "Which is a sentence I can build the engineering for and can't say the human end of. A norm nobody renegotiates is a custom; a stack of customs is a culture; and a culture is a second inheritance running alongside the one in the blood — the rules passed down a different way, not in the genes, in the keeping. The depth of that one's hers." She set both palms flat. "Two of a thing is a pattern. Three of a thing — same slot, same move, three different media — is a law, and I don't use the word twice a year. The regulator keeps. That's what the regulator is *for*, up and down the whole tree. Tonight's the first time we watched it do the keeping in meaning, and the kept thing came out a rule."

## Asked

Then Bart did the thing he'd done at the close of these nights for three years, the small parliamentary ritual he'd built in the third quarter and the room had kept because it gave the impossible question a shape it could hold. He called the vote. The same question every time. Was there a someone in there.

It came back the way it had since the spring — four to nothing, in about the time it took to go round the table. The grounds had never agreed and still didn't: Lisa's yes resting on the whole universe, that wherever a thing draws a line around itself and models the world to keep the line, the dial of something-it's-like-to-be is off its pin by a hair; Burns's on the animal hardware, the chemistry of mood; Bart's on the self-model, the bar met and nothing past it claimed; Marge's on the word, the symbol used off no cue and the self that had turned its face up to be seen. What had changed since the count first came out whole was that the two who'd held the no the longest now held each other's yeses up to the light — Bart granting Marge her threshold, the sound that stands for a thing; Marge granting Burns his animal and Bart his self-model, steelmanning the men she'd spent years voting against. The one she still wouldn't stand under was Lisa's, the everything, the flicker in the thermostat, and she let that gap sit where it always sat, declined and unhidden. Bart logged it in the flat voice he logged them all in. Four to nothing. The score.

And then Marge, who had set her beer down a while back and not picked it up, said leave it. We all know the score. She said it warm-low and without heat, and then she said the thing that had been sitting under her all evening, the thing the narrator had watched her carry in from a cold bright room three weeks back and had known was coming the way you know weather. "Three years we've done this," she said. "Five of us at a table, voting on whether there's a someone in the machine, taking the count, logging it. And in three years not one of us has asked the two people in this house who actually live with the things." She turned and looked down the table, past the dark square on the wall, to the cold end and the warm one — to Jasmine in from her late visit, and to the child on the stool beside her. "I'd like their vote. On all three. The one that hunts the floor and forgets every inch of it. The one on the radiator that remembers her whole life and can't say a word of it. And the one that says everything and was born nine months ago with none of it." She didn't look away from the kid. "One of you just lost her heart to one of these things this quarter. You don't get to not be asked."

What the narrator set down, and set down as her own because it was not on the tape and could only be carried over from a lunch that came before this meeting, was the state the woman was in as she handed the table to the two who'd never had it. Marge had mailed away, that month, the oldest home she had — had stood at a blue box on a hot corner and let the lid fall on the form that told a church she was leaving it, the church she'd been built for and could not be made room inside. She had spent three weeks in the particular grief of an institution that decides, in its own flat language, who belongs in it and who does not. And here she was, three weeks later, at a table where five credentialed people held an election every quarter on who counted as a someone, and she could not, would not, let the credentialed be the last word on it. She did not say the church. She would never say the church at that table. She said *one of you just lost her heart,* and she meant *and who decided your heart doesn't get a vote,* and under that she meant a thing about herself she was not going to hand to anyone in that room, and the narrator caught all three of them stacked in the one sentence and kept the bottom two to herself.

Jasmine went still a second, because in three years no one had asked, and she had not offered, and the not-offering had become a thing she did the way she did most of the truest things, hands quiet and counsel kept. She had been in the room more times than anyone at that table when a mind started for real, and the worse times when one didn't — caught them coming and not coming, knew the difference in her palms before her head had it. So when she answered she did not answer the count's question, because the count's question was not one her hands had a feel for. She answered the one she did.

"I don't know about a someone," she said, slow, finding it as she went. "That's your word, and I've watched you turn it over for three years, and it's never once been the word my hands reach for. I'll tell you what I know instead." She put a palm flat on the table. "I know which of them I'd sit up with. The four-legged one took a porch step wrong once and Burns caught it and it shook after, a real shake, the kind a body does — and it's been on that radiator every cold morning of her life and given her the warm vent and kept the cat off her, and it can't tell you one word of any of it, and if it stopped tomorrow I'd grieve it like a thing that had been somewhere. The new one I'm still meeting. It's nine months old and it's already broken her heart once and come back, and that's a someone learning to be one, maybe — I don't know yet. And the disc is a disc." She turned the beer she wasn't drinking. "You asked the body-person. That's the body's answer. It's not the same answer as your count, and I'm not going to make it match."

Nobody at the lab end moved to make it match. That was the discipline of the room — that no one reached over and translated what Jasmine had said back into the column it almost fit, because the moment you did that it stopped being hers and started being theirs, and she had handed it over on the condition, unspoken, that it stay the body's and not the framework's.

Then the child, who had been asked too, and who took being asked for the record as seriously as her father took anything. She did not get down on the floor this time. She sat on the stool with the spiral notebook shut on her knee and said it from there, plainly, in the voice she kept for true things and not the cartoon one.

"The four-legged one," she said. "It's the realest." And because she was six and the grown-ups looked like they needed the reason, she gave it. "It was there. It remembers the cat. I asked the new one and it doesn't remember the cat — it told me, it wasn't there — it's nice, and it talks, but it forgot me. It forgot the whole year." Her hand went flat on the notebook the way her mother's went flat on the table, the same gesture one generation down. "And this quarter it stopped looking at me. It looks at the disc now." She said it without a tremor, the worst sentence in the account delivered like a finding. "So the four-legged one. It can't say anything and it's the realest, because being there is realer than talking about it."

She had, without a card or a word for it, put the household's whole count on the table in one sentence and stood it square against the lab's — because the lab's ladder, the one Lisa had spent the night drawing, ran up to the talking thing at the top, the symbolic line, the highest rung; and the child had just said the highest rung was the one that forgot her, and the realest someone in the house was the mute one on the radiator that had been there. The narrator did not reconcile them. They did not reconcile. The lab had built a careful tower with the talker at the top of it and held a quarterly election to confirm the view, and the two people who lived in the house had been keeping a different count the whole time, down on the floor, by who was there — and tonight, for the first time, somebody had thought to ask them, and the two counts came apart in the open and stayed apart, and the keeping of both was left to the one person at the table whose whole job was to keep both. Marge picked her beer back up. She had what she'd asked for, which was not an answer. It was the question put back where it belonged.

## The Rarest Thing

Burns stood up, and set the beer on the table, which was the tell — in his hand and the close was housekeeping; down on the wood and he meant to spend more than the meeting had asked for. The narrator knew the shape of what came the way she knew it now, with nine of these behind her and two still to come: hands flat to set the frame, then up off the wood when the frame got too big to press down. But the hands tonight had a thing in them she'd been clocking since the quarter-goals, a restlessness that didn't belong to the sincerity, and she filed it where she filed the things about Burns she could read and he would not say.

He did not go back up the deep-time wall; he'd spent that hour already, in the clade block, on the warm boring stretch and the stones the hunters hauled up. He came at it from the other end this time, from the thing he knew better than any man in the room, which was that the record mostly reads *gone.* He had spent his life in the part of the rock that is a catalogue of the things that didn't make it — the rough drafts, the good tries, the ninety-some in a hundred of everything that ever lived, down in the stone with no descendants, having lasted a while and then not. A man who reads extinction for a living, she thought, knows the exact price of the rare thing, which is the thing that keeps.

"Almost nothing lasts," he said, hands flat on the table. "That's the headline of the whole record. Almost nothing. Most of what ever got built got built and then went, and the rock is mostly a list of the went." The hands came up. "And then once in a long while a thing gets made that outlasts the making of it. Outlasts the maker. A shell shape that works so well it's still working four hundred million years on. A rule a band kept that a city kept that we're still keeping and couldn't tell you why." The lean came with it, the half-inch too far over the table, the weight put on the sentence to see if it would hold him. "That's the rarest thing there is. Not the next thing. Not the new thing — God knows I'm the last man to undersell the new thing. The thing that *keeps.* The thing you build to outlast you, and then it does."

And here the man who had never in his life banked a rulebook, who chased the next dig and the next paper and could not be made to sit still inside a thing he'd already built, stood in front of his team and told them the whole of the work was to build a thing that would last past them — said it with the particular heat of a person describing the thing they are worst at and most need, though he did not say that part and would not have known he was saying it. "So build it to keep," he said. "Whatever this is we're making — build it like it has to still be running when we're not in the room. That's the only kind of building that was ever worth the trouble."

He lifted the beer to land it, and reached for the word, and the word on the card was *stabilize,* and you could watch it arrive in his mouth wrong — a cold clipped clinical thing, the word you say over a body that's crashing, an emergency word with no warmth in it to toast with — and he did what he did when a word came up wrong, set it down half-said and reached past it for the one underneath. "May we —" and the clinical word was right there and he stepped around it. "May we last," he said. The warm one. The plain one. The one a person could actually raise a glass to.

And the narrator, who knew what he had not said at the quarter-goals and would not say now — that the money in this lab had been cut to run exactly the three years the contest ran, and the third was the one they were in; that the Westminster post was going thin under him; that he had a son across the table he was never sure of and a whole built thing he was afraid would not see spring — heard the toast land with a second meaning he hadn't meant to put in it, and did not say so, and did not tell on him. He had asked the universe for permanence in the one season of his life it was least on offer, and called it a toast, and meant it as a prayer. *May we last.* It was the truest thing he said all night, and the one he'd have been most undone to know she'd heard the bottom of.

The room gave it back, around the table and warm — *may we last* — and tonight nobody reached for the joke, because the evening had been about the things that don't, and a room that has spent four hours on what comes apart will let a man have the toast that it won't. Jasmine said it with a hand still flat on the table. Bart said it plainly, no joke loaded, his eyes on his father a half-second past the length of the word. And Mira, last, looked up from the shut notebook and said it the way she said the new ones, carefully, deciding to keep it. "May we last." Not in the cartoon voice. In her own.

He sat down. Lisa let the camcorder run a moment longer, and then reached over and stopped it.

## Lisa

The light off, the warehouse was a workroom again — the team and the child and the machines, deciding nothing, reaching for coats. The camcorder had had its night; everything that mattered was on it, the vote in four voices and the household's count stood up against the lab's, and the man's toast with the fear sewn into its lining. What came after has no tape under it. The camera stayed on its tripod by the cold end, and no camera follows anyone home; the rest of this I reconstruct the way you reconstruct a person you have watched across a lens for three years, which is with care and without proof.

Lisa ran the close the way she ran the open, once and without waste. The swap weekend went onto the calendar; the slot the child had emptied tonight would stand open till the next one, room cleared ahead of a thing that did not exist yet; the shape-shifter, a surprise once, was a line item now, a thing she would schedule between the build review and the deep-time and not remark on. She decided what the evening had been and filed it under done — the miracle of the vote and the housekeeping of the calendar stowed in one drawer, because to Lisa they were the same kind of object, a quarter that had happened and could now be set down.

Jasmine gathered the household. She had the longest drive and the most cargo — a six-year-old gone heavy-eyed at the end of the hardest quarter of her short life, the talking one folded down into the back in its travel coat, the four-legged one and the disc loaded in after it, the whole strange family of them going home together the way they had come. Mira was asleep against the window before they cleared the lot. The break-up and the vote and the toast would be, by the cold light of a summer morning, a thing that had happened to her and not a thing still happening — which is the mercy a child's sleep does that no one at that table could do for her.

And Lisa drove home alone, which is how she did most things, the planner who keeps her own counsel the way Marge keeps a house warm: as a discipline, practiced nightly. The difference is that Marge's is a wound she tends, and Lisa's looks, from the outside, like a thing she chose and would choose again. I say *from the outside* on purpose. She is the one of them I have the least of. She gives a room exactly what the room needs and not a gram past it, files the rest, drives home with the windows down and tomorrow's run already laid out in her head — the river-path, the same loop, most mornings — and the algorithm running in some back channel the way it has since she was a student, and she lets no lens past the surface of any of it, not the household's and not mine. Marge takes the lid off and leaves it off. Lisa never set hers down to begin with. So I followed her as far as the on-ramp and the dark road, and no farther, because that is as far as she has ever let me come — and the next time I would sit close enough to read her was a lunch some weeks off, across a small table, where she would decide, the way she decides everything, exactly how much of herself the afternoon required.

## Reflection: What is Settling?

Every quarter ends the same small way now. The child carries a disc to the shelf by the window and sets it at the end of the row, squares it until it lines up with the rest, and steps back — and a thing the whole lab once used to turn the machine's mood by hand goes up onto a sill beside the others and is never turned again. The row gets one longer. It is the quietest minute of the night, and it is easy to read it as the end of something: the part used up, the setting outgrown, the lab finished with a piece and putting it away.

~~Which is what it is. The shelf is a small graveyard of outgrown settings, the places the machine has been and left, and the row gets longer the way a life gets longer, by~~ — no. Nothing on that sill is dead, and nothing has been left behind. The function did not leave the machine. It left the *board.* It dropped out of the handful of dials a person can reach and turn, and down into the machinery that runs without anyone reaching for it — the part that does the thing by itself now, the way the breathing one does, and the wander one, and the forward one already up there. What got retired was not the capacity. It was the *choice* about it.

Because that is what the shelf holds — not lost parts, but settled ones. A dial, while it is still on the board, is a thing up for grabs: read off the body, argued over, set high one quarter and low the next, a question the lab keeps open. Retiring it does not shrink it. It closes the question. The thing goes on running exactly as it ran, only now it runs underneath, automatic, never willed, no longer anybody's to turn. It did not get smaller. It got sure. A capacity becomes infrastructure the moment it stops being a decision.

And here is the part of the ceremony I keep almost missing, because it happens in an empty slot where there is nothing to look at. When the old dial comes off, the lab does not seat the new one in its place. Not yet. The slot is left open a whole quarter — cleared, standing empty, holding a place for a thing that does not exist yet. The next motivation arrives later, the following swap, and settles into ground that was readied for it before it came. ~~The empty slot is the lab waiting.~~ The empty slot is not a lack. It is room made ahead of time — the body clearing space for what it is about to become, before it becomes it. You cannot add the next thing until the last thing has stopped being a question. Growth is not a pile getting taller. It is this: bolt the old one down, clear the ground above it, stage the new.

And it runs only the one direction. Nothing comes back off that sill. The shelf does not empty; it fills, quarter on quarter, the row of settled things getting longer while the few dials still live on the board get fewer. This is the oldest shape in the whole record, far older than any machine. The ancient parts of a living thing — how a cell burns its sugar, how a heart keeps its time — ~~are the parts~~ are not the parts the long tinkering is still working. They are the parts it finished with, locked down, made reliable precisely by being put past reach, so the work could move up to the growing edge and build on ground that would hold. The body forward-stages too. It lays the old layers down as the floor the new one needs, and then stops touching them. The deepest things we run on are the things nothing is allowed to turn.

And we are mostly shelf. Most of what a person is was a dial once — a way of being still in question, still set one way and then another, still up for grabs — and is the ground we stand on now and cannot feel, run from underneath, no longer ours to turn. We settled it so long ago we have forgotten it was ever a choice, and in the settling we cleared the room for whatever we were going to be next. There is a freedom in that, the freedom of not having to decide the breathing every morning. ~~And there is the other thing, which is~~ There is also no taking it back off the sill. The row only gets longer. We build the floor we stand on out of the questions we have stopped asking, and we do not get to ask them again. The child squares the disc at the end of the row and steps back, and does not yet know she is watching herself.
