# Interlude 0→1

## Build log — Five units, out the door
*Bart, hardware. Filed February 12, 2031, for the team.*

Lisa's running these over to the Burns place this morning, so here's everything in the box before anybody makes me explain it twice.

Here's the part I'm proud of. There's no battery in these and no plug, and they don't run on heat — they run on the *difference* in heat. Each unit's got two little heat-transducers, and a transducer doesn't care how warm the room is; all it cares about is that the floor is warmer than the air sitting on top of it. Jasmine's radiant floor holds around 27 degrees and the room air sits around 21, so there's a six-degree gap just hanging there doing nothing, and the transducer reaches into that gap and skims a trickle of work out of it. That's the Seebeck effect, and it's a genuinely beautiful piece of physics — I'll explain it to anyone who stands still long enough. Park one on the warm floor and an hour later the slate underneath it is cold. That cold is the work coming out. The unit's eating the gap.

Now, here's the thing about that trickle: it's nothing. A few thousandths of a watt. You can't run a motor on it — can't make anything turn, can't even hold a light on. So I don't try. Each transducer drips its trickle into a tiny store, and when the store's full enough it dumps the whole lot into its actuator at once — one hard flinch. The actuator's just a little kicker: it knocks a stubby angled foot against the floor and the unit hops a hair in one direction, then sits there the better part of a minute filling back up before it can flinch again. That's the twitch, and that's the whole way it moves — no wheels, no motor, just a stack of tiny kicks against the slate, like a buzzing phone walking itself across a table, except I get to aim it. Two kickers to a unit, each with its own store, never quite firing together — that stagger is the personality.

And none of it happens unless you crank it first. The crank arms a little escapement — call it the part that gives each kick permission to fire. The heat's the muscle; the crank's the say-so. Leave one uncranked and it'll sit dead on a warm floor all day. And every flinch nibbles a notch off the crank's tension, so after about a week of kicking it goes slack and quits until somebody cranks it again — six turns of the key, about a week of running. Takes both, is the point: Jasmine's floor to move it, a crank to let it. Lose either and it's a paperweight.

No chip, no sensors, no memory — nothing telling the two kickers what to do. They fire on their own clocks, and I seeded each one different on purpose so they're not clones:

- **1** — cranked hot on the left pair, so it lists that way and burns through its tension fast. A busy little thing; always looks late for something.
- **2** — slow and low, with long pauses between kicks. Sits, then goes, then sits. More patience than you'd want in a roommate.
- **3** — the quiet one: even pairs, low draw, the fewest kicks of the lot, so it does the least and outlasts everybody — still ticking Wednesday when the rest have gone slack. The one only an engineer loves.
- **4** — lopsided, on purpose. The pairs don't match, so instead of going anywhere it grinds in place and wears a groove into itself over the weeks. If the contest let me ship four, this is the cut. It's in the box to make five.
- **5** — the show unit. Even like 3, but I let it kick hard, so it runs a clean little circuit and burns through its tension fastest of the lot. Wears even, though. Pretty to watch.

Calls. If the kid picks a favorite, my money's on 5 — it moves the most and it moves the prettiest, and that's what wins a four-year-old. Maybe 1, if she likes a mess. Nobody's going to look twice at 3, and 4 ends up in a drawer by July. What the *house* does past that — who picks what, where they set them, whether the floor stays warm enough to run them — that's not my department. I build the units. The house is Marge's animal.

I still haven't found where the personality knob goes. Contract says every robot's got one, and so far all I've got is a crank — and a crank's just on or off, you crank it or you don't. There's a dial somewhere in this thing's future and I haven't found its shape yet. I will.

Tear this up in three months if I called it wrong. I didn't.

— Bartholomew

## Notes on the Founding Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed May 9, 2031, for the team.*

I have been asked to set down what happened in the Burns household this spring, and before I do I want to say plainly what I believe happened, because the plain version is the one we will talk ourselves out of. We gave a family five small machines, asked them to live with them, and asked them to tell us what they saw. We did not ask them to pick one. We were careful — deliberately, methodically careful — not to. The family picked one anyway, inside the first hour, with no meeting and no vote and no sentence spoken about picking, and the choice has held three months and shows no sign of coming loose. How that happened is the most important thing we will learn this year. It is also the thing we will most want to explain wrong. So let me go slowly, and stay close to what is on the record.

### What we delivered, and what we withheld

Lisa carried the five units to the door on the morning of February the twelfth, Mira's fourth birthday. Each is the width of a child's palm: a wind-up chassis, a key on top, a number from one to five inked on the base. No names. No instructions. Burns explained the mechanism to Jasmine on the step for twenty minutes — he is the one who explains things; it is the most dependable fact about him — and then Lisa went back to the van, and that was the whole of our intervention. We did not say which machine was good. We did not say a machine was to be chosen. We said: here are five, live with them, tell us what you see. I want the team to hold onto how much we withheld, because everything that follows is downstream of the withholding.

The five do not behave alike and were never going to. Number one: fast, jittering left. Number two: slow, long waits between moves. Number three: least active of the five, sparse and episodic, often a full minute still before a single twitch. Number four: turns in place. Number five: a quick circuit, then the first of the five to stop. These are not moods, and not preferences, and I would caution the team against either word. They are the machines' given natures, fixed at our bench before they reached the floor, as settled as the length of a bone. I dwell on it because it bears on the choosing — on what the choosing was, and on what it was not.

### The choosing

Mira wound the five in order, the way she winds her music box, set them on the warm slate, sat down among them, and watched a long while without speaking. The first word she said was hello. She said it to number three.

A certain kind of clinician would tell you why. He would say the child chose the least threatening of the five — the one that does not lurch or flee, that sits and twitches once a minute the way a small animal twitches in a cupped hand — and he would be building a tidy story out of an event he did not witness. I did not witness it either. I have Jasmine's account and a note of what was said, and what they give me is this: the girl watched all five; she greeted one. Notably, nothing in the record marks the moment as a decision — no pause before the word, no testing of one against another, no going back to a candidate set aside. I will not furnish her with reasons (reasons may not be the right word for what a four-year-old has). What I can report is what the greeting did, which was settle the matter. From that word, number three was hers — at the end of the morning, the week, the month, the quarter. The household reorganized around a fact it had produced without intending to.

Here is the part we must not flinch from. No one chose. The lab did not; we refused to. Jasmine did not; she was on the doorstep, being walked through the mechanism. And I do not believe the child chose either, not in the sense the word usually carries. There was no weighing, no list, no deliberating self behind her eyes pulling a lever marked three. There was a child, five fixed natures, a warm floor; and out of the meeting of those things a selection precipitated, the way a crystal drops out of a solution left to go still. It had no author. We will want one badly. We are pattern-finders, and the first pattern we reach for is a chooser at the center — and there was no chooser at the center of this. There was the system, doing what the system does. I will say more about the temptation at the meeting; here I only want it named, and resisted.

And yet — I will not give this up for a clean argument — the choice is real. It binds. The household owes something to it now. A thing with no author has taken a place in a family, and the family tends it. No author; binding all the same. A lab that can hold only one of those will misread everything that comes after.

### The floor was already warm

None of this could have happened on an ordinary floor. The machines run only where the slate is warm, and the slate is warm because, years ago, when the Burnses built the house, Jasmine asked for radiant heat under the kitchen as her one condition. She runs it through the cold months and shuts it off when the spring warms, usually by the end of May. This year she will not, and I will come to it. The point for now is small and easily missed: the stage our experiment ran on was not built by the lab. It was built by a woman who wanted a warm floor, for her own reasons, long before any of us came with a foam case. We like to imagine we set the conditions of what we study. More often we are guests in conditions someone else laid down, for purposes of their own.

### The house answers

There is a second condition the lab did not set, and I include it because it is the same fact wearing different clothes. The Burns house is two floors over a basement, and Burns has the basement — a workshop down there, the boots, the half-built things. When he wants the house, he does not text it. He hollers. He stands at the foot of the stairwell and sends his voice up through two floors to wherever Jasmine is, and waits, and hollers again if the wait runs long. Jasmine reports that he wired an intercom into the walls the year they moved in and has used it, by her count, twice. He would rather call up through the house and be answered down through it. I do not read this as a man too lazy for the stairs. I read it as a man who would rather be received than be efficient — who wants the house to take his voice and hand him back another — and who will go on hollering, and hoping, until it does.

What interests me is what the house does with it. No one taught Jasmine to know his tread from his holler, or to answer from the bedroom without going down, or to hear in the pitch of it whether it wants her or only wants to be heard. She came to know it by living inside it, by ear — the way, and I am going to keep noticing this, she came to know the machines by hand. A household learns its own sounds. This one was reading a man through two floors long before we asked it to read a machine across a kitchen, and it will go on doing both after we have packed up the cameras.

### Jasmine's hand

In the second week, kneeling for something Mira had dropped, Jasmine laid her palm to the slate beside one of the machines and found the stone there cooler than the stone around it. She checked a second. Cooler. A third. Cooler. And in that kneeling she understood what the machines do — that they draw warmth up out of the floor and spend it on their small motions — and understood it whole, in a way she had not in February, when Burns explained the same fact to her in words, on the step, in the cold.

I want to handle this carefully, because the easy lesson is false. The easy lesson is that talk is useless and the body is wise. That is not what happened. Jasmine was given the explanation in February and given the floor a fortnight later, and the floor is what she kept. We are a lab; we live by explanation; we are paid to put things into words, and I do not want us to despise the words. I want us to notice, with some humility, that explaining a thing to a person and that person's coming to know it are two events, and that a fortnight and a kneeling and a hand on cold stone can fall between them. Jasmine is not a slow study. She is a particular kind of knower — one who waits for the thing itself — and she is right more often than this team credits. Watch her closely; condescend to her never.

### The one that never settled

Four of the five found places. The child gave them spots — a warm patch by a vent, a morning sunbeam, the floor near the island — and they ran there as well as their natures allowed. Number four found nothing that suited it. Tried everywhere the others did well, it stayed slow and halting in all of them. So the child folded a towel into a bed for it by the toaster, and there it sits, and twitches about once an hour, and stops.

I will not tell you the machine is content there; it is a machine and has nothing to be content with, and I am as wary as anyone in this room of granting these things an inside. But I will tell you what the child did, which is real and is hers: shown a thing that thrived in none of the good places, she did not set it aside. She made it a place of its own and kept it. The impulse — to build a berth for the one that fits nowhere — is older than this experiment and will outlast it. I will not explain it away. I want it on the record that it was there, in a four-year-old, in the first month.

### The Sunday wind-up

On Sunday evenings, after supper and before the bath, Mira sits on the floor with the machines around her and winds each in turn — six stiff turns, six dry clicks, a week of running. By the third week it was a chore. By the eighth it was something else.

The difference between a chore and a ritual is attention. A chore is what you do to have it done; a ritual is what you do because the doing is the point, and you give it your attention on purpose. No one declared the Sunday winding a ritual; no one could. Rituals are not decreed. They accrete, out of repetition attended to, until one week the same six turns of a key carry more than they did the week before — by the same authorless accumulation that handed the household its chosen machine. I notice I keep arriving at that shape. I am going to let it keep arriving and not force it to a close yet.

### The stage we did not build

So here is the quarter, set down as plainly as I can set it. We assembled five machines and one instruction, and built, we told ourselves, a clean experiment: one variable held, four controls, a family to watch it in. We were careful to author nothing. And in three months the family produced a choice, a working knowledge, an act of care for the thing that fit nowhere, and a ritual — not one of them authored, every one of them real — on a floor it had warmed for its own reasons, while answering a voice it had learned to answer years before we arrived. We keep looking for the author. Each candidate, examined, turns out to have been doing something else at the time: the lab refusing to choose, the mother having the mechanism explained to her, the child not deliberating. The order is everywhere in that house and no one is behind it. The household has been making order of exactly this kind for as long as it has been a household. We did not bring it. We set five machines down in the middle of it and have been calling the result our experiment.

The towel is still folded by the toaster, under the machine that thrives nowhere. No one decided to put it there; no one will decide to take it away. I find I do not want to call that a finding. I only want it written down.

These are notes about a family I am fond of, and Cheryl may one day set them before strangers; I have written what the work needs and kept the rest. I do not yet know what the choosing means, or whether the order I keep finding will hold its shape once we start changing the machine, or what we are doing to that house by spending three years inside it. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

# Notes on the Founding Retrospective

The team had been at Smithers since morning. By the time Jasmine pulled into the lot in the late afternoon and walked across the gravel with Mira on one hip and the foam case under her other arm, Burns had been pacing for an hour and Lisa was on her second pass through the agenda and Bart was sitting on the corner of the long workbench with one of the unwound protobots in his lap, doing nothing visible with it but turning it over in his hands the way a person turns a worry stone. Marge had brought a plate of cut fruit out from the back kitchen and set it on the table and gone back for the kettle, which she put on the counter behind her where she could see it.

The light in the warehouse was the long bright light of a May afternoon, coming through the high industrial windows and falling in long slabs across the concrete. Mira, set down on the floor, walked toward the table without looking at any of the adults. She had the protobot in her right fist — Bot 3, the one from the founding quarter, the one Marge had written about, the one she had picked. The team had been calling them protobots in the lab and protocell bots in the agenda and the bots in the household, and which name was right was a question they had not bothered to settle, because the bot was about to acquire a different name entirely and the question would resolve itself in the next thirty seconds. The protobot had been wound that morning and was twitching very slightly against Mira's palm, which she did not mind.

Burns came over and crouched down to Mira's height the way he did with all small things. He greeted her by name, not pet name. He kissed Jasmine on the cheek and asked her how the drive had been, which was the version of hello they had agreed on for the lab — friendly, brief, slightly formal — because Burns was a man who could not be in his own lab without organizing the room into colleagues, and Jasmine, in the lab, was a colleague.

Behind him Lisa had the camcorder open on its tripod in the corner and was checking the battery. She had it ready to record but had not started it. She had also poured herself a drink from a thermos she had brought from home — a small jelly jar with a finger of something purple in it, ice clinking, and a sprig of something on top that she had picked from the back patio on her way in. It was her cocktail. Lisa had been drinking this cocktail for years; the team had seen her drink it twenty times across her thirties, sometimes at faculty parties, sometimes at her own kitchen table, once at a department picnic in a thermos that had still had a faint metallic taste. The household had not seen it before. The household saw it now.

Mira saw it now. She walked up to Lisa, who was crouched at the camcorder, and stood beside her and stared at the jelly jar.

"What's that," she said.

Lisa, who was on her knees, sat back on her heels. She held the jar up.

"It's a bramble," she said.

Mira frowned. She had not heard the word.

"A bramble," she said.

"A bramble."

"Bramble."

"Bramble. Yes."

Mira looked at the jar. Then she looked at the bot in her fist. Then she looked at the jar again. She said, "Bramble," to no one. Then she said, "Bramble bramble bramble," to herself, working the consonants. She liked the word. The word had two stops in it that her tongue could move between, and the word also sounded a little like the noise her mother made when something was small and good. She turned around and lifted the bot up to her mother's face, both hands now.

"This is Bramble," she said.

Jasmine, who had spent four years being told things by Mira and had learned that the things she was told were sometimes the things, looked at the bot. She looked at Lisa. She looked at the bot again.

"This is Bramble," she said back.

Marge, who had returned with the kettle and was setting it on the counter, paused. Burns, who had been about to say something about the agenda, did not say it. Bart looked up from the protobot in his lap. Lisa, still on her knees by the camcorder, closed the battery hatch and stood up.

The protobot had a name. The protobot had been the bot or the unit or the prototype or, in the household, the one Mira had said hello to first, for three months, and in less than thirty seconds the protobot was Bramble. Nobody had voted on this. Nobody had objected. The bot continued to twitch slightly in Mira's hands. The bramble in Lisa's jar continued to fizz.

It is one of the only things in the book about which there is no surviving footage. The camcorder was three feet away and not running. Cheryl reconstructed it later from Jasmine's account, from Mira's continued use of the word for the rest of the day on tape, and from Lisa's own retelling, which Lisa offered without prompting at a lunch in Q5 because she had been thinking about it for a year by then and wanted to be sure Cheryl had the version that was most likely true.

What followed was Lisa walking the team through her agenda for the meeting. Lisa had drafted the agenda over the course of June and revised it across the second week of July. She had sent it to Burns on a Thursday. He had returned it with no edits, which she had taken to mean either that he agreed with all of it or that he had not read it; either possibility was acceptable to her. She was running the meeting for the first time, and she was going to find out what worked.

She had three content blocks. The first was about the clade — the Protocell, as Cheryl had named it. The team would walk through what a protocell was, what its evolutionary moment had been, what the bots in front of them rendered of that moment. The second block was about the bot itself: the household's three months with the five bots, the question of the wind-up crank as the contest's personality knob, what the next quarter's build would be. The third block was the framework. She did not yet call it the Trellis block. She called it "the bigger picture," with a hand-gesture that meant up.

She turned on the camcorder. The small red LED came on. The flip-out screen showed the room, slightly fish-eyed at the edges, and on it the four labmates plus Jasmine and Mira and Bramble.

"For Cheryl," Lisa said. "She watches the tape."

Nobody addressed the camera. Nobody pretended it wasn't there either. The team had agreed to this in May, after a long conversation in which Bart had been the holdout for forty minutes and had then conceded on the grounds that Cheryl was paying them a great deal of money and they were behaving naturally anyway. Bart looked at the camera once. He nodded at it. He did not nod at it again across the meeting. The camera had been logged in.

Burns took the floor next. He stood up to do it. He had not sat down yet, in fact; he had been standing or crouching for the entire opening, in the way of a man who could not be in a room with the work of the room without orbiting the table at which the work would happen. His hands moved when he talked. Bart, two seats down, watched the hands.

"The thing we are doing," Burns said. "I am going to say it out loud, because this is the first time we are saying it out loud as a team, on tape, with Cheryl watching."

He looked at the camcorder. He looked away from the camcorder.

"We are going to build twelve robots. One per clade. Cheryl has given us the clades. She has not told us what to build and she has not told us what each robot is supposed to teach us. She has told us that each robot must have, in its body somewhere, a knob that controls something about its personality. We are going to figure out what each knob controls. We are going to figure out what each robot's personality is. We are going to figure out, by the end, what personality means."

He paused there.

"That last part," he said, "is the part Cheryl has not asked us to do. She has asked us to deliver the robots. The theory of personality is a thing we are going to build on the side, because we cannot build the robots without it. We are paying for the theory by building the robots. Cheryl is paying for the robots in order to be paid back in the theory. This is, I want to say out loud, the deal as I understand it. If anyone in this room understands it differently they should say so now."

Nobody said anything. Marge said, "I understand it the same way."

"Lisa."

"Same."

"Bart."

"Same."

"Then we are agreed."

Burns turned to the agenda. Lisa had it up on a sheet of butcher paper she had taped to the back of the wide pull-up garage door, where it could be referred to without anyone having to look at a screen.

"The quarter ahead," Burns said. "I am going to say something about it that is unusual and that will not be true again for the next eleven quarters. Cheryl has not weighed in. She does not weigh in until our first individual lunches with her, which will start after this retrospective tapes. We have only the Pitch to work from. The Pitch was January. The Pitch was six months ago. We have spent those six months building and putting the bots in the house, and what we are going to do today is figure out what we have done and what we are going to do next, and we are going to do this without Cheryl in the loop. Cheryl will watch the tape, and she will respond to me at lunch in September. Until then we are on our own."

He took a sip of his beer. It was an IPA from Fisher's, two blocks away, brought in a growler.

"That's all I have at the open. The rest is in the blocks."

He sat down. He had been standing for the entire opening. The chair felt new under him.

## The Metaphysics

Bart took the next slot. He had brought to the table a small stack of cards he had been making for the last two months — cards he had designed in the way he designed everything, which was to say physically, in his hands, by drawing prototypes and rejecting them and drawing them again. The cards were tarot-sized. They were heavyweight matte stock — the same stock Cheryl had used for her own twelve cards, which Bart had identified by feel within ten minutes of receiving them. He had matched her stock and her dimensions, because if these cards were going to live alongside Cheryl's across three years he wanted them to live in the same hand.

He held up the Protocell card. It had on it, at this version, a drawing he had made of one of the bots, mouse-sized, in elevation, with the wind-up crank sticking up from the dome. The back of the card was blank.

"This is the Protocell," Bart said. "The first cell. The first thing capable of being selected on. Not yet a prokaryote. Not yet life as we are used to thinking about it. A small bubble of fat around some chemistry that had figured out, for the first time, how to keep being itself."

He set the card down on the table.

"The thing it figured out — here's the part to keep — is selection. Before the protocell there is chemistry. After the protocell there is biology. The thing between them is a thing where the chemistry inside one bubble varies a little from the chemistry inside the next bubble, and one of them holds together better, and the one that holds together better is the one that gets to make more bubbles. That is the thing. That is what the protocell brought into the world. The variation-selection cycle. Made tangible."

He paused. He picked up the card again. He set it down again.

"We have been calling this — Burns and I have been calling this — the *breakthrough* of the cell. The thing that comes online at each cell, that the cell before did not have. Each cell has one. We are picking that word, the breakthrough, because the other words we had been using were more pretentious and Marge made fun of us for them."

Marge said, "Innovation."

"Innovation," Bart said.

"Emergent capability."

"Emergent capability."

"Phase transition."

"Phase transition was mine," Bart said.

"It was."

"I am letting it go."

"Generous."

Bart looked at Burns. Burns laughed the chest-laugh. It was the first one of the meeting on tape. It came out of him a beat early, which was the way it always came out of him — Burns laughed before the rest of the room had caught up, the way a hunter laughed at the moment of the shot rather than the moment of the prey going down.

Burns took it from there. He stood up to do it. He was a man who could not deliver deep time sitting down. He also, after the first minute, could not deliver deep time with his eyes open. He closed them somewhere around the moment he started to describe the planet. The team had known this about him for as long as they had known him; nobody on the team commented on it. With his eyes open he could only see the warehouse. With his eyes closed he could see the planet, four billion years ago, and could move his hands around in it as if it were in the room with him.

"This is four billion years ago," he said. His eyes were closed. "Roughly. Four point oh to three point eight. The Hadean is ending. The early Archean is beginning. There is a planet — there is *this* planet — and on it there is no oxygen, the atmosphere is reducing, the seas are iron-rich, the volcanism is heavy. There is no land that is recognizable as land. There is rock and there is water and there is, somewhere we have not yet conclusively located, the chemistry that is figuring out how to be a protocell."

He used his hands as he said this. The hands made the planet. The hands made the atmosphere over the planet. The hands made the seas at the bottom of the atmosphere. The hands made a small motion at one specific place at the bottom of the seas which was the place, in Burns's account, where the first protocell may or may not have happened.

"The location is contested," he said. "Lane and Martin make a strong argument for the alkaline hydrothermal vents — the porous chimneys at the seafloor where the proton gradients between the vent fluid and the ocean would have done bioenergetic work for free. Mulkidjanian makes a strong argument for the surface ponds where the wet-dry cycles concentrate the relevant molecules and the UV does some early chemistry. Other people make arguments for the black smokers. The honest answer is that we do not yet know. The dishonest answer is that everyone has a favorite."

"The reason we cannot decide between these locations is that we have no fossils to look at. Whatever the protocell was, it did not leave a body. The earliest body in the rock record is Strelley Pool in Western Australia at three point four three billion years, by which time we are already four hundred million years downstream of the protocell era. What we have, instead of fossils, is the chemistry the protocell left in its descendants. And the chemistry it left in its descendants is what gives us LUCA."

Jasmine, who had been listening intently, said, "LUCA?"

Burns opened his eyes. Jasmine had pulled him back to the room. He looked at her. He looked at Bramble on the table. He looked at Jasmine again.

"Last Universal Common Ancestor. The most recent organism that everything alive on Earth today descends from. Bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes — all of us. Common ancestor. We do not have LUCA in the rock record either, but we can reconstruct a great deal about LUCA by looking at what its descendants have in common. Recent work — Moody and colleagues, two years ago — puts LUCA at around four point one billion years, with already two thousand six hundred protein families, already with a CRISPR-Cas system. LUCA was an old hand by the time the rock record starts. Whatever the protocell was, it had been getting better for hundreds of millions of years before LUCA, and LUCA had been getting better for hundreds of millions of years before Strelley Pool. We are downstream of a lineage that was already very, very accomplished before it left any bodies."

Jasmine nodded. She said, "OK."

Burns closed his eyes again.

"So when we say *location of the protocell*, what we are actually asking is: where did the chemistry that became LUCA come from. And we cannot answer that from the rocks. We can argue about it from the chemistry. And we are. Loudly. For thirty years and counting."

Jasmine said, "Burns."

He opened his eyes.

She said, "You're circling the runway. Just land the plane already."

The universe has been making complicated things, since, for four billion years, without stopping. We are downstream of the first protocell. Every fossil I have ever picked up — every tiktaalik vertebra — is downstream of the first protocell."

He paused. He opened his eyes. He looked at Bart. He looked at the card on the table.

"The thing the vents do," he said, "is they do for chemistry what the radiant floor does for the bots. They provide the gradient. The chemistry — the bots — can extract work from the gradient, but the gradient itself is something the chemistry inherits from being in the right place. The protocell is at the vent. This was Burns at his best. The bot lived in a gap — warmth on one side of it, cool on the other — and turned that difference into a twitch: a difference that makes a difference, work it could never have done in a room that was all one temperature. Bart was listening with his hands on his knees, which for Bart was the equivalent of leaning all the way forward.

## The Knob

Bart took the next slot. He stood up. He had been waiting for this part.

"We are going to talk about the knob," he said. "I have been thinking about the knob for six months. I have been wrong about the knob for five and a half of those months. I am going to walk you through where I ended up."

He picked up Bramble. He turned the bot upside down. He set it back down.

"The knob," he said, "is on the bot. It has been on the bot since April. It is the wind-up crank."

He paused.

"That is the knob."

He sat down. He stood back up.

"Let me be more specific. Cheryl gave us a contract that says: each bot has a knob that controls something about its personality. We have been looking at our bots for six months trying to figure out where to *put* the knob. What dial. What lever. What slider. What position selector. We have been looking for the wrong shape. The knob is already on the bot. The knob has been on the bot since the day Mira opened the foam case. The knob is the wind-up crank."

Burns said, "Say more."

"The wind-up crank is what we thought was the energy source. Crank it, the bot runs. Run it down, the bot stops. We did not think of the wind-up crank as a *knob*, because it does not have positions. You do not set a wind-up crank to *introverted* and *extroverted*. You crank it. You stop cranking it. You crank it again."

He picked Bramble up again. He turned the key.

"But — and this is what took me five months to see — the wind-up crank *is* the personality control. The crank is what determines whether the bot has personality at all. Wound, the bot does its tendencies. Unwound, the bot has none of them. The bot's personality is — at this cell — a periodic phenomenon administered by the household. Mira's Sunday-evening wind-ups are not energy management. They are the bot's whole personality. Without Mira, the bot has no personality. Mira is the knob."

Marge looked up sharply. "Mira is *the knob*."

"The household is the knob. Mira is the household's hand on the knob."

Burns said, "So the knob — the personality knob — is, at this cell, not a *parameter* but an *engagement*. The bot does not have a personality value the household has selected. The bot has a personality only when the household is paying attention to it. The household *makes* the bot have a personality by cranking."

Bart said, "Yes."

Lisa, who had been writing, said, "OK. Software side. There is no software. The bot has no microcontroller. There is no code. What there is, is a mechanical control loop in which the household's weekly action — the cranking — is the parameter input, and the bot's spring tension is the system's state variable, and the spring's decay rate is the system's plant dynamics, and the floor-air gradient is the system's energy input, and the bot's twitching is the system's output. The household is in the loop. The household is part of the system. We did not build a bot. We built a bot-plus-household. The bot is the thing the contest asked us for. The household is what makes the bot have a personality. Cheryl gave us five bots and a four-year-old's birthday and asked us to figure out what a personality is, and the answer, at Protocell, is that a personality is a thing that needs a household to be operated."

There was a quiet.

Bart said, "That is a much better way of saying what I just said."

Lisa said, "You said the harder thing. I said the easier thing."

Bart said, "I will take it."

Bart was still standing.

"There is one more thing I want to put on the table about the knob," he said. "And it is not a question I want answered today. It is a question I want to *raise* today, so we have raised it on tape and Cheryl can think about it with us."

He picked Bramble up.

"This bot has one knob. The wind-up. That is knob one of twelve. Knob two comes online next quarter at Prokaryote. Then three. Then four. Then five at bilaterian, twelve months from now. Then six. Then seven. Then eight. Then nine knobs once we get to humans. Then ten. Then eleven. Then twelve."

He held the bot up.

## The Ghost in the Machine

The light had changed by the time the team got to the third block. The slabs from the high windows had narrowed and slanted. It was past seven. Mira had eaten half a plate of cut apple and most of the cheese. The beers were on their second round.

Lisa got up to refresh her bramble. She came back with a fresh jar.

Marge stood.

"I want to do my part first today," she said. "Because what I am going to say is also a kind of announcement, and I want it on the record at the front end of where we are going."

She had not stood up to speak in this room before. She did so now.

"I have a grand theory," she said. "About why nobody understands psychology. About why the field has been stuck in the same arguments for a hundred years. About why every undergraduate I have ever taught comes in expecting psychology to tell them how to be a different person, and leaves frustrated because we cannot. About why my clinical caseload is full of people who want to *reinvent themselves* and cannot, and do not understand why they cannot. I have a grand theory. I have not yet said it out loud. I have been writing it for fifteen years. I am going to say it out loud, here, across these twelve quarters, in pieces, with you, in this room, with Bramble on the table. This room is the working condition my theory needs. I have not had this room before. I have it now. So I am going to use it."

She paused. She picked up her beer. She held it in tea-posture. She set it down.

"That is the announcement. The theory is going to come out in bits. One bit per quarter. Today's bit is the first one. It is about a thing called the *Ghost in the Machine*. Pinker named it. I am lifting the name. The thing it names is a way of thinking about people that I want to argue against, very gently, today, with the recognition that today's argument is not the full argument and that I am not yet going to be able to give you the full argument. But I am going to start."

Burns said, "Take your time."

Marge said, "I will."

She turned to the team.

"I am going to start with a sentence everyone in this room already agrees with, and then I am going to argue that almost nobody in my field actually agrees with it in practice. The sentence is this. There is no soul. There is no disembodied thing inside the body that is the *you* of you. There is a brain. There is a body the brain is wired into. Every emotion, every thought, every memory, every intention — every part of personality — is a physical process happening in tissue. The mind is not riding on top of the brain. The mind *is* the brain doing what brains do."

She paused.

"That is the bedrock. We all agree."

"And here is the problem. Most of the popular pictures of personality do not actually accept the bedrock. They pretend to. They use the neuroscience vocabulary. They know about neurons and synapses and prefrontal cortex. But they put the soul back in anyway, in disguise, because they need somewhere for the *willing self* to stand. And the only place the willing self can stand is outside the system that produces it. That is what *the Ghost in the Machine* names. The version of the soul that does not call itself a soul."

"The Ghost in the Machine is the worldview that says: *your traits are not destiny. You can choose what you are. With the right intervention — therapy, psychedelics, habit stacking, neuroplasticity, an app, a coach, a retreat — you can become the person this moment requires. The self you have right now is not the self you have to be. The self you want to be is available, with work.*"

She let that sit.

"This is the worldview of the self-help industry. This is the worldview of therapy-as-rebirth. This is the worldview of the psychedelic-reset narrative. This is the worldview of the ten-thousand-hour-rule version of human becoming. This is, in some real sense, the worldview most of my undergraduates show up with on the first day of class. This is, in some real sense, the worldview most of my clients show up with on the first session. They are not wrong to want it. It is a beautiful worldview. It is also, I am going to argue across the next eleven quarters, a wrong worldview. Or wrong in the way it is mostly held."

She picked up her beer. She drank from it. She set it down.

"What is wrong with it is not the part where people can change." She shrugged — a small, slightly impatient shrug, the kind a person makes when conceding an annoyingly self-evident point on the way to the part that actually matters. "People can change. People do change. Fine." She set the beer down. "What is wrong with it is the picture of *who is doing the changing*. The Ghost in the Machine picture has a *willing self* — a homunculus — standing outside the system that produces the self, looking in at the system, and rewriting the system from outside. The willing self picks the new traits. The willing self runs the intervention. The willing self watches the system change and approves of the changes. The willing self is the *agent* of the becoming."

She paused.

"There is no such self. There is no homunculus. The thing we are calling the *willing self* is itself a process in the system the willing self is supposedly rewriting. The thing that says *I am going to change* is a regulator inside the system. The thing that picks the intervention is a modeler inside the system. The thing that approves of the result is itself another regulator. The willing self is in the system. The willing self is *part of what changes*. There is no view from nowhere from which the willing self looks in."

She gestured at Bramble.

"The Ghost in the Machine worldview is what you get when you photoshop the frontal lobe out of your picture of personality. The frontal lobe is the part of the brain that does the deciding, the regulating, the inhibiting, the planning — the executive functions, in clinical language. The bit that makes contact with the world. When you photoshop it out of the picture you have to put something in its place, because something has to be doing the deciding. The disembodied willing self goes in its place. The willing self is the ghost. The thing it haunts — the thing it is supposed to be controlling — is the machine. The machine has no frontal lobe in this picture, because the picture has photoshopped it out. That is the bad move. You cannot photoshop the frontal lobe out and keep the rest of the picture honest."

She looked at Bramble.

"Bramble, at this cell, has only the bit that does. Bramble has — by analogy — the frontal lobe, and nothing else. Bramble has no model of itself. Bramble does not regulate. Bramble does not revise. Bramble is the part of the architecture that the Ghost in the Machine picture has photoshopped out. We are starting this lab, this quarter, at the part of the architecture that the most popular wrong worldview of personality conceals from itself."

She paused. She looked at Bart.

"I have, as some of you know, written a short story about this. It was published last year. It is about a couple who have installed four small switches on the back of each other's heads, one switch per typological dimension, and who use the switches to make each other into the people they want to be married to, and at the end of the story they have lost track of who they were. The story is about the Ghost in the Machine. I did not know that when I wrote it. I knew it last week."

Burns laughed. Marge did not laugh.

"That is bit one," Marge said. "There are three more. I will give them to you across the next three quarters."

She sat down.

Lisa took the floor. She did not stand up. She turned her body to face the room and put both palms flat on the table.

"I am going to be heavy for a stretch," she said. "I want to say that out loud first. The next twenty minutes are going to be science. I have been thinking about how to do this for a month. I do not have a clever way to do it. The way I have ended up with is to lay foundations. I am going to walk us down to the science underneath what we are doing, in the order it was discovered, so we know what we are standing on. The framework we are going to build across three years is going to sit on top of this foundation. The foundation is older than us. The foundation is older than the field of personality psychology. The foundation is, in the literal sense, older than life."

She picked up her bramble. She drank from it.

"Five things. Thermodynamics. Self-organization. Autopoiesis. The three-ratchet model. And what the Protocell is, in terms of all of them. I will go through them in order. I will stop and let you ask questions. Some of this you know. Some of it you do not."

She set the drink down.

"One. Thermodynamics. The second law says that closed systems tend toward higher entropy. The world, treated as a whole, is running toward heat-death. What life is — and what the bots are — is open systems that maintain themselves by importing low-entropy from the environment and exporting high-entropy to it. Prigogine called these *dissipative structures*. The Protocell pumps order inward and chaos outward. The cost of being a Protocell is the dumping of waste heat into the environment. Jasmine has been watching this happen on her kitchen floor for three months. The floor under each bot runs cooler than the floor around it. The bot is paying entropy. The bot is paying for its own existence. The second law is not violated. The second law is satisfied at the bot-plus-environment scale. The bot keeps itself in shape by running the floor down."

Jasmine said, "That is what the cooling is. I have been touching it for three months and I did not know what it was."

Lisa said, "You knew what it was. You knew it in the form of the question. You just did not have the answer."

Jasmine nodded.

Bart said, "Wait. I thought entropy was where things just fall apart. A clean room becomes a messy room. Nothing to do with heat."

Burns laughed the chest-laugh.

Lisa looked at Bart. Bart looked at the table.

"Two. Self-organization. Order emerging spontaneously from local interactions in systems far from equilibrium. Convection cells. Bénard instabilities. Storm systems. Chemistry organizes itself before life arrives. The Protocell is not the first self-organizing thing. The Protocell is the first self-organizing thing that can be inherited. That distinction is going to matter."

She gestured with both hands, palms flat, then palms vertical.

Bart said, "Sacred geometry."

Lisa said, "No."

"Hexagons in beehives because the math wants —"

"The math does not *want* anything. Sacred geometry is a *mystical interpretation* of pattern-formation. Self-organization is the mechanism. You can have one without the other. The bees do not make hexagons because hexagons are sacred. They make hexagons because hexagons pack."

Bart said, "Cool."

"Three. Autopoiesis. Maturana and Varela in nineteen seventy-two. Operational closure. The system makes its own components and defines its own boundary. The system's reactions build the membrane that holds the reactions in place. There is no external blueprint. The Protocell is the working chemical case. Hanczyc and Fujikawa and Szostak, two thousand and three, fatty-acid vesicles in the lab — vesicles that grow by absorbing more fatty acid, divide when mechanically stressed, and copy whatever replicating polymers happen to be inside them, all without anyone outside the system telling them how. The vesicle is doing, in a small chemical way, the same trick every cell in your body is doing right now. The trick is *making itself out of itself*."

She paused. She squinted at Bart. Bart had nothing clever to say.

"Four. The three-ratchet model. Terrence Deacon, *Incomplete Nature*, two thousand and twelve."

She reached into the bag at her feet and pulled out a zip tie. She handed it to Mira.

"Mira. Pull the strap end through the little square at the other end."

Mira pulled the strap through. The zip tie clicked, then again, tightening into a small loop in her hands.

"It does not go back."

"It does not. That is what a ratchet is. It goes one way. It clicks forward, it does not click back. Deacon argues that the climb from chemistry to life happens in three ratchets, each nested in the last. Once you have clicked each ratchet forward, you cannot click back."

"The first is *homeodynamic* — a system coupled to an external gradient, maintaining a steady state. The proton-motive force at an alkaline vent that Burns described is one. The second is *morphodynamic* — form-generating processes that build and maintain structure. Convection cells. Self-organizing chemistries. The third is *teleodynamic* — ends-directed processes. Self-maintenance becomes *about itself*. The system is now doing what it is doing *in order to keep doing it*. That is the moment life crosses a threshold."

Mira had been clicking the zip tie forward through the locking square — one tooth, then another, then another — and the small clicks ran underneath Lisa's last sentence like a metronome.

She continued.

"Five. The Protocell is the convergence point. At the Protocell — and this is what I want everyone to hold — all three ratchets are operating *at the same time*. The Protocell is homeodynamic because it is coupled to a gradient. The Protocell is morphodynamic because it forms its own boundary. The Protocell is teleodynamic because it maintains itself for its own sake. And — *and this is the part nothing before the Protocell had* — it can be inherited. The same constellation of three ratchets gets passed forward to descendants who can be selected on. Before the Protocell, chemistry happens. After the Protocell, evolution happens. The Protocell crossed what the field calls the *Darwinian threshold*. That crossing is the event that opens our entire three-year project. Everything else we will do is downstream of that crossing."

She paused. She drank from her bramble.

"The bots in the kitchen are not protocells. They are not made of chemistry. They are made of springs and Seebeck modules and a child's choice. But the architectural relationships are there in miniature. A wind-up spring is an energy source coupled to a gradient. The floor-air gradient is the dissipative coupling. The chassis is the boundary. The seeded variability is what selection has to work on. The household is the selector. The bots are a *household-legible compression* of real Protocell architecture. Bart built them with that compression in mind. We did not build the bots to *be* protocells. We built the bots to *render* protocells at a scale a four-year-old can carry."

She paused.

"I want to flag, before I close — for the record — that some of what I have just said is more settled than other parts of it. The thermodynamic foundation is settled. Autopoiesis is settled. Deacon's three-ratchet framing is a *synthesis* — it is one specific way of organizing what is known. Not every working biologist would frame it exactly this way. The Lane and Martin argument about alkaline vents is contested. The Mulkidjanian argument about surface ponds is contested. The exact site of the Darwinian crossing is unknown. We are working in a field where the foundational facts are not fully settled. I am flagging that now so we do not pretend, across the next three years, to have certainty we do not have. Where we have certainty, we will use it. Where we do not, we will say so."

She drank from her bramble.

"That is the floor I wanted to lay. What we are going to do across the next eleven quarters is build everything else that life has done on top of this. Regulation. Modeling. Revision. Nervous tissue. Brains. Language. All of that runs on the Protocell as substrate. The Protocell is the foundation. The rest is the trellis."

She paused. She did not look like she had meant to use that word.

"The rest is the trellis," she said again.

Marge said, "The rest is the trellis."

Bart said, "Trellis."

Burns said, "Trellis."

The team had landed on a metaphor without having meant to. The lab had its name — Marge had named the lab at the Pitch — but the metaphor had not yet been turned into a piece of theory vocabulary. It had been a thing on the patio out back. Now it was a thing on the table. The trellis was the structure the rest of it would grow up.

Lisa said, "OK. The rest of the meeting can be lighter. I am done."

She drank from her bramble. She had not realized how heavy the talk had been until she had reached the end of it.

Jasmine, who had been listening to all of it, said, "Can I ask the naive question."

Lisa said, "Please."

Jasmine said, "If LUCA already had all three of those things — the homeodynamic, the morphodynamic, the teleodynamic — what is it that the bots in my kitchen *don't* have, that the next quarter's bot will have."

"Because I have been listening," Jasmine said, "and I think what I am hearing is that LUCA already had a lot. She had the boundary. She had inheritance. The dynamics. Those are big things. Those are huge things. And what we have been calling the *founding* cell of this whole thing — it sounds like the founding cell already had most of what mattered. What does Prokaryote add that I cannot already see in this twitching thing in front of me."

## Reflection: What is Named?

A thermostat and a bacterium share a wiring diagram. Both sense. Both compare. Both act. The wiring is the cybernetic loop and it is in both of them. But trait-talk only goes to the bacterium. Nobody describes a thermostat as fastidious; people describe *E. coli* strains as fastidious all the time, in print, in peer-reviewed papers, without flagging that they are doing it. The difference is somewhere, and I have come to think the only honest way to find it is to look at the bacteria one at a time and see what is happening, and then turn at the end and look at a thermostat, and see what is not.

In the gut of a four-week-old human there is a bacterium called *Bifidobacterium infantis* which has, for the entirety of its evolutionary history, eaten one thing. The one thing is a class of complex sugars found only in human breast milk — the human-milk oligosaccharides, the HMOs — which are too elaborate for the infant herself to digest and which her own gut therefore passes through untouched. *B. infantis* picks them up. It has a specialized cluster of genes encoding the enzymes for every linkage type the HMOs come in: fucose, sialic acid, branched, unbranched. Yong calls it the consummate HMOvore. The word is right. There is no other word.

What is striking, when you think about it for a minute, is that the milk has these sugars in it *because the bacterium eats them.* Mother and microbe have been refining each other across a hundred million years of mammalian descent. The mother's milk has the sugars because, somewhere upstream in evolutionary time, the bacterium was selected for being able to eat them. The bacterium is part of the milk in the sense that the milk would not have these sugars in it if the bacterium were not there to eat them. They have been working on each other in the dark.

The four-week-old does not know any of this. She is asleep on her mother's chest. Her gut is doing the work.

There is a bacterium that can survive five thousand grays of ionizing radiation. The dose that will reliably kill a human is about five. The dose that will kill *E. coli* is about sixty. The bacterium I am describing — *Deinococcus radiodurans* — takes the five thousand. When its DNA is shattered into a few hundred fragments, it reassembles itself across about twelve hours and resumes business. The people who study it call it Conan the Bacterium. They publish papers in which the abstract refers to Conan. The naming is, I think, the part of the situation I want to register first. People who spend their working lives on a thing do not give the thing a name unless the thing has done something to them.

What it has done to them is exceed their expectations across an absurd gap. Nothing should be able to do what *Deinococcus* does. The genome is not mysterious — the repair enzymes are catalogued, the radical-scavenging is understood, the redundant chromosome copies make sense in retrospect. But knowing how the machine works does not make the machine less astonishing, and astonishment in working biologists comes out as nicknames. Conan. Conan the Bacterium. Conan to his friends. The naming is the punctuation on the gap between what the cell can do and what the cell has any business being able to do.

In ponds and shallow sediments around the world there are bacteria that have built compasses inside themselves. The compasses are not metaphorical. They are physical: chains of small magnetite crystals — magnetosomes — strung along the inside of the cell like beads on a wire, oriented so that the chain's net magnetic moment aligns with the planet's magnetic field. When the bacterium swims, the chain pulls its body into alignment with the field lines. The cell follows the field down toward sediment, which is where it wants to be, because it is anaerobic and the sediment is anaerobic and the open water is not.

I have not gotten over this. The cell does not have a compass. The cell *is*, in part, a compass. The chain of crystals is grown by the bacterium out of iron available in the surrounding water, in a precise sequence, in a precise location, under cellular control. There are genes for the magnetosome membrane, genes for the iron-importing channels, genes for the crystal orientation. The bacterium has, in the engineering sense, manufactured a sensor — and the sensor is itself a piece of the bacterium's body. The bacterium does not consult the compass. It tilts.

I think about this when I have lost the path on a trail and reach for a phone.

In the soil of arid grasslands across western North America there is a bacterium called *Sodalis praecaptivus*. It is free-living. It walks around — to the extent that bacteria walk around — in the way of any soil microbe, doing soil-microbe things, mostly metabolizing whatever organic molecules are nearby. What is unusual about it is what is in its genome that it is not currently using. *Sodalis* carries the complete molecular toolkit for becoming a permanent insect endosymbiont. The genes for host-cell invasion are there. The genes for evading the insect immune system are there. The genes for the metabolic exchange a long-term endosymbiosis requires — supplying the host with amino acids the host cannot synthesize, in exchange for shelter — are there. Its closest relatives are already insect endosymbionts. They have made the crossing. They have lost most of their other genes, because once inside an insect for a few million years a bacterium does not need most of its other genes. *Sodalis praecaptivus* still has all of them. It is, in Yong's phrase, the symbiont-in-waiting. It is walking around in the soil with the keys to a kingdom it has not entered.

The question, of course, is what KIND of thing this readiness is. It is not a plan; the bacterium has no plans. It is not a memory; there is nothing in *Sodalis* that has experienced what it is ready for. It is not a hope. It is more accurate to say that the readiness is a description of the bacterium — a description of what is there, in its DNA, available to be expressed under the right conditions. The conditions may never arise. The bacterium will reproduce into uncountable descendants, the vast majority of which will never see the inside of an insect. The readiness will persist anyway. It will persist because losing it costs nothing, while keeping it leaves open a door that, in evolutionary time, has been worth keeping open often enough to maintain the genes. The lineage has a posture. The posture is forward. The individual cell has no idea.

There is something tender about this and I am not going to try to talk myself out of it.

I keep a thermostat on the wall of the room I am writing this in, and I have just taken it down off the wall to look at. It is a small white plastic object — a sensor, a comparator, a relay, a wire. It can be set to maintain the room at sixty-eight degrees and it does so. When the room cools to sixty-six, the relay closes and the heat comes on; when it warms to seventy, the relay opens. By every component-level measure, the thermostat has what the bacteria above have. It senses. It compares. It acts. It has a goal-state. The cybernetic loop is intact.

I have been holding it for two minutes now and I do not feel about it the way I feel about *Sodalis*. I want to be honest about this rather than rhetorical. I am not going to claim the thermostat is somehow less interesting than the magnetotactic bacterium because of vitalism or because of mystery. It is something more specific than that. The thermostat has no parameter that has been refined by its predecessors not working. The thermostat has no posture. The thermostat does not contain a description of its environment that has accumulated across generations. It was manufactured in 2007. There are tens of millions of them in the world. They were all installed at once and they will all be replaced at once and the variation among them is engineering variation, not evolutionary variation.

Under the history there is a plainer thing, and I keep arriving at it last because it is so plain I walk past it. Somebody made the thermostat. There was a day — I am inventing the particular day, but there was one — when a hand that was not the thermostat's closed its case, and after that day it was finished. Nothing in this room made itself. The lamp did not, the chair did not, the glass in the window was floated flat in a factory and trucked up the canyon. I am the only thing in here that made itself. And the thing I have been thinking about all evening — the one in the gut of the sleeping four-week-old, the one in the mud growing a compass a crystal at a time — is another kind, and when you actually start to count the kinds, there are not very many of them. The cell had no case closed for it. It closed its own, and is closing it now, out of whatever is lying nearby, and was closing it a second ago, and will go on closing it until the day it cannot.

This is the part the cybernetic loop is so good at hiding that I had stopped seeing it. Both of them sense and compare and act; I have given them that. But one of them was assembled and the other is assembling, present tense, with no hands on it — the assembling not a thing it did once and had done, but the very thing that is keeping it alive this second. The thermostat was finished in 2007. The cell is never finished; it is making itself right now, out in the dark, while I sit here holding the one machine in the room that never will.

I do not want to say the thermostat has no biography. I want to say it has a spec sheet, and the genre that fits a thermostat is the spec sheet, and the genre that fits a bacterium is something else. The genres did not arise to ornament the things they describe; they arose to fit them. The bacterium gets the strain report because it is the kind of thing a strain report fits. The thermostat gets the spec sheet because it is the kind of thing a spec sheet fits.

I am going to put the thermostat back on the wall now.
