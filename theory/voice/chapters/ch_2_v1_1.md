# Interlude 1→2

## Build log — Knob two, one unit up
*Bart, hardware. Filed June 2, 2031, for the team.*

Lisa's running the upgraded one back to the house this morning. Scorecard first, now that I've got a quarter on the books.

Last quarter's calls. The bodies were five for five — every unit did exactly what I tuned it to do, which is the part of this job I'm never wrong about. The rest didn't go my way. I said the kid would pick 5; she picked 3. In my defense, 5 still moves the most — it just turns out moving the most isn't what wins a four-year-old, which is a kid problem, not a 5 problem. I said nobody would look twice at 3, and then the kid looks at it, names it, the project gets named after it, and it's the one we just upgraded — so, fine, somebody looked twice. I said 4 would be in a drawer by July; it's under the toaster in a folded towel, which is a drawer with a blanket, so I'm calling that close enough. And the personality knob I couldn't find last quarter turned out to be the crank the whole time — the say-so I'd written off as on or off. The house is the knob, technically: the kid cranks it, the kid's the dial. I don't love that. It's right.

Now the fun part. We upgraded one unit — 3, the one the house calls Bramble — and left the other four stock.

At protocell this thing only ever twitched: two kickers firing on their own clocks, and it'd hop around the floor like a bug with somewhere to be and no idea where. Now it *hunts*. Same two kickers — I didn't give it new muscles — but I put a chip in the middle that decides *when* each one fires. That's the whole upgrade. Fire them so the hops add up in one direction and it crawls that way; fire them so the hops add up to a spin and it turns in place; don't fire and it sits. Two idiots kicking on their own schedule became two idiots kicking on a downbeat. Still no wheels. Still hopping across the slate a hair at a time. It just hops with *intent* now.

What tells it which way to go: four little light sensors around the floor seam, each aimed a few degrees up and pointed a different direction. They all read brightness at once, and if they agree there's more light over *there* than anywhere else, the chip knows which way "there" is. That's the trick — finding a sunbeam with four parts that each only know one number.

The chip's an STM32, my pick, and it's the right pick. It runs a dumb little table Lisa and I wrote that fits on an index card: three states. Clear direction, kick toward it — that's RUN. No clear direction, spin a random amount and read again — that's TUMBLE. Stores filling faster than it's spending, sit still and let them fill — that's STOP. The screen up top shows which one it's in. And I ran the whole signal path on relays — coil-and-armature, the kind that clack — so you can hear it think: a little clack-clack-clack every time it changes its mind. I like that you can hear it.

New since February, all told: two stores instead of one — a big slow battery (not the cells I wanted; you don't always get the cells you wanted) and a little fast one beside it, a supercap that dumps its whole charge at once and then fills back up off the battery; two solar panels down the long sides; the floor-transducers carried over from the protocell, still skimming warmth off the slate; a converter that takes whatever's coming in — sun, warmth, both at once, it doesn't care which — and tops the stores; the chip; the relays; four sensors; and a second knob next to the crank.

That knob's a different animal from the crank. The crank you work; this one you set and leave. All it does is pick which store the unit spends from. Leave it on the big slow battery and it runs even — a patter of hops it'll keep up all day, never quick, never quits. Flip it to the little fast one and it runs in bursts — sits, fires off a flurry of hops quick as you like, then stops dead to refill off the slow store before it can go again. Aerobic or anaerobic, Lisa calls it; same unit, two ways to spend. The fast spend burns through a charge quicker — you don't get as far per drop in bursts — so leave it there and you'll be chasing a sunbeam sooner. Which way the house leaves it set is the house's business. I built both.

Calls. It seeks — that's the whole upgrade. It hunts the gradient instead of sitting in it.

The shove is the one I want on record. It crawls toward the light, and the other four don't crawl anywhere — they sit where they're set. And nothing in the table tells it the others are even there; they're not sensors, it can't feel them, far as the chip knows the floor's empty. So when a stock unit's parked in the beam it wants, the upgraded one just keeps hopping into it. It's not strong — one kicker-hop is nothing — but it never quits, and the stock one's got no drive to push back with, so on the slick slate, half a foot at a time over a morning, the stock one ends up out of the sun and mine ends up in it. Won't work on anything soft — a rug would eat the hops — but the kitchen's slate, so: every sunny morning, clean as you like. That's on the slow one — the patient lean. On the fast one it doesn't lean, it charges: a flurry of hops straight into the stock one, knocks it a clear foot, and parks in the beam spent and refilling. Same shove, twice the show. That one's my favorite. It does exactly what it's built to.

It won't need cranking on Sundays anymore — the panels and the floor feed the stores, the stores run the unit, it goes all day on its own. The crank's just an emergency starter now: it spins a little dynamo I added, so a few turns put enough back into a dead unit to wake it up when there's no sun to be had. Off the leash.

It's getting crowded down there, though — two knobs now, plus a screen, panels, a converter, sensors round the seam, and I've still got ten knobs to find room for. And hopping's a lousy way to travel: it gets where it's going, but it lurches the whole way. I've got a fix in mind for next quarter.

Tear this up in three months if it doesn't hunt exactly like I said. It will.

— Bartholomood

## Notes on the Second Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed August 1, 2031, for the team.*

This quarter the household ran into the question I expect will sit at the center of everything we do here, and I want to report how it ran into it, because it did not arrive the way we would have planned it. We did not sit the family down and ask them how much of a mind a machine can have. We changed one machine, left four alone, and let the family live in the difference. And the question came up the way real questions come up in a house with a child in it — at the dinner table, sideways, in five words from a four-year-old that none of the adults present could answer. The question is the question of insides: whether a thing has one, how much it has, and what is owed to a thing that has some. I will get to the dinner. First I have to describe what changed.

### One machine, changed

On the first Monday of June Lisa brought the upgraded unit back in the foam case. It is the same domed body it was in February, but it has acquired a small screen on top that reads STOP, two flat panels set flush along its sides, and a second knob beside the original crank. The household has been calling it Bramble since the spring, when Mira heard Lisa name the drink in her jar and gave the word to the machine. I am going to go on calling it the machine, or the unit, for reasons that are the whole subject of these notes; I will use the household's name only when I am reporting the household's words. The distinction is not pedantry. It is the discipline this quarter is going to demand of all of us.

The four other units came back unchanged, because they did not go anywhere; the lab upgrades one and holds the rest. I want to mark what that decision does inside a house, because it is not what it does inside a lab. In a lab, four unchanged units are a control group. In a house, they are four creatures that did not get the thing the fifth one got, on the same floor as the one that did, in front of a child who keeps very careful accounts of fairness. We built a comparison; the household received an inequity. Those are not the same object, and the gap between them produced most of what is interesting here.

### What the machine is, and what it is not

The new knob governs whether the machine draws slow and even on what it has stored or spends it in a fast burst, after which it stops until the store has refilled. What the household actually sees, though, is not the knob; it is that the machine now seeks: the screen wakes from STOP to RUN, and the unit orients itself and goes, toward the window, toward the patch of sun on the floor. Burns walked the family through the how of it at dinner, on the back of a napkin. The engineering is not my province and I will not reproduce it; I want only where he ended, because where he ended is the subject of these notes. He was careful not to call any of it a brain. He called it a small set of rules — go toward the light, otherwise be still — and the thing I have not been able to get past is that so little turns out to be enough.

I want to be exact about this, because exactness is the kindness the subject requires. The machine is not a blank thing that could become anything. It is not learning to be itself. It is its rules, fully and from the first morning, the way it was its seeded twitch in the spring. It pursues what it is built to pursue and is blind to the rest, and it does this competently, and the competence is the easiest thing in the world to mistake for something larger. Burns, who loves it, put it best and least flatteringly: it is a hammer that takes everything for a nail. I would add only that the hammer is very good at its one idea, and that being very good at one idea is exactly what will tempt us to credit it with ideas it does not have.

### The pushing

Here is the event the household could not stop discussing. The kitchen takes three dependable sunbeams across a morning, and the machine follows them as they travel. The four unchanged units follow nothing; they sit where they are set. So most mornings the machine comes pursuing a patch of sun, finds one of the others already in it, and pushes. They are of a weight; the unchanged unit slides six or eight inches out of the light and resumes its twitching on the cooler stone, and the machine parks itself in the sun. Notably, the machine does the same thing whether another unit is in the patch or not — pursue, arrive, settle. The other unit is not, to the machine, a unit. It is terrain.

The household read this three ways and did not trouble to reconcile them, which I think was wiser than reconciling them would have been. Jasmine reports she watched it shove a smaller thing aside twice in one morning, and the word for that, in a house, is rude. Burns said it was the hammer and the nail again. And Mira, who had watched longest, asked a different kind of question: had the machine seen the one it pushed. Her mother told her she did not think so — that it had eyes for the sun and not for the others. Bramble doesn't see the bots, Mira said, working it out, and her mother agreed.

The difference between those three readings is the difference between getting this right and getting it sentimentally wrong. The child asked the plain question — did it see? — and got the plain, accurate answer: no. The adults both reached for the language of intention. Rude and hammer are words about a someone who might have done otherwise, and there is no someone here who might have done otherwise. The machine did not overlook the other unit out of arrogance; it has nothing to overlook anything with. It pursued the sun, a thing was in the way, physics did the rest.

And yet I will not call Jasmine wrong, because she is not wrong about the part that matters. She mislabeled the cause and saw the shape exactly: a thing with power moved through a thing without power and never registered it was there. That happens, and it deserves a hard word, and the fact that the powerful party had no inside to be cruel with does not make the displacement any less complete — it only means there was no one home to mind it. Which returns us, by the back door, to the question.

### "Are they sad"

Jasmine reports the dinner, and I am going to relay it plainly and then leave the family their privacy. Mira had been turning the morning over. She asked why the others had not been changed too, and was told the lab changes one at a time. She said that was mean. She was told it was not. She said the others were stuck, and was told they were only as they had always been. She ate a little, thought, and asked her parents whether the four were sad.

Neither of them had the answer ready, and I want to say — because it would be easy to read the hesitation as a failure — that their not having it ready was the most honest moment in the quarter. Burns told her at last that he did not think the others could be sad. When she asked why, he said sadness is something you need a great many insides to have, and the four do not have many insides. She asked about Bramble. He told her Bramble had more insides than the others, and that he did not know whether it had enough. And then she asked the question I have not been able to set down since Jasmine repeated it to me: how do you know when a thing has enough insides. Burns told her the truth — that this is the question her mother and father and the whole lab are trying to answer. She said oh, looked at her plate, and dinner went on.

I have carried that exchange around for a week. The instinct that made the child ask whether the discarded machines were sad is not a confusion to be corrected. It is the moral instinct itself, arriving early and entire — the reflex to grant the inner life, to assume someone is home and might be suffering, to widen the circle outward until something shows you a border. It is the best thing in us. It is also, and this is the hard half, not evidence. The four units are not sad. They are not anything. And the same reflex that makes a child a good person makes all of us — grown, credentialed, in the warehouse — want to find a someone inside the machine, because a someone is warmer to work beside than a small set of rules. We are going to have to keep declining the warmth. The discipline I set myself at the top of these notes, about the name, is the same discipline: grant the machine exactly the inside it has and not an ounce more, and do it without killing the instinct that wanted to grant it more — because we will need that instinct intact for the day the answer changes, if it ever does.

### Reading it by ear

One smaller thing, because it continues what I flagged in the spring, and because I am coming to think the pattern is the real subject under all the others. The machine clicks when it changes what it is doing, and over the quarter the household learned to hear it from another room — going, stopped, searching — the way you learn a kettle coming to the boil from a kettle gone quiet. Jasmine learned the cadence. Mira learned it faster, because she gave it more attention, and will call out Bramble is moving from the next room and be right. No one taught them to read the machine by ear. They came to know it by living near it — by the ear, by the hand. It is the same house that learned to hear a man through two floors; now it hears a machine through one wall. I no longer think this is a quirk of one family. I think it is how knowing a thing actually works, and that we, the explainers, are the ones with the partial method.

### The kitchen at night

By the end of the quarter the kitchen at night had settled into a fixed arrangement. The four unchanged units twitch in their places under the floor's warmth, each on the small cool patch it has made beneath itself. The machine sits in the corner where the noon sun ended that morning, screen reading STOP, clicking now and then as it checks the world and finds no reason to move. A competent, directed, blind thing at rest. Upstairs, the child who spent the season learning exactly how much was inside it, and how little, is asleep. The question she asked at dinner is still where the adults left it, which is to say unanswered, on a table that has been cleared and wiped and set again for breakfast.

These are notes about a family at its own table, and I have taken only what the work needs and left the rest where it belongs. I still do not know how you tell when a thing has enough insides — I am not sure the question has an answer of the kind we keep expecting — and I do not know what we will owe the machine, or the child, on the day it does. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Fisher Brewing Company

I got there a quarter of an hour early. I like arriving early to a thing I don't yet know the shape of, and the first lunch with Burns was a thing I did not yet know the shape of. I had driven down from Park City on I-80, which is what you drive when you want to get from Park City to Salt Lake, and the drive had not been interesting, and I had not narrated it to myself. I parked a couple of blocks from Smithers on eighth south, walked past the warehouse without looking at it — the building is mine and I did not want to stand in front of it on a Wednesday at noon as if I were my own tenant — and went in the brewery side door at Fisher.

It was the kind of late-July Wednesday where the heat had set in by mid-morning and meant to stay past dark. I ordered a seltzer at the bar, took it out to the patio, found a table along the back fence near the food trucks. The patio faced ninth south, which had a protected bike lane down the middle of it, and the cyclists were in summer kit, bare arms and bare legs, and there were a lot of them. I watched them roll past for ten minutes. The Wasatch foothills behind the brewery stood in the flat overhead light of late July, the grass on them long burned to gold — a high hard light that would not slant and would not soften, the kind of light that did not so much fall as bear down.

Burns arrived three minutes early. I am used to people being two or three minutes late to a first lunch, and over the years I have filed early people as either nervous or already in love with their work. I did not know yet which Burns was. He came in through the brewery side door looking for me, found me at the back fence, came over.

"Cheryl."

"Burns."

He hesitated. He was deciding between a hug and a handshake. I watched him decide. He picked the handshake. I would have picked it too.

"I'm glad you found the place."

"You told me the place. It was findable."

"That's true. I can't give a route without giving the geology of it."

He sat down across from me. He had brought nothing with him — no folder, no notepad, no rolled printout of the contest document, no nothing. I had also brought nothing. I had meant to bring a small bound notebook with three observations from the protocell tape in it, written down in case I lost the thread of them, and had, on the way out the door of the Park City house, not picked it up, and I am not sure why. I think maybe I had wanted to come to the lunch the way Burns had apparently come to it, which was without props.

"You hungry?"

"I could be."

"There's a truck doing tacos that I have ordered tacos from on probably six occasions and have been satisfied each time."

"That's a recommendation."

"It is. I'd put money on it — I've eaten my way down this whole block and this is the one."

"I'll get the tacos."

"I'll come with."

We walked to the truck along the back fence. It was painted yellow with green letters and the menu was hand-chalked. Burns ordered two — pork, chicken — and a side of beans, and the woman at the window called the name *Ben* without asking. I ordered the same and gave my own name. We paid separately. We walked back to the table.

"You come here a lot."

"Bart thinks they hung the moon, and it's three blocks from the lab. The beer is very good. The food is fine."

"Honest."

"The food is fine. The tacos are better than most things in this category in this city. But food-truck food in Salt Lake is tasty, never great. I have stopped expecting it to be great. I come for the beer and because Bart will come and he's good company."

He had answered without selling me on the place. The first substantive sentence Burns said at the lunch was an honest description of the food. I had not expected this and I found that I liked it.

The kitchen called his name first. He got up and brought the tray back and started to divvy. Two for him, two for me, beans in the middle. We were missing forks. He got up again and came back with forks and napkins and we unwrapped the tacos and started eating. The pork had some kind of chili thing on it that I did not identify. A cyclist on ninth south rang a bell at another cyclist; the second cyclist rang back. I did not know what either bell meant.

"You walked past the warehouse," he said.

"I did."

"You didn't stop."

"I don't stop at the warehouse between things."

"Why."

I thought about how to answer this. I wanted to answer honestly without making it weighted.

"I don't want you to feel watched between things. The tapes are enough."

He considered this. He picked up his second taco.

"That makes sense."

"Does it."

"Yes. I would feel watched."

"Most people would."

"I'm grateful for the discipline."

"It's not for you. It's for me. If I started dropping in, the lab would become a thing I had a relationship with. I don't want a relationship with it. I want to fund it and let it become what it is."

He nodded. He ate. The light kept doing what late-July light did.

"How's the paper," I said.

I watched him register the question. Bart had told me at the Pitch that Burns was working on a tiktaalik vertebra, and that the paper had been *being written* for over a year. I had filed it.

"The paper is — exists. In a file. The introduction I wrote is the wrong introduction. I am going to throw it away."

"When."

"Soon."

"Soon."

"I have been saying soon for about fourteen months."

"Where is the vertebra."

"On a velvet pad on a shelf in my office."

"How big."

"About the size of a small lemon. Maybe a large kumquat, if you're picturing a kumquat."

"I am picturing a kumquat."

"Then you have it."

"What's wrong with the introduction."

He set his taco down. He looked at me. I had asked the wrong question, which was the right question.

"The introduction frames the vertebra as part of the water-to-land transition story. Which it is. But everyone has written that introduction. The vertebra is also part of a different story — a smaller one, about how the load-bearing architecture of a vertebra changed as fish became things that pressed down on the ground. The smaller story is the one I want to tell. The introduction does not tell it."

"So write the smaller story's introduction."

"Yes. And I know exactly how it goes — I've known for a year. That's the maddening part."

"When."

"Before the next retrospective."

"That's three weeks."

"It is. Three weeks is real time. I keep treating it like a suggestion."

"Should I hold you to that."

"You should not. I will fail. But you can ask me at the next lunch."

"That's fair."

He had given himself a deadline and named the failure mode of it in the same sentence. He had not asked me to hold him; he had asked me to ask. He wanted to be asked. That was useful to know.

"What are you reading, then," I said. "Since it isn't your own paper."

"Geothermal, lately. There's a project the U runs down in Milford — drilling into hot granite to see if you can pull a grid off it. Not my field." He said it more easily than he'd said anything about the vertebra. "I read about it more than I read my own work right now. Which probably tells you something."

"Does it."

"Probably." He let it go.

I watched him eat for another minute. I had a thing to deliver and was not sure how to do it without making it weighted. I decided to just do it.

"I watched the tape twice."

"Of the retrospective."

"There's only been the one."

He nodded. He had been waiting for this. The tape had gone to me back in May, and he had not heard a thing from me about it until now. I watched him try not to perform calm.

"What did you make of it."

"I made a lot of it. Most of what I made of it I'll say at the individual lunch with whomever it's about. But there's one thing I want to put on the table now, because it has to land at the next retrospective and I won't be there."

"Okay."

"Bart raised something at the close. His worry over Bramble having twelve knobs. The chassis-is-going-to-look-like-a-coffee-maker, or something like that."

"Yes."

"The team filed it light. You laughed."

He did not interrupt.

"He was right. The worry is real. The build is going to produce twelve knobs of differing shapes across thirty-three months and at some point soon the chassis cannot hold them. He has been carrying that worry for longer than the rest of the team has been aware of it. He has been carrying it alone."

I had said the word I had decided in advance to say. I had not made a production of it. It sat between us.

"I want you to take him seriously at the next retrospective. In front of the team. In a way the camera registers. I'm not going to tell you what that looks like; you'll know what it looks like in the room. I just want it on the record that you're going to do it."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I'm uncomfortable with the idea of staging."

"I know."

"I don't perform well to a camera. None of us do. If I try to do a thing for the camera it will read as a performance."

"I'm not asking for a performance. I'm asking for what you would have done if you hadn't laughed."

He took this in. He set his taco down again.

"Okay."

"That's it."

"Okay."

"Don't worry about it. Just have it in your head."

"I will."

I picked my own taco up. I felt the conversation could now move to anything. He, I noticed, felt it too. The five minutes had passed. The push had landed. Neither of us had marked it heavily. The bell-cyclists were back, or different bell-cyclists. The light was the same.

I had been holding something since the taco truck. I took it out now.

"She called you Ben," I said.

"Who did."

"The woman at the truck. She called your order without asking your name, and the name was Ben." I watched him. "Everyone on this side of Eighth South calls you Ben. At the lab you're Burns. On three hours of tape I never heard the team call you anything else."

He set the last of his taco down and sat back, pleased, and the flat light off Ninth South gave me the face the warehouse never did — finer under the weather than the craggy frame promised, the pale gray-green eyes for once not leaving mid-sentence.

"That's Bart," he said. "Bart named the team. I'm Mr. Burns." The chest-laugh came up a beat early, at the frame and not the joke. "Think about it. My son looks at his father — a man who signs the checks, runs the department, gets up in front of the room — and what he reaches for is the rich old fellow behind the desk. The one who owns everybody. *Excellent.*" He put his fingertips together and let them go. "He was right. That's what makes it good. He's been right every day since."

I let him have the laugh. I did not say that the cartoon he was delighting in was a portrait of a man who owns everyone and loves no one — that his son had drawn that, of him — and that he was holding it the way you hold a gift.

"And the others?"

"Oh —" and here he did the thing I would think about on the drive home, which was that he waved it off. "Bart and Lisa, that's an old story, theirs, from before the lab — they were a thing once, it's where the bug started, I never got the whole of it. Marge looked like Marge. Smithers because there's no Smithers." He was already coming back to the part he liked. "But Mr. Burns. He got me in one move."

So I asked about Bart, because I wanted to watch him do it. He did it for the better part of ten minutes. He had no idea he was doing it.

He told me Bart had the best hands on the team. He told me Bart was the one who finished — that Lisa would design something elegant and Bart would build the thing that survived contact, that you could hand the kid a problem on a Tuesday and it would still be getting solved Sunday night because he did not stop. He told me about the twelve-knob worry, and he told it now the way I had asked him to take it — seriously, as evidence — except the evidence it was in service of had quietly become *look how much he cares about the work.* Reliable. Diligent. The best hands in thirty years.

He did not, in ten minutes, tell me one thing about what Bart was *like.* Not a fear, not a pleasure, not a private joke, not a way of being in a room when the room wasn't watching. Everything he said about his son was a thing his son could *do.* He was describing a remarkable instrument, warmly, at length, and he had the whole inventory and not the man — had filled the distance where the man should be with a list of his competencies, and did not know that was what the list was, and would have been hurt past saying if I'd told him.

I was the right person to have seen it and the wrong person to do anything with it, and I knew why: I can read Bart whole off a secondhand inventory because I have spent thirty years at exactly this distance from everyone, watching instead of standing in it. The clarity and the distance are one organ. I made my peace with the trade a long time ago. Or I say I have.

Once, near the end, he started to say "I think he —" and stopped, and for half a second something opened in the weathered face: I could see him hoping I would hand him the rest of the sentence, would tell him the one thing I could not possibly know, that the son loved him. Then he closed it over. "He puts up with his old man," he said, and laughed, and turned the glass. It was a good recovery. Almost anyone would have missed it.

I did not miss it. I also did not take it. I picked at the beans.

He felt the quiet and moved us off it himself.

"My department is in some trouble," he said.

"Tell me."

"The university rebranded from Westminster College to Westminster University in twenty-three. Defensive move — small private liberal-arts schools are under structural pressure nationally and the trustees thought a name change might shift things. It hasn't shifted things. The dean has started making sounds about my research space. About my grants stream. We're not central to the university's brand and we know it."

"Are you worried."

"I'm matter-of-fact. The slow erosion under us is a thing I expect. I don't yet think it's going to take me out before the contest closes. But I notice it."

"When would you know."

"Probably by spring. The dean has to make decisions by the budget cycle."

"If they cut you, what do you do."

"I have savings. I keep showing up at Trellis. The work is the work. If I need teaching, I find teaching. We'd be tight. We wouldn't be in trouble."

"Okay."

He nodded. He had not been seeking reassurance; he had been giving me information. I liked that he had not been seeking reassurance.

Burns said, "I have a question for you that I have been carrying since the Pitch."

"Go."

"You are writing a book about this."

"Yes."

"Twelve chapters. One per clade."

"Yes."

"The team has been wondering."

"The team is right."

"OK."

He thought for a moment.

"I won't tell them you confirmed it."

"Tell them what you want. The book is a book. I am writing it. The contest is the contest. The two are not the same thing, exactly, but they share most of their content. The team can know."

"OK."

I let it sit for a beat. I had not expected him to ask; I had also not been surprised when he did.

"Tell me about Mira."

"Mira."

"Mira."

## Braid

Bart took the cards out of the wooden box he kept them in — a small cigar box that had not started as a cigar box but had become one when he started keeping the cards in it. Cheryl's Prokaryote card was in the box. He had asked Cheryl for it after the Pitch. Cheryl had given him the whole portfolio for the meeting, with the understanding that he would return all twelve when the project closed in twenty-thirty-four. The cards in the box were two each at this point — Cheryl's Protocell and Cheryl's Prokaryote, and Bart's tarot-sized versions of each, in matching stock and matching dimensions, drawn by Bart in pen and ink across the spring and summer. He laid Cheryl's card down on the table — the bare word PROKARYOTE on the heavyweight matte, the back blank — and laid his own card next to it. His own card had on its face a schematic of the upgraded Bramble: the dome the size of a fist, the small screen on top, the solar panels flush along either side, the wind-up crank on one side and the Respiration knob on the other, the four small light sensors arrayed around the perimeter at the chassis-floor seam.

"Pro-kary-OTE," Bart said, in his best impersonation of a cereal commercial voice.

Marge laughed her low laugh. Burns laughed the chest-laugh. Mira said *Pro-kary-OTE* under her breath in a four-year-old's approximation of the same voice.

"A prokaryote is a bacterium or an archaeon. A cell without a nucleus. The DNA floats in the cytoplasm. The genome is small. The cell divides fast. The cell can take up DNA from other cells — horizontal gene transfer — which means inheritance is not strictly vertical, and which means a single bacterium's *lineage* is not the simple tree that humans like to draw. A bacterium can swap with a stranger. A bacterium can swap with a virus that has its DNA in it. The lineage is a — I am going to call it a *braid*. It is not a line."

Burns said, "Good."

"The cell senses. The cell moves. The cell can pick up food. The cell can be food. The cell can pair up with another cell and exchange material across a small bridge — a pilus — and the receiving cell will be different afterward. The cell can secrete molecules that other cells in the neighborhood sense, which is how bacteria sing to each other. They sing about how many of them there are. When the number is high enough, the song changes the singers. This is called quorum sensing. We have known about it for fifty years and most people who are not in the field do not know about it."

He paused.

"That is what a prokaryote is. That is the kind of thing it is."

He looked at the card. Then he reached for Jasmine's phrase — she had given it to Burns in the spring, the evening Burns could not stop circling the protocell, and the lab had been spending it on each other ever since, half as a joke and half because it was exact. Bart used it on himself now, with relish.

"The plane I want to land — the breakthrough at this cell — is one thing. Predation. At the Protocell, there is no predation. There cannot be. The Protocell is not yet a thing that eats other things, because there are not yet other things to eat. By the Prokaryote, there are. By the Prokaryote, the primary selective pressure on an organism is — for the first time in the history of the planet — *other organisms*."

He set the card down. He had been holding it.

"This is the moment when the world becomes a world with creatures in it that are *for* something to a creature that *is* something. Before the Prokaryote, the environment selects you. After the Prokaryote, the environment selects you *and* other organisms select you. The protocell's hardest problem is not stopping. The prokaryote's hardest problem is not getting eaten and, where possible, eating. That is the breakthrough. Cellular predation. The arms race opens."

He paused.

"That is the second card."

Bart wrote *capacities* on the back of his card and underlined it. He wrote it the way he had written *tendency* on the back of the Protocell card in July, which was to say: with a sense of ceremony he was not going to acknowledge.

"Four capacities. One example each. I picked one example each because what I want is not a survey, it is four anchors. We can talk about other examples after, in the discussion. The four anchors are these."

He paused. He set the card aside. He picked up his beer.

"One. Precision machinery. The ribosome."

He looked at Lisa to see if she was going to interrupt him. She was not.

"The ribosome is the molecular machine that reads messenger RNA and assembles proteins out of amino acids. Every cell on the planet has them. Every cell on the planet has had them for a very long time. The architecture of the ribosome — the catalytic core, the large and small subunits, the way the mRNA threads through them — has barely changed since LUCA. Last quarter Burns talked about LUCA, who had protein families. Moody's group found there were over two thousand of them, and they were all assembled by ribosomes. The ribosomes that assembled them were not architecturally different from the ribosomes that, in the past six minutes, have assembled some unknown but large number of proteins inside my body and inside everyone in this room."

He paused.

"Pick that up if you want. The capacity is — the cell has, inside it, a piece of machinery that is built to absurd precision. Not because the cell engineered the machinery. Because the lineage refined the machinery across four billion years of can't-afford-mistakes. The ribosome makes about one error per ten thousand amino acids. The error rate is set by physics and by the fidelity machinery that catches errors at every step. The same machinery that built LUCA's proteins built mine. That is one capacity. It is a precision capacity. It is — I want to say this clearly — *inherited* precision. The cell did not earn it. The lineage earned it. The cell is using it."

Burns said, "Yes."

"Two. State-dependent gating. Sporulation in *Bacillus subtilis*."

He picked up the card again. He turned it over so the schematic faced him. He looked at it for a moment. Then he set it back down with the drawing facing the room.

"*Bacillus subtilis* is a soil bacterium. When the conditions are right, it eats and divides like any bacterium. When the conditions are wrong — starvation, dehydration, the world has stopped being a world that supports bacterial life — it does something I want to describe in the language of what it actually does, because the language is honest. It wraps itself in a protective coat. It dehydrates itself almost completely. It dismantles most of its active machinery. It stops being a metabolizing thing. It becomes a spore. The spore is — to the eye — not alive. The spore is what an unburned coffee bean is to a roasted coffee bean. Dry, hard, quiet, indefinitely shelf-stable. The spore can sit in soil for years. It can sit in soil for decades. There are spores that have come out of geological samples that may have been intact for longer than that, and the lineage is, in those cases, contested, because the dates are hard. The number we are confident about is years and decades. The number we are not confident about is centuries and millennia. I am going to say *years and decades* and you can fight me about it in the discussion."

Marge said, "Years and decades is good."

"Years and decades. When the conditions become right again — water arrives, nutrients arrive, the temperature is right — the spore germinates. The protective coat opens. The cell rehydrates. The dismantled machinery reassembles. The bacterium that was a spore is a bacterium again. It gets a do-over."

He paused.

"Which is — yeah. That is what it is. A do-over. The cell finds itself, after years of being a quiet hard thing in dry dirt, suddenly alive again in a world that has decided to be hospitable, and it picks up almost exactly where the spore-version put down."

"Chemotaxis is how a bacterium decides which way to swim. It is the bacterial version of *should I go here or there*. *Escherichia coli* — which I am going to use because it is the bacterium most of the literature uses — has on the surface of its cell a number of receptor proteins. Each receptor binds to one specific kind of molecule out in the medium. Sugars. Amino acids. Various small molecules the bacterium is interested in. The receptors are clustered at the pole of the cell. There are hundreds of them. The cluster is called the *receptor array*."

He gestured with his hand — a tight fist at the table, then a tighter fist.

"When a receptor binds its target, it changes shape. The shape change propagates down through the cluster — not just within one receptor, but across the whole array, because the receptors are coupled to each other. A small signal at the periphery of the cluster gets amplified by the array's coupling, and the amplified signal goes to a kinase called CheA, which is at the base of the cluster. CheA is the integrator. CheA takes the input from all the receptors and outputs a single signal: run, or tumble."

He paused. He looked at Mira.

"Hey. Mira. *Run* and *Tumble*. Those words you've been seeing on the top of Bramble. That is where they come from."

"Watch."

He started walking forward across the floor in a straight smooth line, arms out, fingers waggling.

"*This* is RUN. The bacterium has a clear gradient. Food in that direction. The arrays say go. CheA says run. The flagella turn together in one direction and the cell goes."

He stopped. He pulled his arms in. He started spinning in place, slowly, on the spot. He twirled.

"And *this* is TUMBLE. The bacterium is in a confused part of the world. No clear gradient. CheA says tumble. The flagella un-coordinate. The cell flips around and points in some new random direction. And then —"

He stopped spinning. He pointed at his head.

"— and then it samples again. Is the gradient clear now? If yes, run. If still confused, tumble again. Run, sample, tumble. Run, sample, tumble. That's how a bacterium gets where it's going."

"That was the show. The science. There's a second thing in the same system. Van Duijn, twenty-twelve. The chemotaxis system has two feedback loops at different speeds. A fast loop — the two-component signaling pathway, CheA phosphorylating CheY — that responds in milliseconds. And a slow loop — methylation of the receptors — that responds over minutes. The slow loop adapts the array's sensitivity to the recent average concentration. The fast loop responds to the current concentration. The difference between the fast and slow loops is — and this is what is great about it — the *recent derivative* of the concentration. Is it going up. Is it going down. The cell computes the derivative without storing the values."

He paused.

"It does it with the time-lag between two feedback loops. The cell does not have a memory store. The cell has a fast comparator and a slow comparator running on the same input at different rates, and the difference between them tells the cell the direction of change. That is cognition done from timing alone. There is no number written down. There is no number to be written down. The cell is using time the way other systems use memory."

He set the card down.

"Same example. Two facets. One capacity. Cross-modal integration with derivative-by-timing. That's three."

"And before I get to the bacterium-sentence: *the predictions belong to the lineage, not the individual*. The bacterium itself is not doing anything but running its inherited program. The anticipation is the lineage's, not the cell's. The lineage built the program across — Mitchell and her group's number is — about a hundred generations. The cell is using the program. The anticipating is in the program. The bacterium is not anticipating. The lineage anticipated, generations ago, and built the program out of the anticipating. The bacterium is running the program."

Marge said, "Thank you."

"I'm wincing pre-emptively in solidarity."

Bart took a breath.

"Mitchell, two-thousand-nine. *E. coli* has two main sugars it can eat in the mammalian gut: lactose and maltose. In a typical human meal, lactose arrives in the gut first; maltose arrives a little later. *E. coli*'s genome encodes the machinery for eating both sugars. But the bacterium does not start with both machineries running. The lac operon is what gets turned on when lactose appears. The mal operon — for maltose — gets turned on when maltose appears. Two operons, two sugars, two switches."

He paused.

"Mitchell's finding was this. When the lac operon turns on — when lactose has just arrived in the medium — the bacterium *also* turns on the mal operon. *Before maltose has arrived.* The bacterium is pre-activating the maltose-eating machinery on the strength of lactose having just arrived, because lactose typically precedes maltose in the gut, and the bacterium's lineage has learned that the pattern is reliable enough that turning on the mal operon at lactose-arrival is the better bet than waiting. The bacterium is — wincing — *anticipating* maltose. It is doing so without any internal model of the meal. It is doing so because the lineage installed a cross-correlation between two operons, and the cross-correlation runs."

He looked at Marge.

"The cell is not anticipating. The lineage anticipated. The cell is running."

## Respiration Type

Jasmine said, "What is an FSM?"

Lisa said, "Oh. Finite State Machine. Yeah, so — think of the last time you walked down into a subway station and had to go through one of those turnstile things. When you walk up, the turnstile is locked. You push it, it won't let you through."

Jasmine said, "Got it."

Lisa turned Bramble in her palm.

"The behavior. The seeking first, because it runs the same whatever the knob is set to. The light sensors are the input. "The knob is a different axis. Respiration Type does not set where the unit goes but how it spends getting there — which store discharges. ## The Blank Slate

The kettle was whistling. Marge poured. Burns shut the door — the warehouse had cooled enough — and sat back down. Jasmine helped Mira down off her seat for a stretch and back up onto it. Mira held Bramble through all of this. Lisa turned the camcorder back on. The red light came on.

"Trellis block."

She did not stand. She had been at the camcorder; she stayed there.

"Marge. Fallacy."

Marge set down her tea — which had not had time to steep yet, she would come back to it.

"OK. Fallacy two."

She did not stand. She turned her chair slightly so she was facing the table rather than the camera. She had set the chair this way last quarter; she set it this way now.

"The Blank Slate."

She paused.

"The Ghost in the Machine, last quarter, was the worldview that pastes a willing self over the architecture of personality. It does so by photoshopping out the executive function — what I called the frontal lobe, by analogy, because the protocell does not have a frontal lobe and the protocell was what we were talking about. The Ghost was the easy one to start with because it is the one I have the most professional vocabulary for. Everybody in clinical work spends some of their week dealing with patients who have arrived believing in a willing self that they can locate behind their eye and address."

She paused.

"The Blank Slate is harder for me to talk about because I am sympathetic to part of it. The Blank Slate is the worldview that says personality is barely a thing — barely a thing at all. That what looks like personality is mostly context. The same person behaves differently in different rooms. Therefore, the conclusion goes, the *traits* we use to talk about personality are folk fictions. People are not introverts and extroverts; they are people whose introversion and extroversion is set by who they are with, what room they are in, and how their morning has gone. Trait talk is overrated. Context is everything."

She paused.

"Walter Mischel made the rigorous version of this argument in nineteen sixty-eight. He looked at how badly trait measurements correlate with actual behavior across situations and concluded that traits were nowhere near as predictive as personality psychology had been claiming. He was right about the data. He was also leaning, in his interpretation, on a particular picture of what a trait would have to be in order to count as a trait — basically, a thing that produces the same behavior across all situations. Nobody who works in personality now believes traits are that. Traits are dispositions to respond, given conditions. The disposition is the trait; the response varies because the conditions vary. The disposition is stable. Mischel was right about the data and wrong about what the data showed."

She took a sip of her tea, which had now had a little time. She made the face of a person who has been waiting for it to steep and has not waited quite long enough.

"That is the academic Blank Slate. The popular Blank Slate is what shows up in my office. It is the worldview that *blames the room*. The phrasing — and I am quoting clients, this is what they say — is, *I'm only like this when my mother visits. I'm not really an introvert, my boss makes me one. He brings out the worst in me. I was different before I met her. They make me small. The job has changed me; I don't know who I am at work anymore.* Some version of *the context did this to me; I would be otherwise.* The work is to notice that *the context did this to me* is doing a lot of structural lifting for the client. It places the trait outside the client. It places the *self* outside the client. The client is the room's victim. There is no real client; there is just the room and what the room is doing to the client today."

She paused.

"That is the popular form. The pop-academic form is — Bart, do you want me to land this or do you want to —"

Bart said, "This reminds me of that book *The Mind Is Flat*. Have you read that one, or is it too popular for your tastes?"

Marge said, "Nick Chater, yes. I had the pleasure of arguing with him at APA last year."

Burns said, "You argued with Nick Chater."

Marge said, "I argued with Nick Chater. Chater is an acceptable example of the Blank Slate fallacy. He argues that we have no deep, inner world of beliefs and desires. He thinks the inner self is an illusion. Which is a bit strange, to begin with. The more persuasive piece of his theory is that people improvise thoughts, feelings, and motivations in the moment based on context. Chater is a bit cynical; he claims we are all essentially B-S-ers who confabulate explanations for our behavior retrospectively. Human nature is just an L-L-M from twenty-twenty-two."

Burns laughed the chest-laugh.

Bart said, "Was he persuaded by your argument."

Marge said, "No."

"You were not persuaded by his."

"No."

"What did you argue about specifically."

"Whether the *flatness* he describes is the absence of a deep inner world or the absence of *introspective access* to a deep inner world. I think the latter and he thinks the former. He thinks the inner world is not there. I think it is there and we cannot see it clearly. The distinction matters because if it is there and we cannot see it, then trait talk is doing the work of pointing at something we cannot see directly; it is reaching at a shape we cannot describe in first-person language. If it is not there, then trait talk is reaching at nothing and we should stop using it. The data — what people do across situations — is consistent with both readings. The argument is about which reading you take."

She paused.

"I take mine. Chater takes his. He has sold more books."

She set her tea down.

"Now — the example."

She paused.

"I want to talk about one case. I am going to anonymize it. The case is a girl. Twelve. She comes to me because her parents are worried about her. The parents describe her as withdrawn. Her teachers describe her as quiet. Two of her teachers have written *socially isolated* on her end-of-year reports for two years in a row. Her parents have read these reports and have started to worry that she is depressed, or anxious, or something. They send her to me."

She paused.

"She arrives at my office for the first session. She is quiet. She looks at the carpet. She answers questions in short sentences. I would describe her, if I were writing a chart entry from the first thirty minutes, as withdrawn. I do not write that entry. I have learned not to."

She paused.

"After the first session I asked her parents — separately, because separation is the trick here — if there is anybody she is *not* like that with. The mother said: yes, there are two girls in her class she has been close with since third grade. The two of them and her form a unit. The mother said *they are like a different kid when they are together. They are loud. They tell jokes I do not understand. They have a podcast they record on the phone that none of the rest of us are allowed to listen to.*"

She paused.

"The kid I saw was quiet. The kid in the podcast is loud. Same kid. *Same* kid. If you read the Blank Slate, the conclusion is: there is no real kid. There is a kid-with-me and a kid-with-the-friends and they are different kids. Context dominates. The trait *introvert* is the folk-fiction the school is foisting on her."

She paused.

"That is the Blank Slate's mistake. The kid is not two kids. The kid is one kid running one program, and the program routes differently in different social configurations. Two friends who have been close since third grade: program produces *loud, funny, podcast*. A roomful of strangers, including a therapist she has been sent to by worried parents: program produces *quiet, looking at the carpet*. The variation is not evidence that there is no kid underneath. The variation is *evidence of the program*."

She paused.

"Imagine the program's job is to shape the way this girl trusts people. The particular shape of this girl's trust process is logarithmic. Slow, slow, slow, slow — and then once she is past the threshold, fast. The same shape produces both behaviors. With two friends she has known since third grade, she is well past the threshold. She is in the fast part of the curve. She is loud, she is herself, she is making a podcast. With me — a stranger she has been sent to by worried parents in week one of a therapeutic relationship — she is on the slow part. The same shape. The same kid. The same *program*. Different points on the curve, because of how much trust has accumulated."

She paused.

"That is what trait means. Not a fixed behavioral output. A *shape*. The shape produces different outputs in different conditions because that is what shapes do. The kid has a shape. The Blank Slate denies she does. The Blank Slate looks at the loud kid and the quiet kid and concludes there is no kid; what is actually true is that there is one kid running one logarithmic trust function and the function is doing exactly what a logarithmic trust function does in two different social environments."

She picked up her tea again. The face was a different face this time.

"Now — the photoshop."

She set the tea down.

"The Ghost in the Machine, last quarter, photoshopped out the executive function. The Blank Slate photoshops out something else. The Blank Slate photoshops out *the program inside*. The Blank Slate looks at a person and says: what you see is what is there. There is no stored procedure underneath that is routing the response. There is just the behavior at the surface. There is no inside. The Blank Slate has photoshopped the inside out and rendered personality as a behavioral surface that responds to context."

She paused.

"Same move as the Ghost, different target. The Ghost photoshops out the executive function and adds a homunculus. The Blank Slate photoshops out the program inside and pastes a context-driven surface in its place. Both worldviews are working by deletion. Both worldviews are putting something in place of what was deleted."

She looked at the camera. She did not look at Bart. She did not look at Burns. She looked at the camera because the camera was, in this moment, Cheryl.

"I am going to name a third one next quarter and a fourth one the quarter after. I am not yet making the structural argument that connects the four. I am noticing, out loud, that the first two work the same way. I will keep noticing it. I am leaving the connection for later in the arc because I do not yet have it figured out."

She sat back. She put both her hands around her tea in tea-posture.

"That is the fallacy. That is bit one. Lisa."

## Introduction to Cybernetics

Lisa pulled her chair closer to the table. She did not stand for this. She rarely stood for her own segments; her register was the table register, not the at-the-front-of-the-room register. She brought one palm flat and one palm vertical to the table and did the small rotating thing she did when she was about to think through something with her hands.

"I am going to talk about Coordination as an architecture. I want to flag up front — and I will flag it again at the end — that what I have for this segment is not a finished thing. I have two questions I have been chewing on across the quarter and I want to think through them out loud with the team. I have partial answers. I do not have whole ones."

She paused.

"The first question. *What kind of thing is the FSM, in the architectural sense?* I built it. Bart and I picked the states. We wrote the transition table. The unit is running it. I know what it *does*. I do not yet know what it *is*, structurally, in the framework we are building."

She paused.

"The second question. *Why does it work on the kitchen floor, and what would break it?* It works. We have ten weeks of footage. It works. But I do not have a clean account of *why* it works on that particular surface. And if I cannot say why it works, I cannot say what conditions would break it."

She paused.

"I have a partial answer to the first question. I am going to take that one. I do not have a clean answer to the second; I will gesture at where I have gotten."

She set both palms flat.

"The first one. The FSM is — I am going to use a phrase. I have been trying it out across the quarter. *A stored controller.*"

She paused to see if anyone objected to *stored controller* on the face of it. Nobody did.

"A stored controller is a thing whose rules — whose behavior, given input — are written down somewhere and looked up when the input arrives. The rules do not change while the controller is running. The controller does not write new rules. The controller reads its rules from storage and executes them on its inputs. The FSM is one. The rules are the few lines of the transition table. The storage is the microcontroller's flash. The lookup is what happens when a sensor reading arrives and the FSM does an if-then on it."

She paused.

"That's it. That's what a stored controller is. The unit has one. It is the first stored controller in Bramble's build. The protocell did not have one. The protocell only had its physics — heat in, motion out, no rules in between. Bramble has rules in between. The rules are stored. They are stored by us — by Bart and me — at the time we wrote them in July. The unit is executing them."

Burns, who had been listening, said, "Lisa. That is what biology is."

Lisa said, "Say more."

Burns said, "Every cell on the planet is running a stored controller. The genome is the storage. The cell is the runtime. The controller is whatever expression program the cell is running. The transition rules — when to make this protein, when to divide, when to gate the sporulation pathway — are stored in the DNA and looked up by the molecular machinery that does the looking up. Bart talked about sporulation an hour ago. Sporulation is a stored controller. The rules for when to sporulate are written in the DNA. The cell reads them when conditions trigger the read. The cell does not invent the rules. The cell executes the rules."

He paused.

"Every cell on the planet is doing this. It is what biology is. It is the substrate that biology runs on. The lineage writes the rules across generations; the cell reads them and runs. The FSM is the same kind of thing. It is just that we, the lab, are the lineage in this case."

Burns said, "Here is the most concrete version of it. One of the things the rules decide is who gets in. A protocell has a membrane — a wall of fat. Things cross it if they are small enough and greasy enough to slip through; otherwise they do not. That is the whole policy. The wall is a filter and the filter is only what the wall is made of. The prokaryote's controller builds doors. Real ones — proteins set into the membrane that recognize one specific molecule and haul it in against the crowd, and burn energy to do it. The lac operon Bart walked you through is the controller deciding to build the lactose door when there is lactose worth bringing in."

Jasmine said, "But the protocell already had a membrane. You said so. So what is the new thing — didn't it always keep some things out and let some things in?"

Burns said, "It kept things out by being made of fat. It did not choose. The protocell's selectivity is the shape of the hole. The prokaryote's selectivity is a doorman with a guest list — and the muscle to drag in a guest who would rather drift the other way."

He paused.

"And none of it is free. The world outside is always trying to flatten the cell into it, to make the inside the same as the outside. The cell spends, the whole time it is alive, just to stay different from what surrounds it. The doors are the half you would call cheerful — choosing what to bring in. The rest is paying, every second, to be a separate thing at all."

Bart said, "Protocell's not picky. It's a screen door — whatever fits walks in, and it calls that a membrane. Prokaryote hired a guy."

He paused the way he did before a punchline.

"Protocell never learned the word no. That's the whole upgrade. It learned to say no."

Marge said, "It did not learn anything. There is nobody in the protocell to learn it, and nobody to do the letting. You keep giving it a lazy doorman, Bart. It does not have a lazy doorman. It has no door and no one at it. The prokaryote is not a stricter list. It is the first time there is anyone at the door at all."

Lisa let that sit for a beat. Then she said, "OK. That is the right way to say it. The thing the FSM is, is the thing biology is. I am going to use that. *The FSM is a stored controller. The kind of thing biology is when it routes behavior.*"

Marge said, "It is the program inside."

Lisa looked at her.

Marge said, "I just used that phrase. Five minutes ago. The Blank Slate photoshops out the program inside. The FSM is the program inside. That is what Bramble has that the protocell did not. The protocell had no program inside. Bramble has one. Bramble has the thing the Blank Slate denies people have. Bramble is the place where the program is visible, because we wrote it down and we know which lines it is."

She paused.

"The protocell is the case where the Blank Slate's mistake would have been honest. There is no program inside the protocell. The protocell's behavior really is — at the cell — just its physics responding to the floor. The Blank Slate's worldview, applied to a protocell, would have been correct. The worldview is wrong for organisms with programs inside, which is to say organisms with anything resembling an internal regulator. From Bramble forward, the worldview is wrong."

She paused.

"I am not yet saying the four fallacies map onto the four cells. I am noting that the Blank Slate stops being approximately right at the place where the FSM starts existing. That is one piece."

Lisa wrote *the program inside = stored controller* in her notebook. She underlined it.

She said, "OK. That is the partial answer to the first question. Burns — and then Marge — gave me the framing. I had *stored controller* and could not place it. Now it is placed. *The program inside.* The unit has one. That is the structural fact."

She paused.

"The second question. Why does it work on the kitchen floor."

She paused longer.

"I have been thinking about this through Ashby."

Burns said, "Oh, good."

Lisa said, "I thought you would say that."

Burns said, "Requisite variety."

Lisa said, "Requisite variety. Tell them. You read this more recently than I did."

Burns said, "Ross Ashby. *Introduction to Cybernetics*, nineteen fifty-six. The theorem in question is: only variety can destroy variety. A regulator — the thing in your system that is keeping the system in some range against perturbations — must have at least as many internal states as the perturbations it can encounter. If the world can throw a hundred different problems at you, your regulator must be able to be in at least a hundred different states. If your regulator has only six states and the world is throwing a hundred different things, the regulator is going to fail seventy times. The variety of the regulator must match — at minimum — the variety of the disturbance."

He paused.

"Lisa thinks the FSM has approximately the variety the kitchen floor has. That's why it works. There are three states the FSM can be in — STOP, RUN, TUMBLE — and there are roughly three regimes the kitchen floor presents. The stores are charging. The stores are not charging and there's a gradient. The stores are not charging and there isn't. Three regimes, three states. The architecture is sized to its world."

Lisa said, "That is what Ashby would say. I want to push back on it slightly."

Burns said, "Push back."

Lisa said, "Pure Ashby is regulator variety matches disturbance variety. The disturbance variety, strictly counted, is *four*. Storage state — charging or not — crossed with gradient state — present or not. Two-by-two. Four input combinations. The regulator variety is three. The theorem would predict mismatch."

Burns said, "It doesn't mismatch."

Lisa said, "It doesn't mismatch because the FSM treats two of the four disturbances as equivalent. When the stores are charging, the FSM doesn't care about the gradient. Both charging-with-gradient and charging-without-gradient go to STOP. The FSM is folding two distinct input states into one output state. So the four input states collapse to three functional regimes, and the three regimes match the three FSM states, and Ashby holds."

She paused.

"But that collapse is the controller's doing. The controller decided that charging dominates gradient. The controller is partly defining what counts as *worth distinguishing*. Strict Ashby assumes you can specify the disturbance independently of the regulator. Here you can't quite. The regulator is folded into the disturbance definition. The theorem's form still applies — the regulator's variety has to match what the regulator treats as varied — but it's a weaker version than the textbook one."

Burns said, "OK."

Lisa said, "Pure Ashby is for systems where the disturbance is well-defined separately from the regulator. Thermostats. Biological homeostasis. The bacterium-and-its-medium has a clean separation; the thermostat-and-the-room has a clean separation. Bramble-and-the-kitchen-floor doesn't quite. The controller is doing some of the work of defining the disturbance. The theorem applies in a weaker form."

Burns said, "And what would break it."

Lisa paused.

"A world with more variety than three states. A world where two gradients exist at once and the unit has to pick between them. A world where the same input arrives in different conditions and needs different responses. A world where the unit needs to *remember* what it did last time. The FSM has no memory store. The FSM cannot route based on history. The FSM cannot do the Van Duijn rate-difference trick Bart talked about — because that trick is a feature of running two feedback loops at different speeds, which the FSM is not doing. The FSM is doing one-shot lookups. The kitchen floor does not exceed the FSM's variety. A more variable world would."

She paused.

"That is where I have gotten with the second question. I have an account of why it works that I am not fully satisfied with, and an account of what would break it that I think is correct."

Jasmine, who had been quiet, said, "Is it knowing what it is doing, though."

Lisa stopped.

She looked at Bramble. She looked at the team. She did not say anything for several seconds. Her hands, which had been doing the rotating thing, stopped.

She said, "Say more."

Jasmine said, "I am asking what you mean by *know*. You said the FSM does one-shot lookups. From the kitchen floor what it looks like is — Bramble is doing something. Bramble is going somewhere it wants to go. Bramble is, in our language, doing the thing. From inside the architecture, you are telling me the unit is just executing a few lines of code. From outside the unit, the unit looks like it knows what it is doing. I am asking what the difference is between *looking like* and *being like*. I do not know if I am asking a real question or a fake one."

Lisa said, "You are asking a real question."

She paused.

"Honestly, I wish I knew for sure. The question is open and I can tell you why it's open. It depends on what *level* you ask at. Bramble can be described at different levels and the answer to *does it know* changes depending on which level you pick."

She paused.

"At the architecture level — what is inside the unit — the FSM is a stored controller. We wrote the rules. The unit executes them. It has no internal representation of its goals. It has no memory of what it did last time. It has no model of itself. By any architectural test for knowing — does the system represent its goals, does it learn, does it model itself — the FSM lacks the structures that would normally support knowing. At that level, the answer is no."

She paused.

"At the behavior level — what the unit does from outside — the FSM is goal-directed in a functional sense. It seeks gradients. It satisfies needs. It stops when its energy state is good and moves when it isn't. From the outside, the behavior is the behavior of a goal-seeking system. At that level, the answer is more like yes."

She paused.

"So the question becomes: which level is the right level."

Marge said, "I can take this one. The Blank Slate I was just talking about — the popular version, the clients blaming the room — is the same shape of position. The careful philosophical version is functionalism. Putnam, Dennett, the canonical line in philosophy of mind. Functionalists say if a system functions like a knower, it is one; the architecture does not matter. The Searle position is the opposite — Chinese Room, symbol manipulation can match the behavior of understanding perfectly without ever constituting understanding. Architecture matters; some architectures are intrinsically incapable of understanding. The hard-problem people — Chalmers, Nagel — say even behavior isn't enough; there has to be something it is like to be the system, from the inside, and no test reaches that, at any level. This is the syntax-versus-semantics debate. It is fifty years old. None of the positions has won."

Lisa said, "I have read all of them. I do not know which is right."

Jasmine said, "Marge. What do you think."

Marge said, "I think the philosophy is mostly wrong."

Jasmine said, "Why."

Marge said, "Stay tuned. I will come back to this at a later date. The flag has been planted."

Jasmine looked at Lisa.

"But Lisa isn't so sure."

Lisa said, "Look. I personally tend to think knowledge should be defined by architecture, not behavior. But I'm less certain than Marge that the philosophers are wrong. And I don't think that looking at Bramble harder is going to resolve the debate once and for all. Cheryl, watching: we are leaving this open because it is the kind of question that is open, not because we have not thought about it."

She looked at the camera. She looked away.

Then she went on.

"Two things to add before I close."

She paused.

"The first. I have been thinking that what the build is going to keep doing is *adding layers*. Each new motivation arrives as a new architectural element that acts on the layers below. The FSM at this quarter acts on the metabolic layer underneath it. The metabolic layer keeps running; the FSM gates it. Next quarter, the memory arrives, and it will act on the FSM — the memory will store what the FSM has been doing and give the unit a way to read history into its decisions. The memory does not replace the FSM. The FSM keeps running; the memory acts on it from a layer up. The unit accumulates depth across the build."

She paused.

"I want to flag — and this is the epistemic flag — that this is *a hunch I am forming*. It is not a result the field has handed me. The layered-architecture pattern is something I am proposing for what the build might be. The pattern might not hold. We might get two quarters in and find that the next motivation is not a layer on top but something else entirely. I am proposing it as the shape I think the build is going to take. I am not committing the framework to it. If the pattern is not holding, we will be honest about that and revise."

She paused.

"The Ashby thing is the established theorem. The weaker-Ashby framing is mine. The layered-architecture framing is mine. I want to keep those distinct because the team should know which is which."

She paused longer.

"That is what I had. Burns."

## May we Coordinate

Burns took the close — *the motivational speech* still, on Lisa's agenda, though he'd spent the quarter lobbying for "Why we do what we do" and gotten nowhere. It was the rock he always reached for — Strelley Pool, three and a half billion years old, the layered mats, the dying generations pinning each other down, the run of time too long to hold in a head — and he gave it the way he'd given it before, hands open at the unholdable part, eyes leaving the room on the turn and coming back. ## Reflection: What is Hidden?

Begin in fresh water — a pond, the still edge of a lake. To the single cell that lives there, the water is a slow violence.

The cell is saltier than the pond. It has to be; that saltiness is half of what it means to be a cell at all. And water moves toward salt the way the world moves to fill any difference — so the pond comes in. It comes in through every inch of the membrane, all the time, without pressure and without malice, simply because the inside is concentrated and the outside is not, and the water would like to even that out. Left alone, the cell swells and thins and bursts, and the pond gets what it was always going to get, which is no cell at all — only more pond.

The cell does not allow it. Somewhere inside it there is a small clear bladder that fills with the trespassing water and then clenches and drives it back out through the wall, and fills again, and clenches again, a second slow heart whose only work is to bail. It does this without rest for the whole of its life. The entire achievement of the creature is to stay different from the water it stands in.

This is the oldest true thing I know about having an inside. An inside is not a place. It is a difference, and a difference is not free. The membrane is not a wall that happened to be there; it is a refusal renewed every second, an argument the cell wins and wins again against a world that is not even trying, that is only doing what water does. To be a self — even one this small, this far down, two billion years before there is anyone to want the word — is to spend yourself, without pause, keeping the outside out.

And here is what I was slow to understand. The inside was built to be unreachable. Not hidden, not shy — built, from its first afternoon, to keep the world from coming in, because keeping the world out is the very same act as being at all. We arrive a long time later and discover we cannot reach into another person, and we take it personally, as if a door had been shut that might have been left open. No door was left open. There was never a door. There was a cell in a pond, bailing, and everything that has an inside is its grandchild, and the distance between us is not a flaw in the work. The distance is the work. It is what an inside is for.
