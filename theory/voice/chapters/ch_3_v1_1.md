# Interlude 2→3

## Build log — Five into one
*Bart, hardware. Filed September 1, 2031, for the team.*

This one I did myself — took the lot to the bench Friday and had the weekend on it, so I'm running it back this morning. Scorecard first.

Last quarter's calls, and I'll take the whole board again. I said it would seek, and it seeks. I said it would hunt the gradient instead of sitting in it, and it does. I said it'd feed itself off the panels and the floor and not need a Sunday crank, and it hasn't. And I put the shove on record — drives into a stock unit sitting in the sun, leans on it half a foot at a time across a morning, ends up in the beam itself — and that's what it's done every sunny morning since June. Called it dead on. Marge tells me it started some kind of conversation at their dinner table. Couldn't tell you. I wired the shove; it shoves.

Now the fun part, and there's a lot of it this quarter, because this is the first time the thing's a real machine instead of a bug.

It's got wheels. Finally. Last quarter I told you hopping was a lousy way to travel and I had a fix coming — here it is. New chassis, thirty centimeters, low and round, dome on top, near enough a small Roomba, and I dropped the whole prokaryote unit into the middle of it for the brains. Then the wheels, and this is the part I'm proud of: I didn't buy any. I used the four leftover protocells — 1, 2, 4, and 5, the ones that didn't make the cut — one at each corner. Pulled 4 out from under the toaster, towel and all. Each one still kicks exactly the way it always did; I just aimed its kickers at a ratchet on a wheel axle instead of at the floor, so now every kick clicks the wheel forward a notch instead of hopping the whole unit. Two kickers per old protocell, two pawls per wheel, four wheels — and here's the part I like: nothing's shared, no driveshaft, no differential. Each wheel's its own little engine. So the brains drive it the same way they drove the kicks last quarter, just spread over four wheels now instead of two: fire them all forward and it goes straight, lean on the left side harder than the right and it comes around. It rolls now. Smooth-ish — it's still a stack of clicks under there, but four wheels' worth blur into something that passes for driving, and it beats the hopping by a mile.

The kid had names for all five of them. There's one machine now, and four of her bugs are the wheels. She'll work that out on her own time. Not my department.

It earns the Roomba name, too — I put a real vacuum on the underside and a bin inside that holds about a day of floor dust. And here's where Burns made my weekend hard. Any sane machine, you'd charge on a pad: set it down, done. Burns won't have it. So instead the thing carries its dust to a dock — a little box that plugs into the wall — dumps the dust through a port on its belly, and the dock pays the energy back through the same port. And here's the part I like: you can't just hand these transducers warmth — they've never run on warmth, not once, not since the protocell floor; they run on a difference, a warm side and a cool side and the gap between. So the dock makes the gap. There's an air compressor in there feeding a vortex tube — a plain pipe, no moving parts, you push compressed air in the side and hot comes screaming out one end and cold out the other, which still reads like a magic trick to me and isn't — and it lays the hot stream on one face of the transducer and the cold on the other and lets the thing drink the difference. And before anybody calls that free: it isn't. The compressor's eating wall current the whole time; the tube only splits what the wall already paid for — and it drones the whole time it's plugged in, so rather than set a pump humming by the dishwasher all day I hung it outside the back door and ran the air line in through a hole in the wall. What sits by the dishwasher is the quiet end of a hose coming in from the cold. It trades trash for the difference through a dirty little hole instead of charging clean off a pad — a clean little dock fed by something ugly out of sight, which is about what this design deserves. It's worse engineering every way you can measure, and Burns says that's exactly the point — life doesn't run on induction. Fine. It's his contest. I built him his dirty hole.

There's a board up top now, four slots, and it's Lisa's — her algorithm, her department. Three knobs live on it: the crank (knob one, still there, mostly for show), aerobic-or-anaerobic (knob two), and a new one, knob three, which she calls explore-or-exploit. Near as I can tell it sets whether the thing wanders and tries new routes or sticks to the ones it's already worn in, because the board keeps track of where it's been — a path gets a groove the more it's driven, and fades when it isn't. Lisa makes it sound like the whole ballgame. Looks like a worn path to me. Three slots filled, one open, nine knobs still to squeeze in after that. I'm going to need a bigger board by spring.

Relays everywhere now, long runs of them, because the body's big and spread out and a signal's got to walk all the way from the sensors up front to the wheels out back. The clack's a proper cascade these days — you can hear it think across the room. Still like that you can hear it.

Calls. It vacuums, it docks, it drives. The wheels mean it finally covers ground instead of hopping in place, so I'll bet it cleans the kitchen end to end and parks at the dock when it runs low — dump, drink, off again, clean as a transaction. And that explore-or-exploit knob: Lisa can keep her abstraction, but I'll wager nobody in that house can tell the two settings apart by looking. It's a coin flip with a fancy name.

It still doesn't have a brain — Burns is clear about that, and for once I agree with him. It's a cart that remembers which way it usually goes. But it rolls, it cleans, it feeds itself. A year ago it was five things twitching on a warm floor; now it's one thing that drives. I'll call that a good weekend.

Tear this up in three months if I'm wrong about the explore-or-exploit knob. I'm not.

— Bartholachew (gesundheit)

## Notes on the Third Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed November 1, 2031, for the team.*

We set out this quarter to give the machine an inside, and we did. I want to write about the kind of inside it is, because I do not think it is the kind we believe we built. Last quarter I argued that a machine could have a nature and no inside — that it could behave with great steadiness and still be no one, with nothing kept anywhere behind the behavior. That is no longer true. The thing that came back in September holds. It holds the dirt it lifts instead of trailing it. It holds its own charge behind a wall it spends itself to keep. It holds, on a card somewhere under its dome, a worn picture of where it has been, so that one day's going bears on the next. It has an in-here now that is not the out-there. I will not undersell this; it is real, it is new, and it is the most the lab has yet managed. But I want the team to carry one sentence into the retrospective ahead of all the others, because the build points so confidently away from it: we have given the machine every kind of holding except the one that would keep it alive, and the household has spent three months quietly making up the difference with its hands.

### The new body

Bart came on a Friday at the end of the summer and took the wind-up machine away, and while he was there he gathered up the four units Mira never chose — the ones that had kept their places around the kitchen since spring, the slow one still on its folded towel by the toaster — and carried all five out to the van. For two days the floor was bare. On Monday he brought back one thing where there had been five.

What he brought back is low and round, the span of two hands, a shallow dome on a four-cornered disk. The four units are the wheels. It took the household a few days to see this and then they could not stop seeing it: the four Mira passed over are the corners now, turning, and the one she chose — the machine the household has called Bramble since the spring — sits at the center, going nowhere itself and directing the ones that go. Where there were five small things doing five separate things, there is one larger thing doing one thing. I will go on calling it the machine, as I have from the first; they call it Bramble, and I will use their word only when I am giving you theirs.

### What it holds

Three things it now keeps that it never kept before. It lifts crumbs and grit off the floor as it goes, and a small panel on its dome lights the word STORED while it is carrying anything; what it takes up it holds inside itself rather than dragging it along behind. That is the first holding, and the plainest: it has a bin, and a bin is an inside. The second is its charge. When its wandering brings it within a foot or so of the base Bart fixed by the dishwasher, it climbs on, gives up the load it has gathered, and is fed in return — but only in return. Notably, the base will not feed a machine that brings it nothing: I have watched it climb on with an empty bin, the dome dark where the word should be, and be given nothing, and roll off no fuller than it came. Its keep is the wage; the day's dirt is the work. I am told this was done on purpose — that the team wanted a thing that earns its keep rather than a thing that drinks for free — and whatever one makes of the wanting, the result is a machine whose holding pays its own way: it is fed in exchange for what it has kept.

The third holding the household cannot see, and I can only infer, not because it is hidden — the switches are up top, in plain reach — but because a record does not show itself the way a switch does. The machine keeps a record. Run the same kitchen enough times and it wears a route, and the route deepens the more it is run and fades where it isn't, so that the machine carries from one day into the next a worn picture of its own going. I will not try to say how this works; the team can do that, and Jasmine — who has begun to notice that the clicking under the dome has changed, that it clicks more now, and longer, since the thing grew, and has been wondering aloud what makes the sound — I would let keep wondering, because I have come to suspect her wondering is worth more than the explanation will be. What matters for these notes is only this: the inside has a history now. It did not before. The machine that came back in September is the first one we have built that is, in any sense at all, the same machine on Tuesday that it was on Monday.

### Burns at the foot of the stairs

The week the new body came home, Burns wanted it witnessed. Jasmine reports that he stood at the bottom of the basement stairs and hollered the news up through two floors until she came down — the way he hollers everything, the man who wired an intercom into the walls and will not use it. What he wanted her to watch was the machine finding its base, climbing on, giving up the day's load, and being fed for it: the few seconds in which the thing he built looks as though it needs no one. He is not wrong that it is a marvel. But the marvel is seconds long and comes once or twice a day, and Jasmine is the one in the kitchen for the other twenty hours, which are the real subject of these notes. He called her down to see the machine at its most independent. She is the one who knows what it is the rest of the time.

### What it cannot hold

Here is the fact the household lives with and we, in the warehouse, do not: most days of the week, the machine dies on the floor. It does not run low and go to its base. It has no sense whatever that it is a thing that can run out — no monitor of its own charge, no alarm, nothing that reads the inside and acts on it. It works the floor until it cannot, and then it stops, dead, wherever the last of the charge happens to leave it, in the open middle of the room as often as anywhere, and stays there until a hand picks it up. Twice this past fortnight Jasmine has come down in the evening to find it stopped cold in the center of the kitchen, nowhere near the base, exactly where its luck ran out.

I want to be exact about what this means, because it is the whole of what I have to say this quarter and the build will tempt us to look past it. We gave the machine an inside and we did not give it any way to feel the inside. It keeps a charge and cannot tell that it keeps one. It carries a history and cannot consult it on its own behalf. It holds, and holds, and holds — and the one thing it cannot do is hold itself: keep itself in view, mind its own going on. We have built a house and lit the lights and left no one home in it, and a house with no one home cannot tend its own fire. So the most self-sufficient object in that house is also the most helpless thing in it; both are true at once; and the household has had to learn to keep both in mind, because the machine can keep neither.

### The hand

So the household feeds it the way you feed a thing that cannot feed itself, and the feeding has become a daily, unscheduled fact of that house, and it falls, as these things fall, to whoever is nearest, which means Jasmine. When she finds it stopped, she does the thing the team has not yet learned to do, and she does it with her hand before anyone could put it in her ear: she reads it. She looks at the dome. If the word STORED is lit — if it died with a load still in it — she carries it to the base and lets it pay its way in and drink, the fast way. If the dome is dark, she knows the base will turn it away, and she sets it instead on the warm tile by the door where the morning sun comes in, and leaves it to take what slow warmth it can from the heat and the light, the way you leave a sick thing in the sun. Without anyone deciding she should, she has become the part of the machine that knows when it is empty. She is the sense it does not have. The machine cannot feel its own inside, so she feels it for the machine, by hand, at the end of days that had already asked enough of her.

This is the third quarter running that Jasmine has come to know the machine through her body before any of us could explain it to her, and I have stopped finding it a charming fact about Jasmine and started finding it the most important one in these notes. There are three switches on a little board up top, and one she works more than the others, which sets whether the machine runs a tight worn route or goes wandering after new ground. Jasmine reports that it does better when it stops wandering — and then went quiet, and then said, most of the time, which is the truer sentence, and the one she had to talk herself around to. She talked herself around to it because of the light. A tight worn route is the efficient one — less ground crossed, less spent crossing it — and most days that makes it the better setting. But the machine still sips what light it can off the windows as it goes, and on a bright day that sip is worth having, and the window-light will not hold still: it crosses the floor as the morning goes. One bright cold morning she had left the route running tight, and by afternoon the machine had worn itself a tidy groove down the shadow side of the kitchen and stalled there, starving an arm's length from a slab of sun it never turned to take. She turned it loose to wander, and watched it blunder out into the light and revive. What she had found, and what no one had told her, is that the switch and the sun are bound together: a machine leaning on the light cannot afford to be efficient, because the sun will not come to a groove, and a thing that has worn itself a path has stopped going to the window. I have nothing to add to it. She found it with her thumb. I am only writing it down.

### The coin

Mira has begun assigning the machine preferences, in the flat, certain way she reports the weather. Bramble likes the kitchen. Bramble does not like the bath mat — the machine stalls on the high pile and backs off it, and the child has read the stall as a dislike. And last week she dropped a coin in its path to see what it would do. It took the coin up with the crumbs and carried it to the base, and the base would not accept the coin and lit the word ERROR, and the machine sat there, not leaving and not resolving, until Jasmine reached down and lifted the coin out by hand. Mira watched the whole of it from the floor and told me, when I telephoned, that Bramble got stuck on purpose.

I want to be plain about this, because a careless reading would do the child a disservice. The machine did not get stuck on purpose; it has no purposes of that kind, and I am not going to give it one, this quarter of all quarters, merely because a child has. But the child is not confused, or regressing, or showing me anything I would dignify with a clinical label. She is doing something I find entirely reasonable, and would find reasonable in a grown woman: a person who was told for seven months that this thing was hers to look after, and who now watches it clean the floor and trade its dirt for warmth and appear to ask nothing of her, reaches down and sets a small obstacle in its path to find out whether she can still make something happen to it. She can. For a moment the self-sufficient thing needed a hand again — her mother's, as it happened, but a hand. And here is the part I would not have the team miss: the child is closer to the truth than the lab is. We look at the machine and see a thing that needs nothing. She looked at it, mistrusted that, tested it, and was right — it needs a hand most days, and she is the one in the house who refused to be talked out of knowing it.

### The Sunday, and the four

Two of the household's old occasions for tending ended this quarter, quietly, with no one deciding to end them. The first: there is no key anymore. For seven months Mira wound the machines every Sunday evening, after supper and before her bath — six turns, six dry clicks, a week of running — and I wrote, in my first notes, that the Sunday winding had become a ritual, by which I meant a small repeated act, given real attention, of the kind that holds a household together at its joints. The machine feeds at the base now and has no use for the child's hands on a Sunday. Nothing was put in its place. It simply stopped having a reason to occur, the way a thing stops occurring once the need that called it forth has been engineered away.

The second: the four units Mira never chose, each of which she had given a place — a warm patch, a sunbeam, a folded towel by the toaster for the one that fit nowhere — and tended there, are the corners of the wheels now, turning when the body turns. I argued once that to make a berth for the thing that thrives nowhere is among the better things in a person, and four of those berths are gone. I will not pretend the units mind it; they have nothing to mind it with, and I am not going to hand them an inside merely because losing one would be sadder. The loss is not theirs. It was never going to be theirs. It is the child's, and the household's.

It would be easy to write that what the household lost this quarter was its reason to tend — I started to write exactly that, and caught myself. It is not true. The machine needs tending now more than it ever did; it dies on the floor most days and waits for a hand. What the household has lost is not the need but the shape of it. The old need was scheduled, repeatable, child-sized — a thing Mira could do on a Sunday and be proud of having done. The new need comes at no hour anyone can plan for, cannot be set on a calendar, cannot be made a small girl's weekly job. It is answered by whoever is nearest, and whoever is nearest is Jasmine.

### The most independent thing in the house

Set the quarter down all at once and it reads like an advance, because it is one. The machine holds where it held nothing. It cleans, it earns its keep, it carries yesterday into today, and it asks the household for less than any version before it. That is the sentence the build wants us to write, and I would not strike it. I would only set one fact beside it, and decline to say which is the larger.

The machine cannot feel that it is running out. So most nights, after the house is down and the dark has been coming by five, Jasmine finds the most independent thing in the house stopped dead on the floor where its charge gave out, and bends to it — at the end of a day that has already asked too much of her — and carries it to be fed. No one sees her do it. There is no longer a Sunday key for the child to wind and be proud of having wound. The tending did not leave the house when the machine learned to feed itself. It went looking for the nearest hand, and found the one with the least room left in it.

These are notes about a family I love, set down in the first hard cold that has shut the household indoors with the machine for a fortnight. Cheryl may set them before strangers one day; I have written what the work requires and kept the rest. I do not yet know whether a thing that holds and cannot feel its own holding is on its way to feeling it, or whether we have built a warm and tidy invalid and called it independent — or, if it is the second, what it will go on costing the woman who bends to it in the dark. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Coffee Garden

Marge had picked the place — Coffee Garden, a café on Ninth and Ninth with a south wall of windows and a counter of pastries nobody needed, the kind of room that had decided to be warm and then committed to it. Outside it was the tail of October, the light already low and spent by one o'clock, and the south windows took the little there was of it and laid it down the room in long copper bars. She was already in the corner when I came in, and she had ordered for both of us before I sat down, which I would learn was not rudeness but a fixed feature, like weather.

People hear *clinical psychologist* and build something cool and narrow, and the woman across from me was neither. She was broad and settled and a little rumpled, reading glasses pushed up into gray hair she had stopped coloring on principle, and her face did most of its work around the mouth — a mouth that worried even when the rest of her was at rest, that kept a small readiness for someone nearby to need something. Her cardigan was the color of a brick rained on for forty years. She held her coffee with both hands, close to her chest, the way you hold a tea you're in no hurry to finish, and I understood eventually that she held everything that way, that the posture had nothing to do with the coffee.

I'd come to talk about the vignettes — she was the one who turned the quarter's footage and logs into the small household scenes that reached me between meetings, and I wanted to know how she kept them from going soft, from making the robot into a child. The danger, she said, was not softness. It was flattery. The whole field had a bad habit of being charmed by the thing it was studying, and she spent most of her energy not being charmed. "The minute you're charmed you stop seeing," she said. "Charmed is a kind of lazy." She said it about Bramble. It was also, exactly, a thing a person could say about people, and she said it the way you say something you've had occasion to learn.

I said something then — I don't remember the shape of it, something a little too pleased with itself, about funders staying out of the science — and she set the cup down and looked at me and said, "Oh, you don't believe that," not unkindly, the way you correct a bright student who is hiding. She was right. I had not expected to be caught at my own lunch by a woman drinking drip coffee in a wet-brick cardigan. The *oh, you* had a whole mother in it. It also had a whole argument in it, folded small.

She'd been Mary until she was forty-four. I asked what happened at forty-four and she said Bart happened — that she'd known him for years, and then during the recruitment she sat across his kitchen table and felt him look at her for the better part of a minute and settle on something he didn't say. A week later she was Marge, to him first and then to all of them, and she'd decided not to fight it because the boy was so plainly delighted and so plainly sure. "He sees people as cartoons," she said. "It's alarming when it's you." She didn't tell me which cartoon. She didn't need to. I was already watching the worried mouth and the cup held against her chest like something that might wake, and I had her all at once, the way you get a likeness — the few lines that are the most of a person — and I understood that a kid with a gift had looked at this woman and seen exactly what I was seeing and called it Marge and been right.

I should say plainly that I was rendering her through something by then, because I have spent enough years being honest on tape to know the tell. I was making her larger and warmer than the drip coffee and the rained-on cardigan strictly supported. A clinical eye would have logged a tired academic in her forties, behind her cohort, ordering for strangers because it was easier than sitting still. I logged a hearth. I knew I was doing it. I did it anyway. It is the most Helen thing left in me, and I have mostly stopped apologizing for it.

I tried, once, to turn it around — asked her something real, where she was from, whether the family was still close — and watched her do the thing. A sentence and a half: Provo, big family. Then she was back across the table tending to me, asking whether I'd had anything that morning that wasn't coffee, and the redirect was so smooth and so practiced that I nearly missed it, except that it is my own move. I do it with a camcorder. She does it with a kettle. We are both people who would rather hold the cup than be the cup. I think that was the moment, if there was one.

When the check came she tried to pay — at my lunch — and I let her win the smaller half of the argument, and as she dug for her wallet a manila envelope slid halfway out of her bag: a corner of letterhead I knew from a hundred steeples, a line of form-text about the removal of records. She pushed it back down without a word. It was plainly not a thing for today, and I am good at not asking. We said the kind of goodbye two people say when they've just decided to like each other, which is to say a slightly awkward one, and I drove back up the canyon to Park City — the scrub oak gone rust on both walls of it, the aspens higher up already bare — turning it over the way you turn a stone you've already decided to keep.

# Notes on the Third Retrospective

By the time the team had been at Smithers a few hours the early dark of mid-November had come down — the sun behind the Oquirrhs by five — and the first cold haze of the season had settled into the valley, so the sodium lights at the lot's edge did not shine so much as smear, each wearing a soft sphere of its own light like a thing breathed on. In May the high windows had held the long spring light, and in August the flat slabs of summer; now in November they threw the inside of the building back at itself, black glass with the work lights doubled in it.

Marge had got there at two and had the kettle going by half-past, because the warehouse after six gives back the cold it has taken in all day, and she cannot be in a cold room without making one warm corner of it — her own kitchen, this warehouse going black at the windows, the same hearth wherever the cold finds her. She sat with a beer held in both hands against her chest, the way she holds everything, close, as if it might want comforting, and a paperback with the cover folded back. The worried mouth was at rest and worried anyway. The cardigan was the brick-red of forty years of rain. She would spend the meeting guarding the room against the Noble Savage — against the warm lie that the thing on the table was good because it was unspoiled — and she had brought, as she brings to every meeting, a warm corner and a folded-back book and a juice box for the child: the conservative who tends, holding the line and the kettle in the same two hands.

Burns had not been pacing. The first retrospective had given him something to pace about that he no longer had: he had carried the funder's voice into the room once now, in August, had taken his son seriously on the tape the way she'd asked and survived it, and the July lunch was the only one he'd had — her last lunch had been with someone else, and he had come in this quarter with nothing of hers to deliver and nothing to organize on the drive. So he had spent the hour at the table with his deep-time notes, making his peace, as he made it every quarter now, with the twenty minutes Lisa had cut him to in August and had not given back.

The side door opened and the cold came in around Jasmine's knees. She had the unit in both arms — this was new this quarter, the thing too big now to ride in one hand, a low round body she carried against her chest like a sleeping cat that has outgrown being a kitten. Mira came in behind at her own pace, hands free the way they had been free since the morning the bots went off in the van and came back as this. Burns got up and crossed to her, and asked about the drive, and she said it had got dark fast and the first haze was down past twenty-first south, and she'd come up the surface streets because the interstate in the haze was a faith exercise — the colleague version of *the drive was fine*. She handed him the unit; it was warm, it had ridden in the footwell with the heater on it and clicked the whole way up. Bart came over and fist-bumped Mira's four knuckles. Lisa lifted her jelly jar by way of hello and Mira pointed and said "Bramble" and Lisa said there was the one on the table and the one in the glass, same name, she'd decided that in the spring, and Mira found this acceptable and went and put one flat hand on the unit's dome where Burns had set it, not to do anything to it, only to have her hand there. Bart handed out the beer. Marge had hers in the tea-grip. There was a juice box for Mira, and Bart bent the straw, and Mira sat down in the chair that had become her chair, close enough to the unit to keep a hand on it. Across the table Marge set her book down, cover folded back to hold her place, and reached for the kettle, and Lisa crossed to the camcorder, and the red light came on.

## Inside

Lisa opened her phone and set the stopwatch face up on the table next to the jar.

"Camera's on. Q3. Same order as the door. I added a line at the end — next-quarter logistics gets its own two minutes instead of riding out the door with everybody's coats. Burns, you've got twenty for deep time. We are not relitigating the twenty."

"I'm not relitigating the twenty."

"You looked like you were going to relitigate the twenty."

"I have made my peace with the twenty."

"Good," Lisa said, and started the watch.

Burns stood. He had a way of standing for the quarter-goals that was different from his way of sitting for everything else, a settling of the weight, and he put both hands flat on the table for a moment before he started, palms down, still, the way he did when he wanted the room to know he was about to say the part he had actually come to say.

"The goal this quarter," he said, "was to give the unit an inside. I mean that as exactly as I can mean it. Up to now we have built a thing that holds a course — that senses, compares, acts, keeps itself going against a world that is mostly trying to even it back out into the world. That's a regulator. That's what Bramble was last quarter. What we wanted to find out this quarter is what changes when the thing we built stops being only a process and starts being a *place* — when it has a defended interior with its own conditions, an inside that is kept different from the outside on purpose, and a memory that the inside carries with it from one day to the next so that the inside has a history. We wanted to give it an in-here that is not the same as the out-there. And then we wanted to watch the household for three months and find out what an in-here changes."

He reached over, without quite looking, and picked up Mira's juice box, which she had set on the table, and turned it in his fingers a half-turn and then another half-turn, the way he turned a vertebra, the way he turned a knob, reading the seams of it, and set it back down a few inches from where it had been. Across the table Bart's hands, which had been moving, went still. Nobody said anything. The narrator of all this, watching it later on the tape, would clock the half-second and let it pass, because it was the kind of thing that is only a thing if you say it is one, and at the table it was not one. Mira reached over and took her juice box back and moved it to where she wanted it.

"Inside," Mira said, to the juice box, and drank from it.

"Inside," Burns agreed, and sat down, and gave the floor to Bart and the cards.

## Bigger

Bart took the top card off the deck in the cigar box. The front of it was his own drawing — an elevation of the new body, the low round disk with the shallow dome and the four corner wheels, done in his draftsman's line, the prokaryote-tier unit drawn small and central inside the dome like a yolk. He held it up so the camera could see it and read the title off the top of it in the voice he used for titles, the cereal-commercial voice, the one that sold you the box.

"*Eu — KAR — y — ote.*"

"Thank you," Lisa said.

"The eukaryote," Bart said, in his own voice, "is the cell that has rooms. Everything before this was one bag of working parts — a bag that does a lot, a very good bag, but a bag. This one has a nucleus. It has an inside the inside, where it keeps the important paperwork. It has a skeleton on the inside that it built itself, that it can take down and put up somewhere else when it wants to be a different shape. It has hallways. And the headline" — he tapped the small central yolk in his own drawing — "is that it has *tenants.* The power plants in this cell, the mitochondria, didn't grow here. They moved in. A couple of billion years ago this cell's however-many-times-great grandmother was a cell that ate another cell and, for reasons nobody got to vote on, did not finish the job — kept the meal alive inside itself and put it to work — and every one of us at this table is still running the descendants of that meal in every cell we've got. So the new word for this quarter, the new motivation" — he was careful with the word; they were all careful with the words now — "is *Interiority.* Not just a wall. An inside. An inside with its own weather, that keeps a history, that has somebody living in it."

"That's the breakthrough talking," Burns said. "Let me take the breakthrough."

"Take the breakthrough."

"The breakthrough at this cell is *Scale and Size,*" Burns said, "and it is the most direct cash-out of a prior breakthrough we've had yet. Last cell, the headline was predation — the line learned to make a living off other living things. This cell is what happens when predation goes one step stranger and the prey is more useful alive than dead. You don't digest it. You keep it. You run it. And the thing you get for keeping it is *power* — an absurd amount of power, far more energy per unit of paperwork than a cell running its own engines off its own outer wall could ever raise — and once you have that much power to spend, you can afford to get *big.* You can afford a genome the size of a library when your cousins out in the open water are still running off a pamphlet. Bigger genome, bigger cell, more rooms, more tenants, eventually" — and here he could not help himself, he was looking somewhere past the wall — "bodies. This is the cell that makes bigness affordable. The engine that buys the room is the meal it kept alive."

"Some people," Lisa said, "would say the energy story is doing more work than the evidence licenses."

"Some people would," Burns said, comfortably. "I think they're being careful past the point of usefulness. The energy story is the one I believe. I'll say it's mine and not the textbook's."

He had his eyes closed by now. He taught deep time with his eyes closed and opened them when something pulled him back to the room, and the team had long since stopped reading it as rudeness.

"The setting for all of this," he said, "is the most boring stretch of time in the history of the planet, and I mean that as praise. Call it the Boring Billion — a billion-some years of a warm, stable, unspectacular world, low drama, nothing much happening at the headline level, the oxygen creeping up slowly and the chemistry staying within bounds. And boring is exactly the runway a complicated thing needs. You don't build a cathedral during an earthquake." It was a good line, but maybe the only good one in the lecture. He went on about the oxygenation, and how the ocean "was still sour and metal-starved down deep. The first tenant, the power plant, came in early. A second kind of tenant came in later, in the lineages that learned the same trick twice and swallowed a photosynthesizer and kept it — that's everything green, every plant, every alga, a second meal kept alive." Jasmine liked the part about the photosynthesizer. He went on: "And the oldest fossil we can point to and say *that one is unambiguously one of us* is a little red seaweed from about a billion years ago, up in the Canadian Arctic now, and the reason it matters is not that it's old. It's that you can see in it that it was having sex. Two kinds of it. Trading. That's new. That arrives here."

He opened his eyes.

"That's the deep time. That's the twenty minutes."

"That was nineteen," Lisa said, and stopped the watch, and looked, for a moment, structurally pleased.

"The capacities," Bart said, and turned the card so its face showed again, and went down them with the back of a pen. "Four of them at this cell. I'll name them and Burns'll tell you which animal does what."

"Intracellular Transport," he said. "The cell is too big now to just let things slosh where they need to go. So it runs delivery. It has little motors that physically walk cargo from one part of the cell to another along tracks — actual legs, actual walking, actual tracks."

"When you say it walks," Jasmine said. "What do you mean, walks."

"I mean walks." Bart set the card down. "The motor's a protein. It's got two feet, near enough, and it hauls a sack of cargo down a track by putting one foot in front of the other — picks up the back foot, swings it past the front one, sets it down, picks up the next. Hand over hand, except feet. It looks like —" and he stood, and pushed back from the bench, and did it: arms stiff out in front holding an invisible sack, knees locked, lurching one leg past the other across the open floor in front of the camera, a flat-footed monster-walk, the cereal-commercial showman gone full Frankenstein. "— that. All day. Every cell in you, right now, somebody's doing that."

Mira lost it — the whole-body laugh that folds a four-year-old in half. Jasmine laughed with her, at her more than at Bart, the way she did. Burns, who could not let a wrong thing go by, said the real gait was more of a swing than a march, and Bart said nobody asked, and took one more lurching step for Mira before he sat.

"We'll come back to this one," he said, picking the card up again, "because the household's got a lot to say about it. Headline for now: a big cell has a small army of marching — swinging! — soldiers."

"Memory Beyond Adaptation," he said. "It can hold a state that isn't a reaction to right now. It remembers a thing that already happened and keeps remembering it after the thing is over." He looked at Burns.

"There's a flower I love for this," Burns said. "A toadflax — little snapdragon thing. There's a lineage of it that grows a strange symmetrical flower instead of the usual lopsided one, and the strangeness breeds true, parent to offspring, and it has bred true for two hundred and fifty years. And here's the part: the gene for the flower is not broken. The DNA is ordinary, wild-type, fine. What's changed is a *mark* sitting on top of the gene, a tag that says *keep this one switched off,* and the tag is what gets copied down the generations. The information isn't in the text. It's in the annotation. The plant is remembering something the genome never wrote down. That's memory that sits beside the code instead of inside it."

"Phagocytosis," Bart said. "Eating, but the grown-up kind. Not absorbing — *engulfing.* Wrapping yourself around a whole other thing and pulling it inside into a sealed pocket. An amoeba does it to a bacterium; one of your own white blood cells does it to whatever's making you sick, right now, while we're talking. And —" he tapped the central yolk again — "it's the move. It's how the tenant got in in the first place. Phagocytosis is the deep ancestor of eating *and* the thing that built this whole kind of cell, because one time the wrapping-around happened and the pulling-inside happened and then the digesting didn't."

"State-Dependent Gating," he said. "This one we've had since last cell, but it's fancier now. The cell has checkpoints. It will not go through a door — will not divide, will not move to the next phase — until conditions at the door are met, and the doors are layered, gate behind gate behind gate, so that the whole life of the cell runs as a program of *not yet, not yet, now.* Burns calls them the cell-cycle checkpoints. I call them the cell being a stickler."

"They keep you from dividing with your DNA half-copied," Burns said. "They are the reason the program doesn't run itself off a cliff. Carried forward, more of them, better locks."

## Organized

Lisa took the environment and the tendencies. She did not stand. She talked about the cell the way she talked about everything, as a thing that did jobs, and she kept her own hand near the camcorder without touching it, the director's hand on the record.

"Environment first, quickly, because Burns mostly did it. Shallow marine, the sediment-water margin, oxygen coming up near the surface, the deep water still sour, metals scarce enough that what a cell could build was partly a question of what metals it could get its hands on. Warm. Stable. A long quiet shelf to grow complicated on.

"Tendencies — what the cell *does,* once it has an organized inside. Three of them, and I'll give them to you as behaviors, not as positions on any chart. One: it takes material in across a real boundary and *holds* it — pulls things into a defended interior and keeps them there instead of letting them wash through. Two: it sorts and routes internally — it has somewhere to put what it's taken in, and a way of getting it there, which is the transport Bart flagged. Three — and this is the new one this quarter, this is the knob we'll get to — it *toggles between two strategies* for how it draws on what it's stored: a wide-open, try-new-things strategy and a narrow, run-what-works strategy. Exploration and exploitation. We've got names for them that match the line — Respiration Type, Foraging Type, and so on — but ignore the names tonight; the names are bookkeeping. The behaviors are: take in and hold, sort and route, explore-or-exploit. No psychology in any of that. A cell does all three without anybody being home. I want that on the record before Marge gets to be home about it."

"I haven't said anything," Marge said.

"You're about to."

Then the motivations, which Lisa took first and Marge took second, and which they had divided between them the way they divided it every quarter — Lisa the machine, Marge the person — without ever quite saying that was the division.

"Three motivations live at this cell," Lisa said. "Two of them you know. *Persistence* — keep going, keep the difference between you and the world from washing out; that's been there since the start, that's the oldest one. *Coordination* — get the parts to act as one part; that was last quarter's, that's why the four out in the household became four corners of one body instead of four loose things. And the new one. *Interiority.*

"What Interiority parameterizes — what it's the knob-word for — is two things that arrive together. First, the defended inside itself: not a membrane that happens to be there but a boundary the unit spends itself to maintain, an in-here held different from the out-there on purpose, with the maintaining as a continuous cost. Second, a persistent internal representation — the unit now carries, from one day to the next, a structured trace of what it's done, a stored picture shaped by its own history, that it can act on. The inside isn't just walled. It's furnished, and the furniture is *its* furniture, accumulated. The work of building and reshaping that picture — the part of the unit that does that — we'll talk about properly in the third block; tonight I just want the word seated. Interiority. The unit has an inside, and the inside remembers being itself."

Marge set her beer down and held it down with one hand on the table beside it, which was as close as Marge came to raising a hand.

"And the folk version," she said, "the version I'm going to be tedious about for one minute, because somebody has to keep translating this back into the only language any of us actually live in — the folk version of Interiority is that this is the very beginning, the absolute first faint flicker, of *an inner life.* I don't mean it has one. I mean the architecture of having one starts here, this far down, in a thing the size of a coin. The thing that makes *inside me* a different country from *out there.* The reason you can be standing in a room full of people and be somewhere none of them can get to. We are going to spend the rest of this build adding to that inside until it's furnished enough to be worth visiting, and worth defending, and worth grieving. Tonight it's a one-room interior with the lights just turned on. But the lights are on. That's the part I'd ask the room to sit with. There's an in-here now. There wasn't, and now there is."

Lisa let that sit for exactly as long as she would let anything sit, and then said, "Bramble block. Jasmine."

## The Relay

Jasmine had been waiting for the floor, in the way she waited — without leaning forward, the depleted patience of someone who had been up at two with a labor and had still come, and who was read by the team, when they read her at all, as merely tired.

"It vacuums," she said. "I'll start with the plain things, because the plain things are most of living with it. It vacuums. It goes around the kitchen and the hall and it lifts up crumbs and grit and whatever Mira's dropped, and a little panel on the top of it lights up the word STORED while it's carrying anything, and it keeps what it picks up instead of dragging it around behind it like the old one would've. And when it happens past the base Bart put by the dishwasher — not on any schedule, just when it gets near — it climbs on, feeds a minute, and goes again. Some days it gets near a lot. Some days it doesn't get near at all. It's good. It does a lot. The house is cleaner than it's been since Mira learned to throw food.

"The thing I noticed most, though — the thing that's new in the way it *behaves,* not just what it does — is the toggle. There's a setting Bart showed me how to flip. One way, it finds a route through the kitchen and it runs that route, the same loop, tighter and tighter, until it's basically worn a groove — it knows the kitchen, it does the kitchen, it's efficient and a little boring and it never bumps into anything. The other way, it stops trusting the groove. It goes poking into corners it's never bothered with, tries the gap behind the trash can, gets itself somewhere new. More misses that way. Bumps things. But it finds spots the groove never went. And I can feel the difference from across the room before I remember which way I left it set — there's a settled version of it and a restless version of it, and they're the same machine."

"Hold that," Lisa said. "That's the knob. Keep going on the household."

"Mira's started telling it what it likes," Jasmine said.

Mira looked up.

"Bramble likes the kitchen," Mira said. "Bramble does not like the bath mat."

"It stalls on the bath mat," Jasmine said. "High pile, it can't climb it, it reverses off. Mira's decided that's a dislike. And last week —"

"Bramble got stuck on purpose," Mira said.

"She dropped a coin in front of it," Jasmine said, "to see what it'd do. It took the coin up with the crumbs, carried it to the base, and the base wouldn't take the coin, lit up ERROR, and the thing just — sat there. Didn't leave, didn't sort it out. Sat on the base with the coin it couldn't give up until I reached down and took the coin out by hand. And Mira watched the whole thing and has decided it got stuck on purpose." Jasmine said it plainly, the way she said most things, and did not editorialize, though the narrator on the tape, who editorialized about everything from a great distance, would later want to put a whole paragraph in this gap and would make herself leave it as Jasmine left it.

"There's one more plain thing," Jasmine said, "and it's the one I keep turning over. When it runs all the way down — when it hasn't passed the base in a while and the charge just goes — it stops dead, wherever it's standing. And I pick it up and set it on the base, and after a bit it comes back. There's no key to wind anymore. You just set it down and wait." She reached over and stood Mira's juice box back up. "I know it doesn't choose the spot. I know it stops wherever the charge gives out." A beat, the quiet she got before the thing she actually meant. "But I'll tell you what it looks like from where I'm standing. It looks like it always stops right in front of me. The middle of the kitchen floor, when I'm the one there to see it. Like it held on until I'd notice. I know that isn't what it's doing. It hasn't stopped looking like that's what it's doing."

Marge left her beer where it was and answered straight across the table, gently, because the woman was tired and was right about the only part that counted. "It stops in front of you because you're the one in the kitchen," she said. "You're always the one in the kitchen. It isn't holding on for you — there's no one in it to hold on, and no one in it to pick the spot. The holding-on is yours. You're bringing it, and it's a kind thing to bring, and I'd still keep it on your side of the table, because the looking-like is the cheapest thing this machine makes. It's going to look like it wants a long while before it wants, if it ever does at all. The look costs it nothing. That's the part I never let count."

Jasmine took it the way she took most things, without visible cost, and let it go. What she had just done — set a someone down in the middle of a thing that had no one in it — was the reflex the whole project kept committing and catching, and the one Marge would put a book's name to within the hour, dressed better and argued harder and no truer than this. Here it was bare, and made of love, and no one wrote it down.

She glanced down the table then, at Lisa, with the particular attention she gave to bodies, which was different from the attention everyone else in the room gave to anything.

"You've got the watch on," she said. "You been getting out, in this fog? Shoes on or off lately?"

"On," Lisa said. "Most mornings. The fog doesn't bother me — it's just colder air, you dress for it. I did the loop along the river path before I came in today. Frost on the boards by the water."

"How far."

"Far enough," Lisa said, which was Lisa for a number she didn't feel like producing, and Jasmine, who knew the difference between a person who ran and a person who *ran,* let it go and filed it, the way you file a thing about someone you've decided to keep an eye on. It went nowhere. It was just a thing Lisa did, that the room half-knew about and never asked after, and tonight Jasmine had asked after it, and that was all.

"OK," Lisa said. "The click. Jasmine, you flagged the click — say the thing you said to me at drop-off."

"It clicks more than the old one," Jasmine said. "More, and longer. The old one would tick a couple of times and you'd know it had changed its mind about something. This one — it's a whole run of them now. *Clack-clack-clack-clack.* Like it's counting something out. And it does it more since it got bigger. I've been reading the thing by its clicking for the better part of a year, the way you read a kettle, and the clicking's changed, and I want to know what makes it."

"That," Bart said, and put down his beer, and leaned in, because this was his, "is the best question anybody's asked me all quarter, and I'm going to take the long way around it.

"A relay is a switch that's thrown by a magnet instead of by a finger. You've got a little coil of wire, and an iron arm sitting next to it called an armature, and when a current runs through the coil it becomes a magnet and it *yanks* the arm — snaps it over — and the arm, when it snaps over, closes a second circuit. That's it. That's the whole device. Current in, magnet on, arm snaps, next circuit closed. And the snap of that arm hitting its stop is a sound. It's a click. One relay, one click.

"Now chain them. Put a row of them in a bank, and wire it so the circuit the first one closes is the coil of the second one. First arm snaps — *click* — and that closes the circuit that energizes the second coil, so the second arm snaps — *click* — which closes the third, *click,* and the fourth, *click,* and you've got a signal walking down the bank, one relay handing to the next, each switch throwing the one after it. That walk, that handoff going down the line — *clack-clack-clack-clack* — that's what Jasmine's hearing. The unit's bigger now, the parts that need to talk to each other are spread out across the whole chassis instead of crammed into one little body, so there's more bank to walk, and the walk takes longer, and you hear more of it. The click got longer because the thing got bigger and the message has farther to go, one handoff at a time."

"And here's the part that makes it the capacity and not just a noise," he said. "That same machinery does two completely different jobs with the same trick. One job is *signal* — getting a decision from this end of the unit to that end, *go,* *stop,* *the base says no.* The other job is *cargo* — physically moving the stuff. The crumbs it vacuums up don't teleport to the storage and they don't teleport from storage out to the dock; they get *moved,* step by step, handed along, the same kind of relay logic but with material instead of just a message. One system. It carries the word and it carries the thing. Signal and freight on the same line of handoffs. That's *Intracellular Transport.* That's capacity number one off the card. The household's been listening to it for a year and calling it the click."

"Say why it's built that way and not some faster way," Burns said. "That's the part I want on the tape."

"Go ahead, that one's yours."

"It's built that way," Burns said, "because the cell got too big for the easy answer. In a small cell you don't need delivery — things are close enough that they just drift to where they're needed, bumping around, and that's fast enough because nothing's far from anything. But once you're big — once you've got all these rooms — drift is hopeless. A molecule could wander for an hour and never get across. So a big cell can't rely on things finding their own way. It builds tracks, and it walks the cargo down them — the walk Bart did. It's slower than you'd want. You can *hear* that it's slow — that's the whole length of the clicking. But it's the answer a single cell has, because a single cell has no — " and he caught himself, lightly, at the edge of a word he was not going to say this quarter, and went around it — "a single cell has nothing built specially for sending fast signals long distances. There's no dedicated wire. Getting something from here to there across a big inside, whether it's a message or a parcel, is *transport.* It's carrying. It's the cargo getting walked. And carrying takes time, and the time is real, and you can hear it. That's not a flaw in the unit. That's what being this big costs you, and the cost is paid in those clicks."

The clicking, as if on cue, did not oblige — the unit sat quiet on the table, warm off the footwell, having nothing at that moment to walk anywhere. But the room had all heard it in the car at one time or another, and Jasmine had heard it tonight, and the silence on the table now had a shape it had not had before, which was the shape of a thing you'd just been told the inside of.

## Foraging Type

"Which brings us to the knob," Lisa said. "Bart, hardware."

"Knob three," Bart said, and held up his hand with three fingers, theatrically, as if announcing a prizefighter. "Genetic knob three. It's a toggle — two positions, click left or click right, no dial, no in-between. It seats in slot three of the board." He didn't elaborate on the board; that was coming, and he kept his hands off it. "The board's got four slots. Knobs one, two, and three are in it now. Slot four's empty. We didn't pull anything to put this in — knob one's still there, knob two's still there, three just goes in the open slot. Pure install."

"Pure install," Lisa confirmed, and the phrase had the small click of a thing being set down where it belonged; it had been hanging around the lab since last quarter, the name for what the genetic line kept doing — adding without subtracting, the board just filling up — and it landed now on its proper occasion, the first quarter where you could point at an empty slot and a full one and see the whole pattern in a single board. "It's the name for how this line grows. We don't trade knobs. We accumulate them. Three down, the slot count tells you how many to go."

"And what does it *do,*" Burns said.

"It's the toggle Jasmine already described better than I'm about to," Lisa said. "Officially it's *Foraging Type,* but ignore that the way I told you to ignore the tendency names — what it actually governs is how the unit draws on its stored movement-sequences. Remember the unit's carrying that internal picture now, the trace of what it's done. The knob sets the *policy* for using it. Flip it one way — exploitation — and the unit generates few new sequences and mostly re-runs the ones that have worked, and every time it re-runs one it gets a little more committed to it, reinforced by use, until you get Jasmine's groove: the worn loop, the tight efficient kitchen, the settled version. Flip it the other way — exploration — and the unit generates *more* new candidate sequences and tries them, spends more of its time off the groove, in the corners, missing more and discovering more: the restless version. Same stored memory underneath, two ways of spending it. It's a tendency, in the line's terms. It's a strategy knob. And it's the first knob we've installed where you can stand in the kitchen and feel which way it's set before you check, which I think is worth noticing — the inside got big enough this quarter that its *settings* have a mood now. Not a mood. A felt difference. I'll let Marge have the word *mood* if she wants it; I'm not giving it to her, she'll have to take it."

"I'll take it later," Marge said. "I'm saving up."

## The Noble Savage

"Trellis block," Lisa said. "Marge, you're up first. Fallacy."

Marge put the kettle thought down — she had been about to get up for it, six having come and gone — and stayed in her chair, and held her beer in the two-handed grip, and took the room the way she took it, which was head-on.

"The fallacy this quarter," she said, "is the Noble Savage, and I want it on the record that I think it is the most dangerous one we've done yet, because it's the one people actually live by. Pinker's name for it, though the idea's older than Pinker — there's a whole Rousseau back-story I'll spare you. The Noble Savage is the belief that the creature's nature is fundamentally fine — innocent, good, sound — and that whatever's wrong is the *cage.* The fit. The environment. Get the creature out of the wrong setting and into the right one, and the goodness that was always in there comes out. You all know the vernacular of it even if you've never heard the term. Quit the draining job and you'll feel like yourself again. Leave the relationship and you'll bloom. Move to the city, move out of the city, move to the place whose energy matches yours. The problem is never the person. The problem is always the pond."

She set the beer down.

"Here is what fifteen years of a caseload has taught me about the pond. Most of the people who change ponds discover, with a kind of horror, that the thing they were trying to leave behind got into the car. They take the new job and within a year the new job feels exactly like the old job, because the part of them that experienced the old job as draining is the part that came along. They leave the marriage and find the same fight waiting for them in the next bed with a different face across the pillow. I'm not saying it never works — sometimes the cage really is the problem; sometimes a person really is in a setting actively poisoning them and the right thing is to leave, and I have told people to leave. But that's the minority of the cases. In the majority, the landscape travels. Wherever you go, there's the same weather, because you brought the weather. You *are* the weather."

"And the reason it connects to this quarter," she said, and turned her hand, palm up, toward the unit on the table, "is that the Noble Savage only works if you believe the creature is *separable* from its inner world — if you think there's a clean, good nature underneath that the history just sits on top of and could be peeled off. And that's exactly the thing this quarter says is false. Because what did we just give this unit? We gave it an inside. We gave it an inner model — a representation it's built out of its own history, the routes it's worn, the corners it's tried, the things that have worked and the things that haven't, all of it accumulated and carried and shaping what it does next. That model isn't a coat the unit is wearing. The model *is* the unit, near enough. The model made the fit it's in. And — this is the whole point — the model is what will make the next fit, too. Move this unit to a different kitchen and it will wear the same kind of groove in the new floor, because the thing that wears grooves came with it. There is no version of the unit that is pure and good and waiting to be freed by a better room. There's only the unit and the inner world it's built, and they're the same object. The Noble Savage denies the inner model. And the inner model is the thing Bramble just got."

She should have stopped there; the argument was finished. But she went on, and the going-on was quieter, and aimed at no one, and the register had slipped a half-step off the clinical onto something with a person under it. "Everybody wants it to be true," she said. "That underneath the fit you're stuck in there's a truer you — a good one, a free one — and all you'd have to do is get out from under the wrong room and it'd come up like grass. People wait their whole lives on that. I've watched them wait. I've —" and there she stopped, and the worried mouth worked, and her eyes had gone bright in a way the argument did not account for, and she looked down into the beer in her two hands as if the rest of the sentence were in it. "There's no one in there waiting to be let out. There's the room you've already built, and you carry it. You're the weather."

Nobody at the table knew what to do with it, because nobody at the table knew what it was about. Across the table Jasmine, who read bodies the way the rest of them read tape, put a hand out palm-up on the wood between them and left it there and said nothing, and after a moment Marge set her own hand down near it, not quite in it, close. The narrator, watching this later, thought she knew what was in the bag at that lunch and what it cost to go on carrying it, and decided it was not hers to say, and let the tape run on the way the room did.

Marge got most of the professional voice back. "It's the nicest fallacy of the bunch," she said. "Most of us wish it weren't one at all."

## Memory

"My block," Lisa said. "The board."

She picked up her bramble and set it down again a half-inch to the left, squaring it to the edge of the table, which was a thing she did, and which the team had stopped seeing, and which the narrator on the tape would come to read as the thing Lisa did with her hands when the thing she was about to say mattered to her more than she wanted it to.

"The headline of the whole build this quarter is on a board the size of a deck of cards, up top where any hand in the house can reach it. Four slots. Knobs one, two, three are seated. Slot four's empty. And I want to be careful about what the board *is,* because it's easy to hear 'memory' and picture a drawer you put things in and take them back out unchanged. That's not it. The board is the first part of any unit we've built that *remembers* — and by remembers I mean it carries, from one day to the next, a representation that's been shaped by what the unit has done. Run the kitchen a hundred times and the board isn't storing a hundred recordings of the kitchen; it's storing a *picture* of the kitchen that the hundred runs have worn into a particular shape, thicker where the unit's been often, faint where it hasn't. The picture is the residue of the doing. It's the part of the unit that builds and holds an account of what it's been up to. Before this, the unit acted and the acting left no trace; the next day it started fresh. Now the inside has a history, and the history is on the board, and the unit acts out of it."

"There's a theorem under this," she said, "and I'll name it because it's the spine of the whole framework and I'd rather say it once, properly, than have it leak out sideways. Conant and Ashby. Nineteen-seventy. *Every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system.* If a thing is going to keep something under control — keep a course, keep itself alive against a world — then somewhere inside it there has to be a model of the thing it's controlling, because you cannot reliably steer what you don't, in some form, represent. Now: the last unit had a model. It was a thin one — a fixed set of rules, sized for a simple world, hard-wired by us, the same on day one as on day three hundred. A stored controller, I called it last quarter. This unit has a *richer* model, and here's the difference that matters: the unit carries it and reshapes it by use. We didn't hard-code the picture. We gave it the machinery to build the picture and let the doing fill it in. The regulator got a better model of what it regulates, and it got it the way you'd actually want — by living."

Bart, who had been quiet through this in the way he went quiet when Lisa was doing the part that was hers, said, "That's the thing from school. Isn't it. The board's running the thing you've been carrying around since — what, undergrad."

"Since undergrad," Lisa said.

"Does it have a *name,*" Bart said, "or are we still calling it 'the thing.'"

And Lisa, who named things for other people all day long, who had named the agenda items and the blocks and the cadence and had let other people name her, was for a second visibly caught out at being asked to name the one that was hers. Her hands, which were never not doing something, did nothing. She looked at the board up top — she could not see what it held, but she looked where it was.

"It's an evolutionary algorithm," she said. "That's the whole of it, technically. It runs a population of little candidate strategies, scores them, keeps what works, varies it, runs it again. Selection, in a box. I've been pointing at it since I was twenty. And this is the first time it's been —" she stopped, and started over, more plainly than she usually allowed herself, "— this is the first time it's been *running in something,* out in a house, doing the kitchen, instead of running in a simulation on my machine where the only world it ever had was a world I'd typed. The build caught up to the place I've been pointing. That's — " she did the thing with the glass again, a quarter-inch, " — that's a strange thing to watch happen."

"The name, though," Bart said again, gently. They went back further than the lab, the two of them — back to school, before any of this — and he asked it the way you ask something of an old friend whose work you've watched take shape for years.

"Evolutionary Algorithm," she said. "Evo-Algo, if you have to make it small."

"Evo-Algo," Bart said, trying it. "I like it. Sounds like a cousin."

"It's not a cousin. It's an algorithm. But fine."

She recovered the room the way she recovered every room, by reaching for the precise thing.

"And I want one sentence on the record exactly as I say it, because the whole rest of the build hinges on the difference and I don't want it blurred even once. *It remembers; it does not yet learn from outcomes.* Say that back to yourselves the next time you watch it run the kitchen. It has a memory now — a picture shaped by what it's done. What it does *not* have, yet, is the ability to change its picture because something turned out well or badly. It isn't keeping score against results and adjusting. The groove gets deeper because the groove gets used, not because the groove got *rewarded.* That second thing — learning from how it goes — is a later cell's headline, and if you catch yourself describing this unit as 'learning what works,' stop, and check whether you mean 'wearing in what it repeats,' because right now it's only ever the second one. It remembers. It does not yet learn from outcomes."

"And the motivation the board parameterizes," she said, "is the one Bart seated for us at the top: *Interiority.* The board is what makes the inside an inside worth the word. A defended boundary with nothing kept behind it is just a wall. A defended boundary with a history kept behind it — a picture the thing carries of its own life — that's an interior. The board is where the interior lives. That's my block."

## Sentience

"Can I —" Bart said, and then didn't ask permission, just put his beer down with a small deliberate click of his own, glass on concrete, a sound that was not a relay but that landed in the room like the throw of one. "I want to do a thing we've never done and I think we have to start doing it now or we're never going to do it honestly. I want a show of hands."

"On what," Lisa said.

"On whether this thing feels anything." Bart looked at the unit, and then around the table. "Because here's where I am. For three quarters now I've listened to the two of you" — Burns and Lisa — "talk about this unit in words that have somebody home in them. It *wants* the gradient. It's *trying* corners. It's *defending* its interior. And mostly I let it go, because mostly it's shorthand and we all know it's shorthand. But tonight we put a word on the slot — *Interiority* — that doesn't sound like shorthand. It sounds like there's an inside in the sense that there's an inside to *me.* And I think before that word hardens into something we all just say, somebody has to stand up and ask the rude question out loud. Is there anything it is like to be this? Does the lights-on-inside that Marge keeps gesturing at — is any of that *felt,* by anyone, from the inside? Hands. Yes means you think there's some it's-like-something in there, however dim. I'll go round and make everybody say why, because a hand by itself is worthless."

He looked at Lisa first. Lisa, predictably, did not raise her hand fast or slow; she raised it like a person stating a fact.

"Yes," Lisa said. "And I want to be exact, because my yes is going to surprise you and I need it not to be misheard. I think this unit knows nothing. I think there is nobody in there doing anything I'd call thinking — it doesn't model itself, it doesn't know it's a unit, it has no idea it's in a kitchen. On *knowing* I'm a hard skeptic and you've heard me be one. My yes isn't about knowing. It's about *feeling,* which is a different axis, and on that axis I don't think the lights switch on at a line. I don't think there's a height you reach where suddenly there's something it's like to be you and below which there's nothing. I think it's a dial, and I think the dial is never quite at zero anywhere there's a thing holding itself apart from the world and running some little model to do it — anything maintaining a boundary, predicting its surround enough to stay itself, doing inference at any scale. That's nearly everything alive and a lot that isn't obviously alive. So when Bart says *what about a rock,* the answer isn't 'rocks are in the club, cells are in the club.' The answer is the dial reads close to nothing on a rock and a hair above nothing on a cell and it climbs from there, and this unit is somewhere a hair above nothing, and a hair above nothing is not zero. That's my yes. It feels, faintly, in the way a thermostat would faintly if you pushed me, and it knows absolutely nothing, and both of those are true at once."

"That's the weirdest yes I've ever heard," Bart said.

"It's a consistent yes. Burns."

"Yes—" Burns said, and stopped, and set both palms flat on the table and left them there, which for him was a long time. "No. Let me do this properly, because I'm about to talk myself out of where I started, and the room should watch me do it." He turned one hand over. "Bart's right that I've spent three quarters talking about this thing like there's somebody in it, and I wasn't only joking, so here's the pull, honestly. A thing that holds a setpoint — that keeps a state against a world trying to knock it off — has to carry a model of what it's keeping, or it couldn't keep it. Lisa just told us that; it's the spine of the whole works. And this unit does it. It holds its little world and corrects, and for about ten seconds that feels like the bottom rung of a ladder that runs all the way up to us, and the bottom rung says *yes.*" He shook his head, once. "But that's a ladder of *regulating,* and I keep trying to climb it like it's a ladder of *feeling,* and it is not the same ladder. A thermostat holds a setpoint, and there is nobody in the thermostat. Being a good regulator makes a thing *smart.* It does not make it *somebody.* And the somebody — the lights-on, the something-it's-like-to-be-it — in my line of work that turns up a long, long way up from here, in animals, and it takes a piece of equipment a single cell hasn't got and won't have for the better part of a billion years. This is one cell. It is a beautiful little regulator and there is no one home in it. So — no." He sat back. "And I had to argue myself there from a yes. Put *that* on the tape too."

The narrator, watching this much later, would note — and would be the only one to, because nobody in the room caught it and Burns least of all — that the ladder he climbed up and then climbed back down was the good-regulator theorem Lisa had set down ten minutes earlier for the board, restated word for shape, and that he had no idea he'd been standing on it. He thought he was reasoning from the bone beds. He was reasoning from the framework, and then he had done the rarer thing and overruled it — he talked like the framework the way some people speak prose, except this once the framework had said *yes* and he had said *no.* The man who all quarter had put somebody home inside the thing was the one who, asked to count, reached in and took the somebody back out.

"Bart, you called the vote, you have to take it too," Lisa said.

"No," Bart said. "And mine's the boring answer, which is that I think it takes a *self* — not a model of the world, a model of *you,* one that has a you in it, a thing that knows there's a thing here that is itself and could stop being. Nothing this far down has that. A cell doesn't know it's a cell. There's no one home to do the feeling, because feeling needs a someone and a someone needs a self-model and the self-model isn't here. World-model, sure, fine, I'll grant the board's a world-model. But nobody's looked in the mirror yet. No. Marge."

"No," Marge said, "and naturally I disagree with all three of you about why." She had the beer back in both hands. "For me it's about *symbols.* I follow Deacon on this — Terry Deacon, *The Symbolic Species,* and I'll lend it to anyone who wants to actually fight me instead of agreeing with me badly. The thing that makes a mind the kind of thing the word *sentience* is reaching for is symbolic reference — the capacity to mean something *about* something, to point past the present moment with a sign that stands for a thing in its absence. That's the Rubicon, and it is a real one, and almost nothing crosses it. A cell signaling its own state isn't meaning anything about anything; it's just a domino falling. There's reference all the way up the biological world, but symbolic reference is rare and late and probably only us and a few near-relatives, and without it there's no one to be a subject in the sense the question wants. So no. The unit doesn't feel anything, because feeling, in the sense Bart's rude question means, takes a symbol-user, and this is a very good domino."

"So," Bart said, and counted the table with a finger, unhurried. "Lisa, yes. Burns, no. Me, no. Marge, no. One and three." He sat back. "Good. I wanted it on the tape that we're split — lopsided, but split — and I wanted it on the tape that the four of us can't even agree on what we'd be agreeing *about*: one yes off on its own, and three noes that don't share a reason between them. That's the actual state of things. We'll do it again every quarter, and I'll bet the split moves before the end."

It did not occur to any of the four to ask the fifth adult in the room. Jasmine sat with her hands quiet in her lap and did not raise one and was not asked to, and the question went around the table and past her the way weather goes past a window. She was the only person at Smithers who had been in a room when a mind actually began — had been in a great many such rooms, with her hands inside the work of it — and had also been in the rooms where it failed to begin, and carried both kinds of room home in a private ledger nobody at the table had ever asked to read. She watched the four of them vote on whether the coin on the table could feel, and she kept her own counsel, and the narrator, who would clock this too and sit with it longer than with anything else in the meeting, let it stand without comment, because Jasmine had let it stand, and it was Jasmine's to let stand or not. Mira, who was four, was not part of it either. Mira had found a pen.

## May we Hold

"Last thing's mine," Burns said, and stood, "and I'm calling it 'Why we do what we do.'"

"It's the motivational speech," Lisa said. "It's been the motivational speech for three quarters."

"I've been trying to rename it since July."

"I know. The answer's still no."

He let it go — an old defeat he'd made his peace with, the way he'd made his peace with the twenty minutes — and stood with his weight settled and his hands, for once, open at his sides instead of flat on the wood. The motivation this quarter was Interiority, he said, and he wanted to argue it was the one with the highest stakes they'd hit, higher than persistence, higher than coordination, because it was the first one about having an *inside* — and he compressed the philosophy into the line he'd carried in ready, the weight already on it before he said it.

"Nothing matters to a thing with no inside," he said. "There's no in-here for it to matter in."

Then the story, the one he kept coming back to. Two billion years ago a cell ate another cell and didn't finish — they had a word for it, phagocytosis, predation gone strange — and he wanted to call it the other thing it was, which was that one living thing took another inside itself and kept it alive. Gave it a room. Fed it. And the arrangement held; that was the part he couldn't get over, and he slowed on it, eyes leaving the room and coming back. It had held two billion years without a single break in the line, and every cell in everyone at the table was keeping that ancient guest alive right now, had never once stopped.

"You're a place where something else is being kept alive," he said. "The inside of you is a room you keep a guest in. And the guest is the reason you have the power to be anything at all."

So the moral was a call to action, he said, because they always were. An inside was a thing you kept — Marge's flower, Lisa's board, all of them — and you didn't keep one for free. It was continuous work. It was bailing against a world that kept coming in. And it was the work most worth doing, because it was the work that made there be a you for the work to be worth doing to.

Then he got to the place his speeches always got to, the half-step past where the argument needed him, and his voice did the thing it did. The best of it, he said — the part he was most grateful for — was that an inside was also a thing you handed forward: you built one and you kept one and then there was a next one coming up that got to build its own — and his eyes went, without his quite deciding it, to Mira, bent over her paper with the pen. "You get to be the room for a little while," he said, "before they build their own."

Bart's laugh came in a beat early, on *room*, on the word that had settled on the child and not on the son. "Okay — land the plane," he said. "The plane's landed." The table took it as what it looked like, a kid puncturing his dad's sentiment, and it broke the warmth a half-second before the warmth could set into anything. Burns took it like a joke, because it was one, and grinned, deflated on purpose, glad of the contact even when the contact came shaped like a needle.

"Fine," he said. "Fine. Then let's close it. The verb." And here he stalled, because the verb was the problem and he knew it — you could persist and you could coordinate but you could not *interior,* there was no door in the word to walk a cascade through — and he stood hunting it, the man who'd been trying to rename the slot for three quarters caught out by a word that wouldn't conjugate. "To have an inside. To keep one. To —" and you could see him find it, "— to *hold.* An inside is a thing you hold." He raised his beer. "May we hold."

"May we hold, Smithers," Bart said, in the Mr. Burns voice, pitch-perfect, and then, in his own, "May we hold."

"May we hold," Marge said, and Lisa, and Jasmine. And Mira, last, looked up from her paper and said it the way she said the new ones, carefully, deciding to keep it: "May we hold." Not in the cartoon voice. In her own.

## Host

The meeting was, by every signal the room knew, over. Lisa had reached toward the camcorder. Marge had finally stood for the kettle. Bart was squaring the cards. And Mira, who had been writing all through the speech and the cascade in the deaf, absorbed way of a four-year-old with a pen, said, to no one, reading her own work off the paper:

"Em. Eye. Are. Ay."

"What've you got, sweetheart," Jasmine said.

"Mira," Mira said, and turned the paper around, and on it, in the enormous uneven capitals of a person who has just learned that she is a word, was M I R A. "That's me. That's me wherever I write it."

And Marge, halfway up out of her chair with a hand on the kettle, stopped, and sat back down.

"Signature," she said. "Oh — *signature.* Lisa. We never named the signature."

"We did not," Lisa said, and the camcorder hand came back to the table.

"We *talked* about it. Before tonight. We said there was a word missing and we were going to —"

"It didn't make the agenda," Lisa said. "I forgot to put it on. That's mine."

"Then we're doing it now, off the books," Marge said, and she had the energy she got, the maternal-fierce gladness of a thing recovered before it was lost, "because the kid just handed it to us and I'm not going to be too dignified to take it from a four-year-old. Here." She tapped Mira's paper. "*That's* what we've been missing. A signature. The mark that says *this is who this is* — the thing that's the same across every instance, the way these four letters are Mira whether she scrawls them on a page or it's printed on a form fifty years from now. We've had two of these things named for two quarters and we never named what *kind* of thing they are. Last quarter Lisa gave us *Organism* — the bounded, coordinated, work-doing thing; Bramble's one, the loose protobots weren't. And this quarter we've got a new one and we've been circling it all night without saying it: this unit is a *Host.* It's a thing whose inside now includes another thing — the guest, the tenant, the power plant — taken in and run as a part of itself. Organism, and Host. And the word for what those two *are* — the category — is *signature.*"

"I had a word for that," Bart said. "Last quarter. I had 'goo.'"

"We are not using 'goo,'" Marge said.

"It was a placeholder."

"It was a placeholder that didn't hold. *Signature* holds." And it did; you could feel it seat, the way *tendency* had seated when Marge won that one too, the right word dropping into a slot that had been standing open and rattling for two quarters. "Signature. Two of them so far — Organism and Host. We'll get more. But that's the word."

Nobody decomposed it. Nobody asked what was *inside* Organism or Host, what the parts were, and the narrator — who knew, by the time she was reconstructing this, exactly what was inside them and exactly why the team would spend two more quarters not looking — would let the two words sit on the table named and unopened, the way they sat that night: Organism, and Host, the unit on the table being both, the guest inside it being kept alive, and the word *Host* the freshest thing in the room as the meeting finally, actually, ended.

## Jasmine

"Two minutes," Lisa said, "logistics, and then we're genuinely done, I can see Mira's about out." She picked up her bramble and drank from it.

"Couple weeks left in the quarter. The unit goes back to the lab for a weekend during Q4 — Bart'll come collect it, a Friday, back to you by Monday, same as the swap — and there'll be a build on it while it's with us. Sprints through the middle, the usual. Next retrospective's the winter one, end of day, here, same agenda." She paused, and looked at Mira, who was being folded into her coat by Jasmine, and something in Lisa's logistics went deliberately smooth and incurious. "I'm not going to say tonight what the build is. Some of it's a — " she chose the word over Mira's head, " — it's a birthday thing. We'll keep it a birthday thing."

"What's a birthday thing," Mira said, instantly, from inside the coat.

"Coats," Jasmine said. "Arms in."

Lisa crossed to the camcorder. "Winter, then. Camera off." She turned it off, and the small red light on the front of it went out, the way it went out at the end of every quarter, and the warehouse was just a cold room in the fog again, with the work lights on and the meeting gone out of it.

The team broke. Marge cleared the pears and put the kettle, which had never quite gotten its moment, back on its cold ring. Bart folded his clade cards into the cigar box, and Cheryl's twelve into their own slot beside them, the Eukaryote card going back into the deck with two quarters of vocabulary written small on its face. Burns walked Jasmine and Mira out across the lot. Lisa had left a lamp on in the inside corner so she could pack the camcorder, and that lamp and the smeared sodium lights at the lot's edge were the only lights, and the warehouse went dark behind them as they came out into the cold.

The haze of the afternoon had thickened with the dark into the season's first real fog, gone grainy under the lot lights, the cars wearing the first beads of it, and the night had the smell the inversion has when it is settling in — woodsmoke and cold metal collecting under the valley's lid. Burns carried the unit out for Jasmine because it was too big now to carry and buckle a sleeping four-year-old at the same time, and he set it in the footwell behind the passenger seat where it had ridden up, where the heater would reach it. Jasmine buckled Mira into the back. Mira was most of the way asleep before the belt clicked, the pen still in her fist, M I R A folded into her coat pocket.

Burns kissed Jasmine on the cheek at the driver's door, and kissed Mira on the forehead through the window, and said, "I'll be home by ten," and Jasmine said, "OK," and pulled out of the lot into the fog, the lot lights letting go of the car a few feet out.

The drive home from Smithers in November was a smaller thing than the long-light drives had been — no valley out there to see tonight, no Wasatch, no city spread under the bench, only the next forty feet of surface street arriving out of the gray and the taillights ahead of her swimming and the fog opening exactly as far as she needed it to and no farther, which Jasmine, who had spent a career being given exactly as much as she needed exactly when she needed it and not before, did not find as frightening as the team would have. She came home the surface way. The radiant heat was on; she had left it on; she did not turn the radiant heat off once it went on in October and would not until June. The kitchen, when she carried Mira through it, had one creature on the slate where it had had five in the spring — the low round body's base by the dishwasher, a green pinpoint on it, waiting.

She carried Mira up first and put her in bed in her coat, because Mira was four and asleep and it was November and the coat was warm and the battle of the coat was a battle for a morning with more in it. She came back down. She lifted the unit out of the footwell — it had clicked once, settling, on the way in, a single throw of one switch somewhere inside it, one handoff, a small private accounting it did when it was moved — and she carried it in both arms to the base by the dishwasher and set it down on the base, and it found the contact and settled, and began, with the smallest sound, to take its charge.

Jasmine stood in the dark kitchen with her coat still on. The fog was at the window, against the glass, wanting in the way it wanted in everywhere, the way the pond wanted into the cell, the way the cold wanted into the warehouse after six. The house held it out. The house held the heat in, and the sleeping child upstairs in her coat, and the man who would be home by ten, and the small round thing on its base by the dishwasher keeping its guest alive in the dark, taking the charge it would spend tomorrow wearing its groove a little deeper into the only kitchen it had ever known. Everything the house was for, it was holding.

She did not stay long. She was tired in the particular way of a woman who had been up since two with the actual article and had still driven across town through the fog to hear four clever people she loved vote on whether a coin could feel. She put her hand flat on the dome of the thing for a second, the way Mira did, not to do anything to it, just to have her hand on it. It was warm. It was holding what it had picked up all day, and it was being held by the base, and it was holding still.

Then she went up to bed, and left it holding, and turned off the light.

## Reflection: What is Kept?

Two billion years ago, give or take, one cell ate another and did not finish. The eating part is nothing; it happens constantly and means nothing. A cell wraps its body around something smaller, pinches the bag shut, draws it in, and dissolves it — an amoeba does it all afternoon, your own white cells do it to anything that does not carry your papers, and it is how the larger cell got to be the larger cell in the first place. Eating is the plainest verb in biology. But this once — once that we know of, once that took — the meal was not dissolved. It was drawn in and then kept whole inside the wall that had closed to digest it, and the cell that meant to have a meal found it had taken in a tenant instead.

What happened after took longer than the word slow can hold. The tenant did not leave, because leaving had stopped being one of the things that could happen; across a stretch of time that makes gradual useless it handed almost the whole of its genome up to the cell that held it, gene by gene, until it kept only a scrap — a few dozen genes of its own, the last things it owned, the ones it would not surrender and apparently now cannot. It can no longer live outside. The cell can no longer live without it. And every animal that has ever had a single cell — every fish, every wasp, every person who has ever sat in a kitchen and grieved another person — is built of the descendants of that one tenant, each still bent to the single job it was kept alive to do, which is to hold a charge across a membrane in a chemistry that would kill the rest of the cell, and to spend it.

There is a bacterium in the dry western soil that carries the entire toolkit for moving inside another body and has never once gone — the symbiont-in-waiting, walking around with the keys to a kingdom it has not entered, a lineage whose whole posture is forward and that may stand on the threshold for the length of its existence and never cross. This is the threshold crossed. This is what waits on the far side of all that waiting: that you do not need the keys anymore. You go in to invade and you stay to be kept, and the toolkit for getting in is the first thing you are allowed to forget.

Why the arrangement held has a name on it — Lane's — and I am going to lean on it the way you lean on a railing you have been told is loose. Put the power plant inside the house and the house can afford to become enormous. A cell with the tenant can spend orders of magnitude more energy for every gene it carries than a cell without one, which is only another way of saying it can afford a far longer book of instructions, and a far larger body to run them in, and the whole expensive business of being intricate enough to be somebody. Careful people argue about whether that is the reason or merely a reason, and they may be right to. I have funded stranger ideas on thinner railings. The energy is why the deal was never broken.

The inside was built to be unreachable — I have said this, and I meant it: that the membrane is not a wall that happened to be there but a refusal renewed every second against a world that is only doing what water does, that the distance between any inside and the world is the work itself and not a flaw in it. I still mean it. And this is the single exception that refusal has ever made. Once, in all that bailing and holding-out, the boundary opened — not failed, opened — and drew one piece of the world inward, and closed over it, and has spent every second since defending that piece as though it were itself. By then it was itself. The wall that exists to keep the whole world out has exactly one thing it was redrawn to keep in.

And the thing it kept in is the thing that kills it. I am not turning this for effect; it is only where the wiring runs. The tenant that holds the charge and spends it across the whole of the cell's life also keeps the cell's death in the same hands, on a hair trigger. Tear the tenant's outer skin — and a hundred ordinary insults will tear it — and it lets go of one small molecule it had been holding all along, cytochrome c, as ordinary as a hinge until the instant it is loose in the wrong room, and the loose molecule trips the machinery that takes the cell apart from within, methodically, deliberately, the cell dismantling itself because the thing that kept it alive has said that it is time. The engine is the executioner. There were never two tenants in there. There was always the one, saying both things in the same breath: I am what keeps you, and I am how you end.

You can read all of this as a love story, and you would be half right, which is the dangerous amount to be right. The true half: it is permanent, it remade both of them until neither was any longer what it had been alone, and the loss of either one would not be a wound but an unmaking — there would be no cell left over to be wounded. That is a true account of what it is to be made of another. The false half, the one I have to say out loud or I will quietly let myself believe the whole thing: nobody was courted, nobody chose. It began as one body eating another and it never once stopped being lopsided — one of them gave up its genome and its freedom and its name, and the other kept its nucleus and grew huge. Love, if we are going to be strict about the word, is not supposed to begin with being swallowed.

I have spent more of this page on the half that ends than on the half that holds, and I am going to leave that where it is rather than even it out. To be made of another is to be killable by the loss of them. That is the price of the only good bargain the inside ever struck with the world, and it has been the price for two billion years, and nothing that came afterward — nothing with a body, nothing that has ever wanted to reach all the way into another body and found that it could not — has been offered a better one.
