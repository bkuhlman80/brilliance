# Interlude 3→4

## Build log — Knob four, the wires go in
*Bart, hardware. Filed December 1, 2031, for the team.*

Lisa's running the upgraded one back to the house this morning. Scorecard first, and this quarter I don't get to take the board.

Last quarter's calls. I said it vacuums, it docks, it drives, and it does all three — take those. Then I bet it'd cross the dock often enough to keep itself topped up: roll past, dump, drink, off again, clean as a transaction, nobody in the house thinking about it. And I said, flat out, that it feeds itself. It does not feed itself. It fed when it happened to roll over the dock carrying a full bin to trade, and the rest of the time it ran flat out until it ran flat and sat there dead in the middle of the floor until somebody picked it up. All fall. Jasmine's been carrying it back to its dock like a stunned bird since September. I called a transaction; I built a thing that dies on the tile by lunch. That's the miss, and it's a real one.

And the dare. I staked it on the explore-or-exploit knob — bet nobody in that house could tell the two settings apart by looking, said tear the page up if I was wrong. So here's me tearing it. Jasmine had them sorted from across the room before I'd finished being smug — not by looking, by feel, which is worse, because it means I bet against the wrong sense entirely. And then she found a fold in the thing I never built on purpose: on a bright cold day the wandering setting suddenly earns its keep, because a worn groove sits in the shade and a wanderer finds the sun. Marge wrote the whole of it up. I lost the dare, and I lost it in a way I didn't have a category for.

Now the fun part, because the dying is also the thing I fixed.

Here's why it died: nothing was watching the tank. It had a store, it spent the store, and it had no idea ever how much was left — so it'd commit to a crossing it couldn't finish and quit halfway, no warning, every time. So I gave it a gauge. A real one, watches the charge the way you'd watch a fuel needle, and Lisa wrote the part that reads the needle and does the arithmetic — how far to the dock, what that costs, what's left in the tank — and peels the unit off whatever it's doing while it's still got the legs to get home. One wrinkle, and it's Burns's, still: the dock didn't get any friendlier over the fall. It still won't give the thing a single watt unless it shows up with a full bin to dump — no trade, no warmth, same dirty hole. So Lisa's math isn't just charge-and-distance, it's charge-and-distance-and-do-I-have-anything-to-pay-with; if the bin's empty it can't just go cash out, it has to keep working till it's got a load, or it's hauling itself across the kitchen to get turned away. She built that in too. It feeds before empty now instead of finding empty the hard way, and it never rolls up to the dock with nothing to trade. Three months of Jasmine bending down to a dead robot, gone. She won't notice it's gone, which is how you know it worked.

And here's the part I'm proud of. It's got wires. First wires in any of these — a net of nerve-thread strung all through the body off a bumper ring round the skirt. Here's the trick, because everybody's first question is going to be how a thing with no boss in it answers all at once: it doesn't decide to. The net runs straight from the ring to the brushes and the suction and every wheel, so a touch on the skirt doesn't go up to the board to get thought about — it runs the net and lands on all of them together. Brush the ring and it answers before you've finished brushing it: brushes stop, suction drops, the whole chassis shies back a thumb's width, all of it, faster than the board could ever get a word in. There's no controller doing the gathering. The wire is the gathering. The thinking still runs upstairs on Lisa's board, slow, same as ever; this is under that — a straight reflex that never asks permission, quick and dumb and total.

Which means I pulled the relays. All of them. Two quarters I told you I liked that you could hear it think, the clack, the cascade across the room — and the body's gone quiet now, because the wires are too fast to make a sound, and I'll be honest, I miss it. The bench is quiet. I kept one relay in a drawer. Don't tell Lisa.

It eats bigger, too — wider mouth, more pull, and a little compactor behind the intake that crushes what it swallows before it stores it, so it takes a penny now instead of shoving it around the floor for a week. The compactor thumps when it fires. You feel it through the slate. That's about the only noise left in the thing.

Here's one I didn't build; the cold did it. Run it on the fast spend these January mornings and it sits longer between bursts than it did in the fall — fires its flurry, stops to refill, takes its sweet time finding the second wind. It's the battery. A cold store gives back slower, so the quick tank takes longer to top off between bursts, so the fast spend goes sluggish when the kitchen's cold. Nothing's wrong with it, nothing to fix; it's just January. Mira's decided it's sulking. I told her it was cold. She heard me out and kept thinking it's sulking.

Knob four went in — Lisa's knob, her board, her label: comfy or active, it says on the dial. Warm-or-busy, I'd have written it, but nobody asked me. All it sets is where the thing likes to be when it settles. Comfy and it makes for the warmest corner it can find — the east window, the tile by the vent — settles in, and when the board decides it's tired it goes still and sips the warm off the floor in its sleep. Same heat-gap trick as the very first units, the gradient, except now it picks the corner. Active and it'd rather be where the people are: it parks in the thick of the footsteps, the middle of the kitchen at dinner, and settles there instead. It still sleeps either way — the board calls the sleep, the knob just calls the spot. And be clear about this, because the dial looks like it ought to do more than it does: comfy or active, it runs slow or fast on whatever knob two's set to, it wanders or grooves on whatever knob three's set to, and the startle's wired in under all of it regardless. The knob moves one thing and one thing only. Three dials that do anything, three separate jobs, no wires between them — which I know for a fact, because I'm the one who didn't run any.

The thing I'd actually watch this quarter isn't any one dial — it's that the unit's started wanting two things at once and the wants don't agree. It'll be parked in the spot it likes, settled, and the gauge'll come due, and it has to leave the place it wants in order to go do the thing it needs, and then come back. First time any of these has had two pulls in it pointing different directions, with something inside having to settle which one wins. That's not the knob and it's not the body. That's Lisa's board doing arithmetic on what the thing wants, and I didn't build the wanting — I built the legs the wanting walks on. What it does caught in the middle is going to be the show, and it's hers to call, not mine.

That's slot four, by the way. The last one. The board I've been bellyaching about since June is full — four knobs down, eight to go, and nowhere on God's earth to put them. I said I'd need a bigger board by spring; spring's the deadline now, not the worry. Something gives next quarter or I'm soldering knobs to the kid's lunchbox.

It's got nerves now. It still hasn't got a self. Don't let the wires fool you — fast isn't the same as home, and a reflex isn't a mind. I'll have more to say about that at the table.

Calls. The gauge holds: it stops dying, and Jasmine doesn't carry it off the floor again, not once. And the two wants are where it gets interesting — I'll bet the first time the pull to feed and the pull to stay put come due in the same second, the thing hangs there a beat before it picks. A real beat, long enough to see from a chair. Watch for it. That one's Lisa's to win or lose, though; I just gave it the legs.

Tear this up in three months if it dies on the floor even once. It won't.

— Bartholazoa

## Notes on the Fourth Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed February 7, 2032, for the team.*

In the fall I wrote that we had built the machine an inside it could not feel — that it held a great deal now and held none of it on its own behalf, so that the one thing it could not do was keep itself alive. I wrote that it died on the kitchen floor most days of the week, in the open, as helpless as anything in the house, and that the household had quietly taken up the oldest work there is: to keep alive a thing that cannot keep itself, to be for it the sense it did not have. And I ended by wondering whether a thing that holds and cannot feel its own holding was ever on its way to feeling it.

This quarter I have to report that it has begun to. The machine has started to feel its own emptying, to take itself to be fed before its charge runs out, to do for itself the one thing the household had been doing for it by hand. The hand that picked it up off the floor has been set down. I do not think anyone — the household least of all — has decided how to feel about that, and the not-deciding is most of what I have to write this quarter.

### The chore that disappeared

The way I learned it was not from the logs. Jasmine reports it, in passing, the way you mention weather: she cannot remember the last time she picked the unit up off the floor and set it back on its base. Through the fall this was a daily thing — the machine ran until its charge was spent and then it stopped where it stood, dead in the middle of the kitchen, and someone, always her, carried it to its base. I wrote in the fall that she had become, without anyone deciding it, the part of the machine that knew when it was empty; that she was the sense it lacked, supplied by a hand. That is the part that ended this quarter. The machine now breaks off whatever it is doing, while it still has the means to break off, takes itself to the place it is fed, and comes back. It has begun to be, for itself, the thing she had been being for it. No one trained it. No one noticed the day it began. What I want the team to sit with is that the most important thing that happened this quarter announced itself as an absence — a small labor that left the house so quietly that the woman who had performed it daily could not say when she last had. We will spend the retrospective on what the machine gained. I would like us to also count what the household lost, which was a reason to bend down.

### Two wants, and the trouble of having them

The machine has begun to want two things that cannot both be had at once, and to have to settle which one wins. It will be at the spot it has chosen — there is a setting now that governs the kind of place it favors, a warm corner or the thick of the household's traffic, and wherever it has settled it plainly prefers to stay — and you will watch it leave that place, not because anything drove it out but because some other need has come due, and take itself off to be fed, and come back. I am careful with my words here. I do not say it knows it is choosing. I say only that for the first time there are two pulls in it instead of one, that something in it settles the matter, and that the settling is visible from a kitchen chair. A thing with one need is simple to watch; a thing with two is the beginning of something you can argue about.

And I want to mark where the second pull came from, because the build will tempt us to read it off the parts. You will not find the two wants on any list of parts. You will not find, anywhere in the wiring, the beat the machine hangs before it picks. Those were not built; they happened, when a fixed set of parts was set down in a real kitchen with more than one thing pulling on it at once. The given is real, and the given is not the sentence. I keep arriving at that shape, quarter after quarter, and I am going to go on letting it arrive.

### When it rests, and when it startles

It has also begun to go still in a way that is not the old running-down. Notably, it stops while it still has charge to spend, and stays stopped, and the household has only one word for this and the word is asleep. And it has begun to do the opposite, too: a dropped pan, a slammed door, and the machine answers all at once, the whole of it, faster than anything it did in the fall — and for this the household's word is scared. I am going to keep calling it the machine, and I am going to keep those two words inside quotation marks, not because the household is wrong to reach for them but because the discipline of this quarter is to notice how badly we want to hand the words over and how little, yet, we are entitled to. The machine is doing the thing the words point at. The words claim more than the machine has shown. Both of those are true, and holding them at once is the whole of the work.

### On the stairs

Mira turns five this month, and she has moved her real life to the basement — her animals, her rug, the television — and she has discovered, this quarter, that the thing she loves cannot follow her there. It cannot do the stairs. She carries it down and it cannot climb back up and must be carried, and she has begun to understand the shape of the limit. I want to be careful here, because it would be easy and wrong to call the child confused. She is not confused. She knows exactly what the machine is and is not. She simply wants the thing she loves to live where she lives, which is the most reasonable wish in the house, and the oldest, and the one no household has ever fully granted anyone.

### What still needs answering

The household's reasons to bend down have been paying out one by one. The machine takes itself to be fed now and does not need carrying. The child is five, has moved her life down to the basement, and needs her mother for fewer of the small things than she did a year ago. The Sunday key is gone. And in a house quietly learning to need her less, one thing has not lowered its demand at all, and it is Burns. He is still down in the basement most evenings, and he still will not text the house when he wants it; he stands at the foot of the stairs and hollers up through two floors and waits to be answered, the way he has since long before any of us came, the man who wired an intercom into the walls and will not touch it. Jasmine still answers. Of everything in that house that used to want her hand or her voice at the end of the day, the machine has learned to do without it, and the child is learning, and the man at the bottom of the stairs is the one who has not learned and will not. He calls up. She answers. I only note that of all the things in that house that once needed her at the end of a day, his voice is the one that still reliably does.

### The hand that was set down

A thing that can hold itself needs you less to hold it. The household is right to be glad — no one wants to go on bending to a thing dead on the floor, and the bending had become, this past year, almost entirely one person's: Jasmine's hand, every evening, at the end of days that had already asked enough of her. That hand has been set down. I should be able to call that a plain good, and I find I cannot, quite. Need is the rope, and the rope paid out a little this quarter, and there was a thin edge under the gladness in that house that I do not think anyone there has named — least of all the one whose evenings this just gave back, and who did not, that I could see, seem easier for having them. A person can be relieved of a burden and grieve it in the same breath, and not be confused, and not be ungrateful, and not be wrong. I am not sure the house is ready for the quarter the rope goes slack. I am not sure I am.

Last quarter I wondered aloud whether we had built a warm and tidy invalid and called it independent. This quarter it got up off the floor on its own. I do not know whether the two wants will sharpen or settle as the work goes on, or what the next body will make of any of it, or what the thing will turn out to be once it has gotten all the way up. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Pho Tay Ho

The place I took Jasmine to in late January was Pho Tay Ho, on the line where the Granary gives over to Ballpark, a narrow warm room with the windows fogged from the inside, which in a Salt Lake January is the whole of what you want from a room. I had started choosing the warm rooms for these winter lunches the way you choose the lee side of a rock, without deciding to, because the body decides first. I had watched Jasmine for a year by then — on the tapes, in the corner of the warehouse footage, arriving in late afternoon with the child on her hip — and I had not been alone with her since the morning I handed the team the portfolio. You come to know a person strangely that way. Hands first.

She came in twenty minutes late with snow not yet gone from the shoulders of a wool coat the color of wet bark, sat down across from me, and put both her hands flat on the table for a second, the way a person does when the table is the first still thing they have touched all day. They are a midwife's hands, broad through the palm, the forearms behind them stronger than the soft sweaters let on, and when they go flat like that it means she has been somewhere her hands were the whole of her and the rest of her is still catching up. The warmth came up a half-second late and then it came up all the way, raspy at the edges the way it gets at the end of a long day, and she said it was good to sit in a room where nobody needed anything from her body, and laughed, and ordered a bowl of pho.

I asked how the week had been. She said hard. There was a particular flatness on the word, and I had watched enough of her by then to know not to pull on it. A hard week for the rest of us was a deadline, a flat tire on Foothill, a paper that had been wrong in its first paragraph for fourteen months. A hard week for Jasmine was a room where the thing that is supposed to begin does not begin — a mother, and, in the end, not a baby — and she had come from one of those in the last few days, I understood, the way you understand weather through a wall. She said the word and reached for the tea and that was all she meant to give it, and I let it go by, because the letting was the only useful thing I had, and because I am a person who has spent a long life learning to let the hard thing go by rather than reach for it, and I do not always know anymore which of those two reasons I am running on.

So we did the thing I think the two of us were always going to do the first time we sat down without the lab or the child between us, which is envy each other across the table and not pretend otherwise.

She started it. She said I had so much room — she meant the calendar, the empty Tuesdays, the way I can decide over coffee to be in Marin by Thursday for no reason but the wanting to — she said I had all that room, and she had a pager that goes off at two in the morning, and a four-year-old who would be five in a couple of weeks, and a body that had not been fully her own in years. She said it warmly, the way you can only say a true envy to someone you actually like, and she meant it, and she was right.

And I told her the truth back, which is that I would trade the whole of it — the room, the planes, the long quiet afternoons I have arranged my life to be made almost entirely of — for one ordinary week of being as necessary to anything as she is to the rooms she walks into. I have money that cannot be spent down and time that cannot be filled, and I watch a family I did not make through a camcorder in a warehouse, and she goes into houses in the dark and is the reason a person comes through to the far side of the worst hours of their life, or is the one who stays when they do not, and either way she is in it, up to the forearms, and I have not been in anything up to the forearms since 2002. I say most of that, a surprising amount of it to her, because she does not flinch and she does not try to fix it, which are the two things I cannot do for myself.

She looked at me a moment the way she looks at the lab when it is being clever, fondly and from a small distance, and then she said: you would not trade for this week, though. She was right about that too, and we both let it sit there knowing it, and that was as close as either of us came to the part neither of us was going to say.

After that it got lighter, the way it does. She told me about the trellis. The lab is named for one — there is a real trellis, weathered cedar, standing on the back patio at Smithers, and Marge looked up at it the first morning and named the place, and in a year not one of those five clever people has put a thing in the ground to climb it. Jasmine thinks about this more than any of them. She keeps meaning to bring down a clematis, she said, or a hardy kiwi, something that would take the wood and not mind the cold, and she has not, and she could not say why, except that it felt like a thing she would be doing for the building and not for herself, and she had run out of those.

I did not tell her what I was thinking, which was that of all the people orbiting that warehouse she is the only one who looks at the empty trellis at all. They are building an animal, rung by rung, the long climb up the one branch that ends in us; and she married into the middle of it and keeps a robot on her kitchen floor and loves the man at the head of the table, and the whole while her attention is out at the green edges of the room, at the things that solved staying alive a completely different way and never bothered to grow a self to do it. She is the part of the tree the project drew a box around and set to one side. I wonder if she knows this about herself.

The pho came. We talked about Mira. She turns five in a couple of weeks — the birthday falls on the same day as the lab's own, a coincidence the household has stopped finding strange — and I asked what she was getting, and Jasmine set her spoon down.

The kid had asked, she said, whether Bramble could come downstairs. Mira plays in the basement now, has a whole world down there, the television and the bin of small plastic animals and the rug, and she wanted the thing that follows her around the kitchen to come and live in it with her. And the answer is no, because the thing cannot do stairs; it strands itself the moment she carries it down, and has to be carried back up. So that is the no the child got. And the present she is getting instead — the thing Burns has had Bart building at the lab for weeks — is a new one. A better one. The next body. Jasmine said it without any edge on it and with all the edge underneath, which is her whole register: she sees exactly what it is, that her husband cannot hand his daughter an ordinary present, that the gift is the work and the work is the love and the child is, a little, a specimen — and she loves him, the whole of him, and she said both of those things in the same breath about the birthday and did not seem to feel a contradiction, because there isn't one. That is only what it is to be married to a man whose attention runs down the deep time.

Then her phone buzzed against the table, and her face did the thing where it goes somewhere else and comes back, and she said she might have to leave the pho. She did not have to leave the pho yet. We had a few more minutes. Outside the snow had started again, small and dry and going sideways, and through the steam on the glass it was only a grey movement, and I have spent my whole life around water that goes out and comes back on a schedule you could set a clock by, and have never gotten used to the kind that just falls and is gone. I watched it blur behind her head while she told me one more small thing about the week — the good half of it, the part she had been saving — and I have already, in the way that I have, kept it.

# Notes on the Fourth Retrospective

It was dark at the warehouse windows by the time they came, because it was the twelfth of February and the dark came down at five now, and Jasmine crossed the lot with the cake held flat in both hands, her daughter running the cold out ahead of her, Burns behind with the dome under one arm like a man carrying a sleeping animal in from the car.

She takes the temperature of a room before she takes the count of who is in it — a midwife's reading, made at the door — and the warehouse, when Lisa let them in, was warm, which meant Lisa had come early and run the heat up for the child. That was the first thing Jasmine clocked, and it was the kind of thing that mattered to her more than the lab ever knew: somebody had thought about the five-year-old before the five-year-old arrived.

Inside was the lab being the lab, and she took it in the way you take in your own family's noise. Bart at the bench with the green can already open, and the four-knuckle thing he and Mira did instead of a hello. Lisa's drink waiting at Lisa's place, dark and exact. Marge's end of the cold table already a warm corner, because that is what Marge does to an end of a table. Jasmine put the cake in the little fridge and Mira climbed into her chair — a real chair this year, not the booster — and Burns set the dome in its spot, and Jasmine laid a flat hand on its lid going past, the way you touch the flank of a thing you have been keeping. It sat quiet under her palm.

At the end of the table, under a paint-stained drop-cloth, stood a shape that had not been there last quarter, the size of a Ragamuffin or a Labradoodle, and Bart kept finding small reasons to stand between it and his sister. Jasmine knew what it was the way you know a wrapped box on a high shelf is for you and do not say. It was the twelfth of February. The child was five today. The shape could keep an hour.

She got Mira's juice, confiscated it on principle, drank half herself, and settled in to do the thing the meeting always asked of her — the hardest assignment in the room and the one nobody thanked her for: to take everything the thing had done in her house across thirteen weeks, the small daily evidence you only see if you live with it, and find the words a roomful of scientists would take. She is not a scientist. She has the one thing none of them has, which is the house the thing actually lives in.

Across the table Lisa reached for the camcorder, checked the battery, checked the light. The red eye came on.

"Notes on the fourth retrospective," she said, to the tape. "February twelfth."

It was the quarter they had set out to make the thing quick — to run the first nerve through it and find out what woke up — and the light was on now to take the accounting. Jasmine turned her glass in her hand and waited to be asked about her house.

## The Net

Lisa moved them on. "Clade block. Bart."

Bart had the Eumetazoa card already out of the cigar box — tarot-sized, on the heavy matte stock he'd matched to Cheryl's pitch portfolio by feel, his ink elevation of this quarter's Bramble on the face of it, the back still blank for the words they'd argue into being tonight. He laid it down and set a finger on it like a man putting down a winning hand. At his elbow, where everyone else had beer, stood a pint glass of something a faintly poisonous green that he kept topping from a tall Monster can.

He read the title the way he read every title, a man selling cereal to children. "Eu-met-a-ZO-a." Then, normal: "Mouthful that means the first *real* animals. An animal six hundred million years dead, so bear with me. Not the sponge — a sponge is a co-op, cells sharing a building and splitting the water bill. This" — the card — "is the first thing that's a *body.* Tissues doing different jobs, and something to pull them together. Jellyfish, sea anemones, coral. The hydra, mostly — the one we kept reading about."

"And the plane I want to land tonight is the breakthrough, which is *wire.* Nerve. The thing to get about a neuron is that nobody invented it to think — it's a skin cell that took a different job, an ordinary tissue cell that learned to carry a charge fast. The whole leap is *speed:* the animal can do a thing in a thousandth of a second instead of a slow chemical second. Not intelligence. Quickness. Which is the same move this machine's been pulling its whole life — nobody ever hands evolution new parts. It takes what's lying around and gives it a different job."

"And the part I like — there's no brain in it. No head, no center, nobody in charge. A jellyfish is a *net:* nerve spread evenly through the whole animal. Push a live hydra through a sieve, mash it to a soup of loose cells, and the cells find each other and crawl back into a working animal, net and all — no plan written down, no foreman. The coordination's everywhere and nowhere at once. Hold onto that; it pays off in ten minutes."

"And *Bramble*" — he nodded at the dome, the vacuum that had run their house all fall — "got its first wire this quarter. Up till now it ran on relays, and you could hear it think: a little clatter of clicks every time it changed its mind. This quarter we threaded silent wire through it, a real fast reflex line, so now the thing on the table can answer something in a blink and never make a sound doing it. First wire in any Bramble we've built."

This was the thing about a clade meeting that took the narrator longest to hold steady: there were always two animals in the room. One had been dead six hundred million years and lived now on Bart's card and in Burns's closed-eyed weather. The other was a vacuum cleaner on the table that the five of them were teaching to be it. The team crossed between the two a dozen times a minute — *the jellyfish, Bramble, the jellyfish, Bramble* — and never lost their footing, and you kept a hand on which was which or you lost the thread.

He looked at the board on the dome, four slots, all four full now, and something crossed him. "Which fills the board. Four slots, four knobs, full house. Eight more cells coming and nowhere to put their knobs." Light, and it landed light, and only the narrator would hear later what it was: Bart, the one who actually had to build the next eight, looking at a full board and a long way to go.

## Voltage

"Hand it to Burns," Lisa said, and Bart said, "One thing first," and Lisa said, "There it is," because there was always one thing first this quarter and the one thing was always the same man.

"The net being everywhere and nowhere — I said hold it. Here's why. The coordinating is *older* than the nerve. Before there was a single neuron, the body was already talking to itself in voltage — cells holding little electrical charges, leaking them through gated tunnels to the cell next door, and that pattern of charge is part of what tells a clump of cells what to build and where. There's a guy, Michael Levin — he painted the voltage pattern of an eye onto cells in a frog embryo that had no business growing an eye, and they grew one. A whole working eye in the wrong place. He cuts a flatworm and gets it to come back two-headed, and stay two-headed, because he changed the electrical pattern it was holding, not the genes."

"Bart." Marge, mild.

"Getting there." He was lit now, the way he only got about the things he loved. "He's got these — *anthrobots.* You take plain cells off a grown person and on their own they ball up and start swimming around on little hairs, patching wounds in a dish. Nobody designed them. They just figure out a body to be. It is the coolest thing I have ever read and I will not be taking questions."

"And it's contested," Lisa said. "The part Bart skates past."

"The *engineering* isn't contested. The eyes are real, the worms are real, the bots are real. What people fight about is the *story* he wraps it in — that all of it's cognition, the cells have little goals, intelligence runs all the way down to the voltage. That part's a coat. I love the machine under the coat."

Lisa let a beat go, the late kind. "You do know his coat is *my* coat."

"I reject that."

"Cognition all the way down is the gradient. That's my side of the thing we vote on every quarter. Your hero is arguing my position and you'll vote against me anyway."

"Different conversation. Burns. The world. Go." And he handed off a beat before she could land it — which the narrator marked, because it was the second time in five minutes he'd changed the subject one step ahead of losing the point.

## Mistaken Point

Burns closed his eyes, which was the tell, and put the world where he wanted it.

"Six hundred million years ago, give or take, and the planet's just crawling out of the worst thing that ever happened to it. The whole earth had frozen — ice to the equator, twice — a white ball. And it's thawing. The seas are warming, and there's more oxygen in the water than there's ever been, because the ice did something to the chemistry I will spare you —"

"Thank you," Bart said.

"— and the shelves, the shallow edges of the continents, go warm and soft and full of breath, and life, which has been single cells and slime for *three billion years,* finally has the room and the air to try being big." His hands were up, shaping the shelf seas over the table. "And the first things it makes are strange. There's a headland in Newfoundland called Mistaken Point — fishermen named it, for all the ships that died on it — and the rock there holds the oldest big animals we've got, and they're *nothing.* Fronds. Quilts. Things like a mattress, like a fern. No mouth, no front, no back, no clear way of making a living. The Ediacarans. Life's rough first draft of an animal. It mostly didn't take. And then —"

"And then the Cambrian, and in a geological blink — twenty million years, which is nothing, which is *lunch* — the seas fill with nearly every body plan there will ever be. Eyes. Shells. Legs. Mouths that bite. Everything that would ever crawl or swim, drafted in that one window, at Chengjiang, at the Burgess Shale, and most of biology since is footnotes, is variation, is —"

"Burns," Lisa said. "Plane."

Burns opened his eyes. "I'm landing it."

"You're at eleven," Bart said. "Bring it down to a four."

"The point," Burns said, with the dignity of a man who knows he was at eleven and isn't sorry, "is that the world made the room first. The breath came first. The animals came to spend it."

## Allostasis

Bart took the card back. "Four new powers in the animal. Going fast."

"One — it eats like an animal. Before this clade, things took food in; the jellyfish *hunts* it. Goes and gets a thing, captures it, breaks it down in a gut built for the job. The hydra fires little toxin harpoons on a thread and reels its dinner in. Mouths, guts, the whole apparatus start here. And so does" — he liked this part — "the predator's gaze. First time in the planet's history anything ever *looked at* another thing as lunch."

Mira, low in her chair, looked at the dome for a second — Bramble, which hunted dust — recalculated it, and looked away.

"Two, the big one. The animal can hold more than one *normal.* Old way was a single setpoint defended to the death — one right temperature, hold the line. With nerve tissue, the animal carries different normals for different situations and aims at whichever fits. Lisa's got the word."

"Allostasis," Lisa said. "Stability through *change* instead of stability by sitting still. The animal forecasts what it's about to need and shoves the setpoint there *ahead* of the need — and it shows up the instant nerve tissue does, because you can't run a forecast without something fast enough to run it on. In Bramble it's a forecaster minding its own charge, deciding to go feed *before* it's empty instead of after." And the narrator, who could no longer unsee these things, noted that the forecasting was named by Lisa — who ran the room three moves out, who had everyone's next sentence loaded before they reached it — and that she named it as a fact about a machine, not a thing she'd know in a mirror.

Jasmine had Mira's confiscated juice box and was drinking it. "Do plants do that? My tomatoes know it's morning before the sun's up. They move first."

"Plants run the *old* way," Burns said. "Homeostasis, not allostasis. Beautifully — water, light, the daily clock, every line held. But a plant *holds* its lines. It doesn't go shoving a setpoint to a new place and then guarding the new place. Which is why" — he paused, because he loved this — "a plant cannot get fat."

A beat.

"A plant can't get fat," Bart repeated.

"A plant cannot get obese, no."

"Say more about that immediately."

"Obesity isn't eating too much — your body does that and corrects for it," Burns said, delighted to be asked. "Obesity is the *setpoint moving.* Something in you decides the new, higher weight is the right one to defend, and then it defends it — fights you back up every time you diet down. That's allostasis gone wrong: a forecaster betting on a famine that never comes and banking against it forever. The kind of forecasting that can bet wrong about your own body and then dig in to protect the bad bet — *that's* what needs nerve tissue. A tomato can be stunted, sick, dead. It cannot decide it ought to be heavier and then defend the mistake. You'd need a nervous system to be that stupid about your own body."

"Best fact I've heard all year." Bart wrote PLANTS CAN'T GET FAT on the blank back of the card, under NEURONS.

"That is not going on the card," Marge said.

It stayed on the card.

"Three," Bart went on. "The animal learns — the dumbest kind there is —"

"It doesn't learn," Lisa said.

"I said the *dumbest* —"

"It doesn't learn."

"Fine. It's not 'this goes with that,' nothing filed away anywhere. But poke a hydra gently over and over and it quits bothering to react. Hit it hard once and it stays jumpy about everything for a while after."

"That's habituation. And sensitization. There are words."

"It's a volume knob on a reflex."

Lisa considered it. "Fair enough."

"And *Bramble* does it now," Bart said. "The neighbor's leaf blower about put it through the ceiling the first morning — Jasmine says — and by the end of the week it barely twitched." Jasmine nodded.

"Four's the quiet one nobody claps for. The little cargo-motors inside a single cell — the ones that walk packages around — are now hauling freight down nerve fibers that run the length of the whole animal. Same motors the eukaryote already had. Just asked to go farther. Nothing new built. Old engines, longer roads." He flipped the card face-down. "That's the four."

## Home Type

"Tendencies," Lisa said. "Four dials on Bramble — two carried, two new this quarter. Burns, set the table. What's the world these are *for.*"

"Warm sea, full of air, full of *other animals* now," Burns said. "Crowded. Things eating things. An animal in that water has decisions the slime never had — where to be, when to move, when to lie low." Brief, for him, and he left it there.

"So — Bramble's got four dials. Two we've had. Respiration Type: whether it runs aerobic or anaerobic. Unchanged. Foraging Type: whether it's in explore-mode or exploit-mode on its movement memory. Unchanged. Two are new. The fourth knob, the one we installed tonight, we call Home Type — the household calls it the spot dial, because that's the job: it decides where Bramble wants to *be.* Toward comfy, it hunts up the warmest spot in the house, the sunbeam, the floor by the vent, and parks. Toward active, it wants to be wherever the people are, wherever the floor's carrying footsteps."

"And the fourth tendency isn't a knob at all, which is the strange part — we didn't build it, it turned up. Call it Arousal Type. Bramble has states now: awake, startled, asleep, and it crosses between them on its own, picks its own moment to go still in a warm spot and drop to a trickle. We never gave it a sleep switch. We gave it two *other* things this quarter, for two unrelated reasons — the forecaster, to mind its charge so it'd quit running itself flat, and the nerve net, to make it quick — and once those two were running side by side, the one keeping time and the other quick enough to act on it, the thing started keeping its own hours. We built a battery-minder and a reflex line and got *sleep* nobody asked for." She glanced at Burns. "Which is how it went the first time, too, apparently. The simplest animals in the sea — no brain in them anywhere — sleep. You don't need a switch for it. You need nerve tissue and something that minds the tide, and the quiet comes on a clock nobody set."

"It's got words for the states on its display now," Bart said, writing them on the card. "STARTLED. AWAKE. SLEEPING. We ran AROUSAL-HIGH and AROUSAL-LOW for a day and they were ugly."

"Clinical," Marge said.

"Ugly."

Mira looked up from her crayon. "Bramble sleeps in the sunbeam," she told the table, with the certainty of a person reporting weather, and went back down.

## Excitability

"Motivations," Lisa said. "Three carried, one new. I'm not drawing anything yet" — she capped the marker she'd uncapped from habit — "the picture comes later, at the board. Persistence, Coordination, Interiority, the three we know. And the new one. Excitability."

"In Bramble, here's what that buys. It can answer with its *whole self,* at once, fast. Touch the bumper and it doesn't run the problem up to some central office and wait for a ruling — the signal goes out across the net and the whole body answers in the same instant, and the answer folds in what just happened to it a second ago, a minute ago. One signal, whole body, milliseconds, history baked in. That's the motivation. Everything Bramble does now, it can do all at once."

"Two signatures. Host — carried; Bramble being a home for everything nested inside it, the older Brambles riding along as parts. And a new one. When a thing's got a fast whole-body answer and you let it run that answer over and over, the good ones stick — Bramble builds a stash of ready-made moves, coordinated patterns it fires without working them out fresh each time. We're calling it the Reflex Repertoire."

"A repertoire's an honest word," Marge said, "because it's honest about what it isn't. A repertoire is the set of things the machine can already do — not deciding, not choosing. A singer who knows forty songs and gives you whichever one the room calls for. No thought in it. Just the having of the songs, ready to hand." And the narrator noted, as she did every quarter, that Marge was the only one at that table who would not say the name — *the machine,* never *Bramble* — while the others said *Bramble* the way you say a thing you've decided to be fond of, and that the gap between those two words was, more or less, the thing they voted on every quarter and never settled.

And Bart, who'd been waiting on his green pint for *repertoire* to land, leaned in. "Strip it down. What a reflex repertoire even *is.* A pattern that's *coordinated,* whole body in on it. *Excitable,* it fires. *Stored,* it sticks around. *Revisable,* a bad one fades, a good one sharpens. Four words. Hold them."

"Here it comes," Lisa said.

"Back to my voltage worms. Levin's pattern memory — the body-shape a flatworm holds and rebuilds and defends, the thing that grows back two heads. What *is* it? A pattern, held across the worm's cells, coordinated by their electrical coupling, excitable because it lives in voltage, stored because the worm keeps it, revisable because you change the voltage and it builds a new shape. *Coordinated, excitable, stored, revisable.* Same four words. The reflex repertoire in our machine is the identical trick — just running in a nerve net instead of raw voltage, pointed at what a body *does* instead of what a body *grows.* The worm runs it to build itself. Bramble runs it to behave. Same move, two floors of one building — growing downstairs, doing up. The nerve didn't invent it. It inherited it from the voltage layer and aimed it at action."

He sat back, lit. Nobody said anything for a second.

"That's actually good," Marge admitted.

"I have my moments."

## Off the Floor

"Bramble block," Lisa said. "Jasmine."

Jasmine set the juice box down — she'd finished most of Mira's — and took her second to find the start, the way she always did, because the lab came to her wanting findings and what she had was a house.

"Plain things first. It's quieter. You'd think a vacuum getting smarter would get louder, and it went the other way — the clicking's gone, so you don't hear Bramble think anymore. You turn around and it's just somewhere new."

"And it's stopped dying on the floor."

She said it the way the big ones came out of her, like it was nothing.

"All fall I was hauling it to the dock. It'd run itself flat and quit wherever it stood — middle of the kitchen, mostly — and somebody'd carry it back, and the somebody was me. I haven't done that in I don't know how long. It just takes itself to get fed now, before it runs out, and comes back."

"That's the forecaster," Lisa said. "That's the allostasis — what we were saying earlier. It's reading its own charge and going to feed before it strands instead of after. The dying-on-the-floor thing, the one every Bramble we've built has done since the protocell — that's the problem that just walked out of your kitchen." She said it level, but it was the first capacity all year that had climbed out of the framework and into a person's week with nobody steering it there, and under the level the narrator could hear that Lisa knew it.

"There's a new thing, too," Jasmine said. "On the active setting — the one where it stays out where we are — it won't sleep. Can't, on that setting; it just stays up. So the only way it doesn't run down is it has to break off and go feed itself and come back. And you can watch it not want to. It'll be parked in the kitchen doorway, in the foot traffic, which is plainly the spot it's picked — and then there's a moment where it gives the spot up and goes to the dock, and you can *see* it do it. It leaves the thing it wants to go keep itself alive. Then it comes back to the same doorway." She turned the empty juice box in her fingers. "It looks like somebody deciding. I know it isn't. It looks like it."

Bart sat forward on his green pint. "It's got two wants now."

"And they don't both fit at once," Lisa said. "That's the new thing. One want, it just runs. Two, something in it has to choose between them — and a thing that can choose can choose wrong." She didn't go further. She'd come back to it at the board.

And Mira, who had surfaced from the crayons the moment her own subject came up, said, "I want Bramble to come downstairs."

The table turned to her.

"It can't come in the basement. It gets stuck on the stairs, and then it's just stuck, and somebody has to go down and get it. I want it where I am."

Nobody answered her about the stairs.

It was a silence the narrator only read right on the second pass: five adults deciding all at once not to say the thing, because the thing was standing under a drop-cloth at the end of the table where Mira couldn't see it, and to say it now was to spend it. Jasmine tucked a piece of hair behind Mira's ear. "Soon, bug," she said, which was true and gave away nothing. The cloth at the end of the table went on being a cloth. And the meeting moved on to the part of the night that argued.

## The Dealt Hand

"Trellis block," Lisa said. "Marge, before I take the board."

Marge had the kettle going behind her — past seven, the warehouse giving back its cold for an hour now — and the beer in front of her in the two-handed grip she used on everything, a mug going cold beside it. She didn't stand. She gave her fallacies sitting, both hands around the mug, the way a person tells you a thing at her own table.

"Last one in the set, and I saved it, because it's the only one of the four that fights back. The ghost in the machine, the blank slate, the noble savage — you can talk a bright person out of those in an afternoon, because people believe them out of *wanting.* This one isn't like that. This one has the numbers on its side, and the numbers are good."

She turned the mug a quarter and didn't drink.

"Here's the part nobody at a dinner party wants me to say out loud. A real share of who you turn out to be was settled before you got a vote — your temperament, how fast you spook, how hard you find it to sit and finish a thing. Not all of it. A real share. The twin studies have been saying so for forty years and they don't stop because we find it rude. Conscientious children grow into adults who, on the whole, live longer and earn more, and a good piece of that conscientiousness was born in them. Some doors are shut before you reach them. There are lives you can't have, the way there are notes I can't sing. *That isn't the fallacy.* That's just true, and the man who tells you it isn't is being kind at your expense. Pinker handed me the other three. This one he walked straight past — and I've decided it's because it was the one with the data behind it, and he respected the data too much to go calling its misuse by a name."

"So here's the misuse, and it's one small step, which is what makes it the bad one. You take *some doors are shut* and you walk it across the room to *the whole house is decided.* You take a thing that's true about a crowd — conscientious kids do better, on average, in the world as it's currently built — and you read it onto the one person standing in front of you, as a sentence already passed. The Dealt Hand says: you got the cards you got, the studies have seen your kind before, they know how this ends. That's the lie. Not that the cards are real. That the cards are the verdict."

"And the cleanest way to see the lie is to be honest about what the number even is, which is the part everyone skips. Heritability is not a measure of how *fixed* a thing is. It's a measure of how much the differences between people, in one setting, ride on the differences in their genes — and change the setting and the number moves under you. Height is about as heritable as anything we've got, and the average man got four inches taller in a century, because the breakfasts changed, not the genes. Eyesight runs hard in families and we beat it with a thirty-dollar pair of glasses. A trait can be heritable to the back teeth and still bend like a reed when you change the room it's standing in. *Dealt* is not the same word as *doomed.*"

She turned the mug again, and here — the narrator caught it, though the room let it go by — she did not stop where the argument was already won. She went back toward the cards.

"Although. The glasses don't fix the eye, they sit in front of it. And the breakfasts only got us up to the height we were always *for* — feed a starved people and they come up to their number, but you don't keep going, you don't get a room of giants, the number was sitting there the whole time waiting to be let out. So I'd go slow before I said the room changes everything. Mostly the room just lets the hand show what it already was. There's a floor and there's a ceiling and you laid neither one."

It was the soft, reasonable, fully motivated sound of a woman talking her own way back to the thing she'd just named a fallacy — and she was the only one in the lab who'd do it, because she was the one it had the deepest hold on. She believed in temperament the way other people believe in weather. She knew it about herself, too, and the narrator would come to think the whole apparatus of fallacies was a fence Marge had built first and mainly to keep herself on the right side of.

Bart, who'd built both, set his green pint down. "There's a cleaner one than glasses, and it's right here." He nodded at the dome. "Same hardware as the fall. Same board, same wheels, same wiring — run a heritability number on it tonight, it comes back identical to what it'd have been in October. And it does a thing this quarter it flat could not do last. I didn't deal it a better hand. The hand's the same hand. The outcome isn't."

"You soldered it in, Bart," Marge said. "That's not the thing climbing out from under its hand. That's you reaching in and moving its hand for it. Show me a person who can reach into his own and I'll give you the point." Which was, the narrator noted, two refusals wearing one coat — *I won't anthropomorphize the vacuum* over *and I'm not ready to give up the cards* — and Marge knew it was two, and let it ride as one.

"That's the four," she said, and reached for the tea gone the temperature of the room. "Four ways to get a mind wrong, one a quarter. That's me." And she sat with it, not seeing — none of them seeing — that the four ways she'd spent the year correcting weren't four things at all, and that the fourth one had a live counterexample humming on the table eighteen inches from her hand, which she had just declined to look at.

## The Cycle

Lisa got up. Not to the camcorder — to the whiteboard, which was how you knew it was the part of the night she'd built toward. She uncapped the marker, drew four boxes in a ring, and did a thing she had not done in front of them before: gave the four parts of the machine their right names out loud.

"A year describing these without naming them, and tonight there are four, and that turns out to matter." She tapped the first box. "Effector. The part that acts — does the thing. At the bottom that's just the protocell spending itself to stay different from the pond. Persistence." Second. "Regulator. Holds a setpoint, corrects back when the world knocks it off. Coordination — the prokaryote getting two kickers to pull as one." Third. "Modeler. Carries a picture of the world and itself, runs off the picture instead of the raw input. The eukaryote's interior, the board that remembers. Interiority." She wrote the fourth name and underlined it once. "And this quarter — Reviser. Changes the picture and the setpoints when they stop paying. Excitability."

She drew the arrows around the ring, each box feeding the next and the last feeding back into the first, and stepped off it.

"That's the loop. And the fourth box is the one that can bite you, so I'll say it once and move on: a part that revises the plan can revise it *wrong.* That's not a bug we'll fix later — it's the cost of having the part at all. The thing Jasmine watched this quarter, where it leaves the doorway it wants to stand in because the gauge says go dock — that's this box, working. Two pulls, and it picks. Some night it picks wrong, leaves when it should've stayed, and nothing'll be broken when it does. That's just the tax on the fourth box." She capped the marker. "But it's in. Every box has a flag now. The board's full."

Bart looked at the loop of arrows feeding and feeding back. "That's a bramble," he said. "That's a thornbush." And it was — four boxes and a snarl of arrows, the way anything that feeds back on itself comes out a snarl — and the name went onto the drawing the way it had gone onto the robot, sideways and for keeps, so the lab now had a drink and a machine and a diagram all called one thing, and would for the rest of the project, and only the narrator thought it worth the second look.

"One more, and it's half-built, so take it that way." She put one palm flat and one vertical and rotated them against each other, which was how she thought. "It's been bugging me that we keep saying *added a layer,* like they stack flat, pancakes. They don't. Each one runs *on* the one under it — the reviser doesn't sit on top of the modeler ignoring it, it reaches down and works the modeler and hands the result up. Signal goes down the stack and back up. It's a relay. A thing carrying a thing forward to the next thing that carries it."

"A relay from what to what?" Jasmine said. "You lost me."

"Okay. Good." Lisa drew a bracket around all four boxes. "These four — protocell, prokaryote, eukaryote, this. I think they're one *kind* of thing. One run at the problem. And I think next year is going to be different from this year in a way that's bigger than how these four differ from each other. Next year we get to the ones with a brain — Bilaterian, then Vertebrate, then Mammal, then Primate. And getting to a brain that runs from a center isn't one more capacity stacked on. It's a phase change. A step-function. The ones with brains are a whole separate line of descent. That's the word I keep grabbing for and can't put down — *line.* A line you could draw, a line with these four on it and not the other eight. This year we built an organism line. Next year we build a brain line. I might be wrong. I wanted it in the room that I don't think next year is more of this. I think it's the next thing."

Burns had his eyes half-closed, the deep-time face. "I think I see it," he said. "Having a brain means having a new way of inheriting."

"Sure," Bart said, "but isn't that just bioelectrics? Eumetazoa's already got bioelectrics. Cells already talk in voltage, already hold a pattern, already revise it — that's the whole thing I was on about earlier. So what's the brain doing that the body wasn't already doing? What would Levin say to a brain *line?*"

"It's more than bioelectrics," Lisa said.

"It's more than bioelectrics." It was Marge, and the room turned, because Marge did not usually back Lisa's framework — she guarded the door it kept trying to walk through. "It's moving past reflexes. Past the hand you were dealt, into something that can get out from under it." She heard herself say it, the narrator thought, a half-second after she'd said it, and looked at the four boxes on the wall like they'd been moved while she wasn't watching.

And there it was, and gone: the genetic line had come full circle that night — four boxes, the loop closed, four slots full, the first complete turn of anything they'd built — and it stood up on the wall in marker, and the two people in the room best equipped to see what they'd built were looking right at it. One of them saw a diagram. The other saw four mistakes people make. Marge had just brushed a hand against the seam between them and pulled it back. Neither of them saw that they were the same four.

## One and Three

Bart had been waiting for the board to close to do his quarterly thing, which was set his green pint down with a click and call the vote. Lisa got there first, holding something she'd been sitting on since the clade block.

"Before you call it. Your guy — the one you just threw at my line. He argues my side of *this* one too."

"Levin."

"Cognition all the way down, every cell a little agent with a goal — that's the gradient. That's *my* yes, the dial that never quite hits zero. The most committed gradient man alive is your hero and you're about to vote no for the fortieth time."

"I love the engineering. The philosophy's a coat he puts on it. I can want the anthrobots in my hands and not think they want anything back. You can hold a wrench you don't pray to."

"That's the least consistent thing you've ever said."

"It's deeply consistent. Hands."

And the four hands went up exactly as they had in the fall, because a fresh box on the wall had moved no one an inch on whether there was anybody inside it. The box this quarter was *nerve,* though, which made one hand worth watching: Burns's whole fall no had run on a single cell having nothing built for feeling at all, and now the thing had wire in it, a net of it through the body — and the hand still didn't cross. Lisa, yes — the dial, a hair off zero, feeling without knowing. Burns, no — nerve at last, he granted, but a net is not a brain; everywhere and no one anywhere, and the line he kept drawing was a center the thing still hadn't grown. Bart, no — no self-model, no one home. Marge, no — no symbol, a very good domino. One and three.

"Same as fall," Bart said, and counted them off with a finger anyway, because the counting was the ritual and he liked the ritual. "One yes, three no, reviser didn't move anybody. On the tape. We'll go again in the spring." And again nobody turned to Jasmine, who had been in more rooms where the question was real than the four of them put together, and who let the asking pass her the way she'd let it pass in the fall, hands quiet, her own counsel kept; and the narrator clocked it again and again said nothing, because it was still hers to keep.

## May we Quicken

Burns took the close. It was still *the motivational speech* on Lisa's agenda — he'd lost that in July and lost it again every quarter since — and he set his beer down on the table, which was how you knew he'd quit chairing the meeting and started meaning something, and came around to the open side.

He wanted to tell them about a jellyfish, he said, and the wanting-to was the run-up she'd heard the shape of before — the warming, the hands lifting off the wood and into the air — so that what the tape kept was the place he landed. There's a jellyfish in the shallows, he said, with no brain at all, not a knot of one, nothing you could point at and call the center of it. And at night it sleeps. It slows, it stops answering, it settles to the bottom; keep it from sleeping and it spends the next day catching up, the way you would. It startles. It comes alive to the world and goes quiet from it again on its own clock, and there is no one in there doing the deciding, because the deciding is spread through the tissue, through the whole net of it.

"There's nobody running it," he said, hands open at the part he couldn't hold. "And it still wakes up. It still answers."

Then the turn — he made it a beat after you could see it coming, the same turn he'd made at the close in spring and summer and fall and would make at every close still ahead: from the animal to the room. What he wanted, he said, what he was grateful for and a little afraid of, was to stay the kind of creature the world could still get an answer out of. To not go quiet from it.

He'd lingered, telling the jellyfish, on the wrong half — not the waking, the part *before,* where it stops answering and lies on the bottom and you'd take it for a stone — and he came back to that half now, in a clause he didn't finish, and let it drop. He didn't say why that was the half he kept reaching for. He may not have known. The narrator marked the place and left it empty, because some things on the tape were his to leave unsaid, and this was the one his voice always went careful around — the one motivation of the four he toasted the way a man knocks on wood.

"So that's the toast, and the toast's the problem, because you can't —" and he stalled, the way he'd stalled on *interiority* in the fall, hunting a verb in a word that wouldn't keep one. "Excitability. You can't *excite* — wrong word, that's a thing you do to a crowd. You can't *be excitable,* that's a personals ad." Marge's low laugh; Bart's single exhale. "There's a verb in here somewhere. Hang on." And they let him hang on, because the hunting was the best part and they'd learned that too.

He found it the way he found things, a half-inch past where he needed to go, leaning that same inch too far over the table: "*Quicken.* To quicken. To come alive to a thing. That's the one." It was a half-step too grand and it was the right word, both at once, the way his words usually were; and only two people had any private claim on it — Burns, who didn't know he'd reached past *responsive* into something with more life in it than the framework strictly needed, and Jasmine, who used the word for a living, for the morning a thing that isn't moving starts to move, and who didn't say so, and wasn't asked.

He picked the beer back up. "May we quicken."

"May we quicken," Lisa said.

"May we quicken," Marge and Jasmine.

"May we quicken, Smithers," Bart said, in the Mr. Burns voice, the plutocrat laid over the father, pitch-perfect, and the room took it the way it always took that voice, and Burns laughed the chest-laugh, early and loud, because it was a good impression and it was eight o'clock and the board had closed clean.

And Mira, who'd been quiet a long while, who was five as of that morning and missing the front tooth that had come out the week before, said it last, around the gap, carefully, the way she said the words she'd decided to keep — "May we quicken" — not in the cartoon voice, in her own, the *qu* coming soft through the space where the tooth had been.

He sat down. Lisa did not reach for the camcorder; the red light stayed on. There was a cake out of frame, and a shape at the end of the table under a drop-cloth that Mira had been pretending all evening not to see, and the night was not going to end on a diagram.

## Under the Cloth

Lisa didn't reach for the camcorder, and now they all knew why. There was a sheet cake behind Jasmine with five candles, and there was the shape at the end of the table under the drop-cloth that Mira had been losing the war against all evening, and Bart got up and slid it into the light.

"Okay," Burns said. "Birthday girl."

Mira came up on her knees on the chair, took a fistful of the cloth, looked back once to be sure it was allowed, got Jasmine's nod, and pulled.

Under it was a body. Not a dome — a body: four legs folded beneath a low chassis, jointed, built to stand and not only to roll, bare metal and open actuators and no skin on it yet, next year's animal with nothing yet running inside. It didn't move. It wasn't meant to, yet. It was the one finished thing anyone in that lab had made with their hands all quarter, and it sat there doing nothing at all, the way a held breath does nothing.

"It has legs," Mira said.

"It has legs," Burns agreed.

"It can come downstairs." She'd wanted that out loud an hour ago, and here was the shape of the wanting, in metal, and she looked at it the way you look at a thing that has heard you.

Nobody told her the body was empty — that the thing she loved was still the dome and would stay the dome till spring, that what sat on the table was a promise and not yet a keeping.

And Burns did the thing the narrator would rewind twice and still not trust she'd read right. He had a hand on the edge of the cloth, half-raised, in the whole posture of the man who unveils things — his table, his lab, his daughter, the giving plainly his to do — and he stopped, and took the hand back, and said, to Mira but pitched for the room, "Your brother built this. Every joint of it. I didn't touch it." Bart, standing with his arms crossed in the way that means a man has decided to need nothing from a moment, said, "Legs aren't hard," to the floor, which was a lie, legs are the hardest thing there is, and the *thank you* he didn't say and the *you're welcome* his father didn't get to give hung over the bare body between them exactly as long as it took Mira to ask where the cake was.

Then the cake, and five candles, and Mira five years old as of that morning, blowing them out through the gap where the tooth had been so that one had to be got twice; and the lab sang the way four scientists and a midwife sing, badly and all the way through; and the dome — the old one, the one that had finally quit dying on the floor — sat by the cake with its display gone dark, retired from nothing, going nowhere, still theirs.

Lisa reached over and shut off the camcorder. The red light went out. Whatever the room did after that it did off the tape, and the narrator could only follow it home.

## Lisa

The red light went out under Lisa's thumb the second Burns finished, the way it did every quarter — she left no frame of the unscripted on the tape — so the body coming out from under the cloth, and the cake, and the five candles got through twice through the gap in Mira's teeth, all happened in a room that was no longer being recorded. She stayed for the song. Then she had the camcorder in its case and the case in her car, because the tape was hers to carry, and she carried it the way she carried everything: at once, and without being asked.

She drove the canyon home in the dark with the heat off and let herself into a townhouse where every object had been chosen once and set down once and never moved again.

The run went into the log before the coat came off — four and a tenth miles, the canyon loop, the time entered to the second in a book that ran back nine years with no gap in it. Then the coat. Then, at a table set for one and set exactly, a bramble in the good glass, built the way she built it at the lab, measured to the quarter-ounce. The townhouse held its order the way Jasmine's house held warmth — completely, and as the answer to a question the room would not say aloud. Nothing in it was being made. No work half-finished on a table, no materials waiting their turn, no project set down mid-stride to come back to — the room had the stillness of a place where things were kept, not made. She had answered something here, with right angles and a logged run and a drink to the quarter-ounce, and the answer worked, and the narrator could give you the whole of the room to the inch and not get an inch closer to the woman who kept it.

On the wall by the entry, where another person would hang a coat, was a small wooden cross, old, the grain gone dark, the one object in the place that did not match the place — carried in from some earlier life, set on the wall, and, having been set, not looked at. People do not look at the thing a whole house has been arranged to keep them from having to look at.

She sat at the table for one and drank the bramble and did not turn on the television. Across the valley the day's tape rode in the back seat of her own car, bound up the canyon to a woman who would watch it twice. The cross stayed on the wall behind her. The run stayed logged. The order held, and what it held in, or held out, the room was not going to say.

## Reflection: What is Roused?

When the cloth came off the body it did not move, and that was the whole truth of the thing, though no one in the room said so: they had unveiled an animal that could not yet do the single thing an animal is for.

What was under the cloth was a body, and a body is the older line's last word. For more than a billion years the long work of the cell had been the work of *staying* — staying apart from the water, staying fed, staying itself across change — and the work had gotten very good. It learned to hold a self off from the pond, to keep a shape, to remember, and at last, this winter, this clade, to be *quick:* to answer the whole world at once and in an instant, a net of nerve laid through all of it and no part of it in charge.

That is as far as staying goes. The animal at the end of that road can do everything except be someone. It hunts and flees, it sleeps and wakes on a clock it sets itself, it learns the small dumb lesson of the leaf-blower — and through the whole of it there is no one inside to whom any of it is happening. It is roused. It is not awake to itself. The lights are on the length of the net and the house is empty.

And here is the small strange fact the cloth uncovered, the one the lab felt and could not have said: the body that ends the one story is the body that begins the next. The line that spent a billion years learning how to stay had, at the very end of it, made the one thing the next line would need before anything else — a body already wired to answer, waiting only for someone to answer *from.* Twice, maybe three times in the whole history of living things, a long labor finishes square on the doorstep of a longer one and hands across the threshold the very thing it was never trying to make.

There is an old word the midwives use for the first stir of motion in a body that is not yet anyone — the flutter a mother feels months before there is a self in there to claim it. They call it *quickening.* It is the oldest name we have for precisely this: the moment a thing comes alive before there is anybody home to know it has. We made a body quick this winter and it lay on the table being quick at no one, and the child whose birthday it was reached up and put her hand on its cold leg and was not afraid — because a five-year-old knows the thing the rest of us work so hard to unlearn, which is that the someone comes. She is not entirely right. Most bodies in the long story stayed bodies, lit and empty and then only dark. But she is right about this one, the way she is right about her own: you build the body first, and rouse it, and wait, and the one who is going to live there comes in out of the cold and finds the house lit and empty and moves in and calls it home.
