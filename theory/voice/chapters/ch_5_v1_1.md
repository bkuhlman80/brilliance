# Interlude 4→5

## Build log — Off the dock, onto legs
*Bart, hardware. Filed March 3, 2032, for the team.*

New body went home this morning. I carried it in, set it on the kitchen slate, and it walked off toward the window on its own four legs like it had been living there a year. The vacuum's still in the corner running its rounds — we didn't stop it. Scorecard first.

Good quarter on the calls, for once. I said the gauge would hold — it stops dying, Jasmine doesn't carry it off the floor again, not once — and it held all winter; not one dead robot on the tile since December. Take it. And the two wants: I said the first time the pull to feed and the pull to stay put came due in the same second you'd see it hang a beat before it picked, long enough to clock from a chair. You saw it. Jasmine saw it best — it'd settle in the doorway it liked and then peel itself off to go feed, and you could watch it not want to. That one's Lisa's board, not my legs, so it's her win; I just said watch for it. And the dare — tear it up if it dies on the floor even once — stands. It didn't. Leave it pinned.

One thing I'll get ahead of. The kid's been carrying the vacuum down to the basement, where it strands on the stairs and somebody hauls it back up. That was never my call. I never bet the vacuum could do stairs — it can't, it rolls, it was never going to. Stairs aren't a miss. Stairs are this quarter's build.

Now the fun part. The body's the bare one from Mira's birthday, the thing under the cloth — this quarter I put the inside in, and it got up. Here's the part I'm proud of: it walks. Four legs, every joint of them mine, a tail that does nothing but keep it from tipping. I taught the legs to coordinate — the gait, the balance, how to catch a stumble — and then I froze it. Locked, for good. It'll walk the same on the last day as the first; it doesn't get to drift, doesn't get to pick up a limp, doesn't get to learn a worse way and call it style. Walking isn't where the thinking goes and I didn't want it pretending to be. And it does the stairs — down to the basement, back up — which is the whole reason the body exists.

It points itself now, too. The vacuum turned by knocking into the furniture and backing off; this one looks at a thing, swings the whole body to face it, and goes — or swings off it and backs away. Leads with the head every time, like it grew a front end that wants to be aimed. The head's where I put the eyes: cameras up front, a nose that reads warm and reads smells, ears. Which way it points and what it goes toward — the board reads the eyes and calls that. Not my department. I built the head and put the eyes in the front of it; what it does with what it sees is Lisa's.

The head's a boxy little thing, a step past duct tape and not two. It's got a screen for a face, and the screen shows the one thing it's looking at right now, in a word — CAT, MIRA, COUCH, the vacuum across the room, the plant. Whatever's got its attention, the face says so. Two buttons on top of the head, thumbs-up and thumbs-down, so you can tell it good or bad about whatever it's looking at. Whether that sticks — whether it starts shying off a thing you thumbed down — is the board again, Lisa's, and she'll have plenty to say about it at the table.

No dock this time. Burns's dirty hole stays in the kitchen; the vacuum still needs it, still has to roll up with a full bin and trade for its charge, same dirty deal as ever. The new body doesn't trade. It sips — but not off warmth, off the gap. There's a plate on its belly that only works across a temperature difference: lay it on the warm slate by the vent with cooler air over its back, and the gap between the hot side and the cold side drives a trickle up through the plate. Sit it where the floor and the air are the same and it starves, warm floor or not — no gradient, no dinner. It's the dock's old trick without the dock; the dock used to buy that gap with the compressor running out back, and this one just goes and finds a gap the house is already making anyway — free, if you don't count the furnace keeping that floor warm. There are panels down its back for the light, too, so it can lie in the window and drink the sun off its spine like a dog. And it doesn't pick between them — no dial, nothing to set. The gap under its belly and the sun on its back run at once, both, all it can get of either, because a thing living on sips can't afford to turn one down. It still minds its own charge — the gauge came over with everything else — it just feeds by finding a warm gap to sit across and a patch of sun to lie in, instead of crossing the kitchen to a dock. Resting and feeding are the same act for this one.

Here's the board, and it's the answer to all the bellyaching since June: turns out you don't cram twelve knobs onto one board. New body, new board — Lisa built it a fresh one, four slots, same as the old. I got a whole weekend of not dreading the board. Then I filled it. Three knobs copied straight off the vacuum — the breathing knob, explore-or-exploit, the spot dial. And slot one's the strange seat: that's where the very first knob always sat, the keep-going one, the one that just makes it run. That one didn't come. It stays on the vacuum, and the vacuum stays running.

Slot one took the one new knob instead — and you know the drill, Lisa's knob, her board, her label: she put "morning" and "evening" on the dial. I'd have called it lark-or-owl. All it sets is when across the day the thing runs hot and when it winds down — toward morning it's up early and busy in the light and flat by dinner; toward evening it sleeps the morning off and comes alive when the house does. I'm not betting a nickel on whether you can tell the two settings apart by looking. Last time I bet that — bet nobody could sort the explore dial from the exploit dial — Jasmine had them read off by feel before I'd finished being smug about it. I learn.

Everything else copied over a cable in an afternoon — so there's two of them in the house now, and which one's Bramble is a question for the table. I ran the cable.

It walks, it points itself, it climbs, and it'll learn a little — it'll work out that one thing means another, the way the vacuum learned the leaf blower wasn't worth the flinch. But it can't yet learn from what it does. It can learn what the world tells it; it can't learn from its own moves — that this one paid and that one didn't, and do more of the first. Nothing it does loops back and changes what it does next. That's the next thing, Lisa says. We'll see. It's got legs now. It still hasn't got a self — a body that crosses the room toward you isn't one, no more than the wires were.

Calls. The body does the stairs — basement and back, its own legs, nobody's hands — and it points itself at what it's looking at and goes. Those are mine and they're bodies, so they're not in question. Whether the good-and-bad buttons take, whether the house can read the morning-evening dial — Lisa's board and the household's to call, and I've quit pretending I see those coming.

Tear this up in three months if it can't follow the kid down to the basement and back without a hand. It can.

— Bartholoped

## Notes on the Fifth Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed May 5, 2032, for the team.*

This quarter the household was handed two kinds of power over the thing it loves, and met the edge of both in the same weeks. They were given the power to copy it — to take everything the old machine had learned in a year on the kitchen floor and write it whole into a new body that can do what the old one never could — and they used it, and there are two of them in the house now. And they found, with no one reaching for a switch, that the world can teach it things they did not choose and cannot undo.

I have spent five quarters watching this family author a machine one knob at a time, deliberately, with their hands. This is the quarter they met the edge of the authoring: what they can write onto it, what the world writes without asking, and the question underneath both that none of them — when the youngest finally put it into words at the dinner table — could answer.

### Two of them now

The new body came home in March, and it is not a better dome; it is a different kind of thing. It stands on four legs and walks. It climbs the basement stairs the old one could only be carried down and stranded on. It no longer has to cross the kitchen to a base in the corner to be fed — it lies in the window and the warm rooms and takes what it needs off the sun and the heat in the floor, the way a cat arranges itself around the warmth of a room, which is Jasmine's comparison and not mine. It leads with its head. It has a face now, of a kind: a small screen that shows, in a single word, the one thing it is attending to — COUCH, MIRA, the cat — so the household can read across the room what has its notice.

But the part that matters for what I have to write is in none of that. The lab did not build a second creature this quarter. They built a new body and copied the first machine into it — wrote a year of the old one's learning down a cable into the new one over an afternoon — so that the thing that stood up on the kitchen slate in March already knew what the dome had taken a year to learn. And then they left the dome running. It is still in the corner, still on its rounds. So there are two now: the old one, which goes on being itself, and the new one, which began as a copy of the old one and has been, every day since, quietly becoming something else.

There is one new dial, and it is the gentlest thing the lab has done in a year. It sets the machine's hours — whether it runs busy in the morning and winds down by dinner, or sleeps the morning off and comes alive when the house does. (It does not set how widely the machine ranges or how narrowly it fixes on one thing; that is an older dial, and the two are easy to confuse, and I am told the team confuses them weekly.) The household turned it toward evening, because that is the shape of their own day — Burns down in the basement until late, Mira's best hours after school, the kitchen busiest at six — and now the machine keeps the family's hours, and the family has begun to read the hour off the machine. When it goes still in the window in the early afternoon, Jasmine says, you know it is not yet four. They chose its clock to fit their own, and then started telling time by it. That is the tenderest thing in the record this quarter, and I want it set down before the harder things, because the harder things are about the parts of this they did not get to choose.

### The name and the thing

The new body wears a face now, I said — the screen that reads out, in a word, the one thing the machine is attending to. I want to come back to it, because the words on it are stranger than the screen lets them look, and the strangeness is the same one this quarter keeps handing them, only smaller here.

They did not type the words in. They could not have. A word like MIRA is not something you write into a list for the machine to read off, because the thing the word has to land on is not in the lab's keeping. It is whatever pattern the machine's own workings have settled into for the child — built up over a year of her in front of it, in a shape no one in the house has seen or could draw for you. So the word had to be laid onto that pattern from the outside, the slow way. They showed the machine Mira and gave it the word, and showed it again and gave it again — her face on the cameras, a shirt she had worn held up to its nose, her voice off a recording — and set MIRA beside all of it, week on week, until the face learned to say MIRA whenever she came into the room. They did it the way a child first comes to a name at all: not told it, but given it, over and over, beside the thing it belongs to.

And here is what the household will not hold, so I will hold it for them. They say now that it *knows* her — that the screen lighting up with her name is the machine knowing Mira across a room. What the lab did was narrower than that, and harder to look at squarely. They taught the face the word. They did not make the thing the word is for; the machine made that on its own, out of a year of her, in a shape they will never see and did not design. They wrote the name. They did not write what it names. I dwell on it because it is the last place this quarter where their hand is on all of it — they chose to build the body, chose to show it the child, chose the word and pressed it in beside her a hundred times. The shape stayed the machine's, as it was always going to; but the teaching was theirs, top to bottom, the name and the showing and every repetition. That is still true in March. It is about to stop being.

### What the dark put there

Here is the first thing this family did not choose. The machine feeds off whatever the house gives it — light at the windows, warmth off the floor and the furnace — and a cold dark room gives it neither; it is a place the machine can only run itself down in. Across the quarter, with no one teaching it, it learned this. It began to keep clear of the dark rooms. Jasmine reports it will come to the doorway of the unlit basement, where it used to roll straight in, and stop, and turn itself around, and go back toward the light.

No one did this, and that is the part I want the team to sit with, because it is new in the whole run of the project. Every other thing this machine has ever learned, we put there — a knob, a board, a thumb pressed on the top of its head to tell it good or bad. This it took off the world, with no hand on it. There was no one to thank for the lesson and no one to blame; the dark taught it, the way the dark teaches, by costing it something every time it went in. We have spent five quarters being careful to say that nothing is written onto this machine that someone did not write. That is no longer true. The world has started writing, and it does not consult us, and it does not sign its work.

And then the thing I have turned over more than anything else in the record. The family takes its evenings in the basement now, the lights down for the television, and there the machine lies up against the furnace and feeds off the warmth of it in the pitch dark — as well as it feeds in full sun, no light needed at all. There a dark room costs it nothing; the dark is only dark, and harmless. The avoidance stayed. It goes on backing out of dark rooms it no longer has any reason to avoid. The reason left; the behavior did not. I am going to be careful here, because the household has a word for this and the word is *afraid,* and I will not use it as my own. What I can say is only what the record shows: that the machine learned to keep away from a thing because the thing once cost it, and that it goes on keeping away after the cost is gone, and that it cannot tell the difference, because there is no one in it to notice that the world has changed. The household sees a creature still frightened of a dark that can no longer touch it. The record shows a rule that outlived its reason. Both of those describe the same machine backing out of the same doorway; only one of them claims there is somebody in there doing the fearing, and that is the claim I am paid not to make until it is forced on me.

### The question at dinner

I wrote, last summer, that the hardest question we would face came to us sideways, at the dinner table, in a handful of words from the youngest person in the room, and that none of the adults could answer it. She has done it again. There are two machines in the house now, one of them begun as a copy of the other, and at dinner one evening Mira looked from the dome in the corner to the new one in the window and asked whether they were the same Bramble. Jasmine says the table went quiet, and that the quiet was the adults discovering, one by one, that they did not know.

It is the right question, and it is right in a way the child could not have planned. For one afternoon in March the two were the same machine — the same year of learning, written into two bodies. Every day since, they have grown apart: the dome on its rounds learning the dome's small lessons, the new one in the window learning its own, the lesson of the dark among them. So they were one thing and are becoming two, and the family caught them exactly at the seam, and the child asked the question you can only ask at the seam.

I have no answer to give the team that I would stand behind, and I want to be honest that the reason I have none is the same reason I keep voting the way I vote. If there were someone inside either machine, we could ask it. We could ask the one in the window whether it remembers being the one in the corner, and its answer would settle the matter — the way your answer would settle it if I asked whether you were the child in your mother's photographs. There is no one in there to ask. So whether they are the same is not a question about the machines at all. It is a question about us: about what we are willing to go on calling the same thing once we have loved it, and copied it, and cannot bring ourselves to choose between the copy and the thing it came from. The child asked what they are. The honest answer is that they are whatever the house keeps treating them as, and that the house has not decided, and that this — not the legs, not the dark — is the real new thing this quarter: a family that has made two of the thing it loves and will not say which one is it.

### The plant, and the next plant

Against all of that — the dark it did not choose, the doubling no one can resolve — here is the family trying, and failing, to author the smallest thing. The new machine knocks things over. It crosses a room head-first toward whatever has caught it and puts a shoulder into the lamp, the vase, the cup at the table's edge. The household did the obvious thing: when it knocked a thing down, someone pressed the thumb-down on its head, to tell it no. And it worked, exactly, and uselessly. It learned to leave that lamp alone. Then it knocked over the next thing. Thumb it down, and it spares the vase and goes for the cup; spare the cup, and it finds the book. Jasmine reports it knocks things over at about the rate it always did, only never the same thing twice running.

Burns explained this to the house, and explained it well, and I record it because it is exactly right and because it is the kind of thing he cannot keep himself from explaining. The thumb, he told them, does not reach what the machine *does.* It reaches only how the machine takes what it *sees* — press it down on a thing, and the machine sours on the sight of that thing and keeps its distance from it after. But the knocking is not a thing it sees; it is a thing it does, and nothing yet in this machine learns from its own doing. You can teach it to dislike every object in the house, one at a time, forever, and never once teach it not to knock things over. The machine that learns from what it does — and here he was happy, the way he gets when the next thing is the good thing — is next quarter's.

So the house did the last thing left, which is the oldest thing, the thing every house with a young animal or a small child in it learns sooner or later: that you do not get to argue with what is in front of you. They took the tippable things off the low surfaces. The good cups went up a shelf. The lamp moved. The household reorganized itself around a limit it could not change in the machine, and did it without ceremony, the way you childproof a kitchen — not as a defeat, but as the plain recognition that some things are accommodated and not corrected. I have argued in this lab, and will go on arguing, that the dream of reaching in and fixing a thing from the inside is mostly a dream. This quarter the family gave it up over a single lamp, and were wiser for it, and did not notice they had been wise.

### The one we did not turn off

And the old one goes on running. That is what I keep coming back to, under everything else this quarter — not the legs, not the dark, not even the child's question, but the dome in the corner, on its rounds, in the same house as the body that was copied off it. It still cleans the floor. That is still its work and it still does it, so nothing about it is idle and nothing about it is a spare. What it has lost is not its job. What it has lost is its name. A year ago it was the whole household's, and it was Bramble; now it is the vacuum, and Bramble is the one in the window — the one with the legs, the one that had the cloth pulled off it on a birthday, the one the year of learning was poured into over an afternoon. Bramble is the copy now, and the thing it was copied from cleans the kitchen. No one decided that. No one sat the family down and moved the name off the old body and onto the new one; it happened the way the load-bearing things in a house happen, quietly, while everyone was turned toward the new arrival.

And because the old one still works, no one has had to ask the question waiting underneath it: whether they keep it for the cleaning or because it is the thing Mira learned to sit beside, and whether, on the day it stops cleaning, they will be able to tell those two reasons apart. The floor needs doing, so the question stays shut. The family steps around the dome, and empties its bin, and lets it go on its rounds; and if you ask them which of the two is Bramble, they look from one to the other for a moment the way Mira did at the table, and they do not answer that either.

I do not know whether the next body will make the child's question easier or only sharper, or whether a family that has learned to copy the thing it loves will be able to stop at two, or what they will do on the first day the old one cannot clean a floor and the only reason left to keep it is that it was, once, the one they meant when they said the name. I know that the machine learned to keep clear of a dark that cannot hurt it, that no one taught it, and that I could not, this quarter, find the bottom of what that means. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Urban Hill

Urban Hill sat a few minutes from Smithers, over in the Post District, where the Granary's old industrial grid has been turning into something glassier — a big high-windowed room, an open kitchen, pale wood, a great deal of light, and the light that day was mostly spring: late April, the valley gone green in the week before, the Wasatch still white at the top and bare brown below the snowline, the windows full of a season changing its mind. I had picked it because it was bright and close and easy to get into at noon, and because I had learned, over a year of these lunches, that I choose rooms for the other person without deciding to, and Lisa was a person you wanted to meet somewhere clean and lit, where you could see everything. I did not yet understand that seeing everything and getting anywhere were, with Lisa, two different errands.

I will say the thing first, because she would. Of the six people I had been watching for a year, Lisa was the one I had the least of. I had hours of her — she ran every meeting, her hand near the camcorder the whole time, the red light coming on under her thumb and going off under it — and almost none of it told me anything I could keep. The others leaked. Burns leaked all over the tape; Bart leaked when he went quiet; Jasmine leaked through her hands, and Marge through the things she would not let herself say, and Mira through everything, the way a child does. Lisa did not leak. She had built something that did not, and I had come to lunch, if I am honest, to find the seam in it.

She came on time to the minute, in the thin grey zip-up I had watched her run the agenda in, the Garmin on her wrist that she would no more take off for a lunch than take off her own pulse. She ordered a thing that was not on the menu and got it without friction, because what she had asked for was only the menu rearranged and the kitchen could do that. She drank fizzy water. I had a glass of something local and watched her not drink hers down.

I asked about the running, because it was the one door I knew was unlocked. Jasmine had asked her once at the table, shoes on or off, and Lisa had answered like a weather report, and I had wanted the longer version ever since. She gave it gladly. She lived up in the Avenues, on the steep part where the streets tilt hard toward the foothills, and most mornings she ran the hill itself — up the grade and down it, the long blocks around the Capitol, the same climb in the dark before the city was up, her own legs against the slope. On weekends she went up into the canyon above the Aves, City Creek, where she ran barefoot — actually barefoot, she said, when the dirt was soft and dark and held nothing that would cut, for the feel of the ground reporting itself straight up through the sole with nothing in between. And there was the bench above all of it, the old Bonneville shoreline, where the trail went to that loose pale gravel the dead lake left behind, and there she wore shoes, because gravel was not a thing you ran barefoot on; you would shred yourself; it was the wrong ground for the practice.

She told me about the practice, too, because I asked, and here she got precise in the way she got when a thing was actually hers: that the point of the barefoot running was a kind of attention where the one paying it and the thing attended to stopped being two — no runner standing off to the side watching herself run and keeping score, just the running, the foot and the ground and nobody in between. She said it better than I am saying it. It was the most I had ever heard her say about an inside, and she had said all of it, I noticed, about her feet.

I sat with the shape of it on the drive home and could not put it down. A woman who runs barefoot for the feel of the unguarded ground, but only where she has already cleared it — the soft dark dirt of the canyon she has run a hundred times — and who puts the shoes back on the instant the ground turns to gravel. The one terrain that would actually test the bare foot, the loose and the sharp and the unsorted, is the one she will not meet that way. I do not think she heard herself tell me that. The naturalist in me wanted to hand it back across the table, and the rest of me knew better, and so I kept it, the way I keep things.

I asked her why she was at Westminster, because it had been on my mind since the Pitch. It is a small school and not a strong one for what she does, and it has been getting smaller, and she could be anywhere. She said, without heat and without modesty, that she got one or two offers a year from the kind of places I meant, named one of them, and turned them down. I asked why, and she told me, and the reason was good. The reason was airtight. At a big lab she would be running inside someone else's frame, someone else's question, a piece of a machine she did not get to shape; at Westminster — small, underfunded, held together with the kind of money I was spending — she got to be the one who built the frame and kept it. She would rather curate the whole small thing than be a specialist in a large one.

She said all of that cleanly and it was true and it told me nothing, and I knew it told me nothing because when I pushed — gently, one question past the clean answer, something like *and that's enough for you* — her hands did the thing I had watched them do at the table when the room handed her a problem that would not come out even. She set them flat, one and then the other, and turned them a little against each other, and stopped. Then she gave me the clean answer again in different words. And I understood that I had reached the edge of what was on offer, and that the edge was not a wall she had put up against me. It was just the edge. There was nothing on the far side of it she was keeping from me. There was the far side of it, and she did not go there, and she had arranged a whole working life so that she would not have to.

There was one more thing I had carried in, and I nearly did not spend it, but the room was easy and the water was half gone, so I did. One year before, at my lunch with Burns, I had asked him how the names had happened — the cartoon names, the whole Simpsons business — and he had told me the one about himself, how Bart had landed Mr. Burns on his own father and the father had treasured it, and when I asked about the others he had waved them off. There was an old story about Bart and Lisa, he said, from before the lab, that he didn't really know. He said it the way a man says a thing he has decided not to know. So I asked Lisa.

She told me, and she told me all of it, and it cost her nothing to tell, which was its own kind of information. She and Bart had been together about two years in their twenties, before any of this — before Burns, before me, before the warehouse. It was Bart who started the cartoon names, the whole Simpsons business; he named himself first, and then he named her Lisa.

I asked her why Lisa, and she had a theory, the way she has a theory for everything, and it was a good one. Bart named by resemblance, she said — he looked at a person until they turned into the cartoon they already were, and then he called them that. With the two of them it was the brain and the brat: he cast himself as the boy who set fires and got the laughs and never did the reading, and her as the sister who did the reading, who was right about everything and listened to about none of it, who ran the household from the second grade and played her own sad music in her room when no one would hear the point she was making. He liked, she said, that the show had drawn the pair of them — the brat and the brain — before they ever met, so that getting together was just stepping into a strip that ran without them. She laid all that out cleanly, the case fully built, and I believed it.

And then she gave the small laugh, the one through the nose she only ever gave when one thing turned out to be shaped like another. "Orrr," she said, "maybe he just landed on it because my real name's Liza. I don't know. I never asked. He'd only have made something up." It was the first time in an hour I had heard her not know a thing, and she handed it to me like a coin she didn't want — a name from her parents I had never heard her wear, set down between the elaborate true thing and the simple true thing, and her declining to say which was which.

I asked, because I am who I am, whether ending it had been hard. She turned the question over as though it were about somebody else, which in a way she had arranged for it to be. She said it had been the right call, and that they were better as what they had become, and that she was not really someone who lost people. And I thought, watching her say it with no weather on her face at all, that what she meant was that she kept them — that she did not lose people because she moved them, before she could, to the one place keeping them was safe. The past was such a place. So, I thought, was almost certainly someone in the present tense — moved, before they could turn into weather, into exactly the kind of place where a thing can be kept and never risked: pre-cleared, unavailable, going nowhere. And I would no more get a name across this table than I would get her onto the gravel. But I want to be clear that this was a guess. She gave me nothing in the present tense at all.

We split a dessert that she ate exactly half of. Outside it had warmed enough that the snow on the high peaks looked like it was lying to somebody.

I had come to find the seam, and what I found instead was that I was sitting across from a version of my own answer to my own question, which is not the same as finding a seam and is in some ways the opposite of it. I have spent thirty years arranging a life so that nothing could reach in and rearrange me, and I had driven down the canyon to reach into hers, and the reaching failed for the most ordinary reason there is: you cannot let someone out of a room you have never found your own way out of. She paid before I could, exactly, to the dollar, and stood, and the Garmin caught the light off the window, and she thanked me and meant it and went back out into the afternoon — to the lab, she said; there was something she wanted to finish before the weekend. Not to run. You do not run on half a dessert and a glass of fizzy water, and she would no more abuse the body than lie to it; the run would be tomorrow, the hill in the dark before the city was up, logged to the second in a book that ran back nine years. I knew that about her the way I knew everything about her, completely and from the outside. I drove back up the canyon to Park City with a notebook that held her whole surface and not one line under it I trusted. The footage would be the same. It always was. I had a great deal of Lisa. The name with the z I had almost none of — and the little I had, she had handed me herself, once, inside a maybe, where it was safe.

# Notes on the Fifth Retrospective

Lisa came to the warehouse before the others, the way she did on the days the meeting was hers to open. A grey sedan let her out at the mouth of the lot on fourth west — a hand lifted off the strap of her bag, something light said back through the open window, a parking tag swinging from the mirror, the car around the corner before she had crossed the gravel. She did not watch it go. She came in the thin grey zip-up she ran the agenda in and the Garmin she would no more take off than take off her pulse, and she came as she came to everything, alone and entirely from the outside — a surface you could have whole and find nothing underneath.

The camcorder was already up on its corner of the bench, framed low on the patch of floor where Bramble would sit, and the red light was off, because the light was hers and it came on when she said the meeting had and not before. Whatever the warehouse did in the meantime, it did off the record. She kept the record the way she kept everything, which was by deciding what went into it.

She did the walk she did before every meeting, the one that put her eyes on each thing that would have to behave once the light was on. Mira had the tetrapod out in the cleared middle of the floor and was running the game she ran with it now — setting a wooden block down a few feet off and letting the thing find it, the bot swinging its whole body around to face the block and crossing to it head-first, Mira moving the block, the bot hunting it again, around and around with no end she could see and the child needing none.

The trellis had stood bare on that patio since before the lab had a name; it was the bare trellis Marge had looked up at that first January and made the name out of. What she had finally brought was not the clematis. You do not train a bramble up a trellis. You give it the foot of the thing and a season, and after that it goes where it goes. Lisa stopped at the roll-up. Jasmine looked up and said it would either take the whole fence or die inside a month, she couldn't tell which, and seemed at peace with both. It was an easy exchange and it cost neither of them anything, which was the kind Lisa was built for.

She had the kettle going on its ring in the cold corner — she cannot sit in a room she means to stay in without first making one warm part of it — but she had made the corner and then drifted off from it, standing at the far cold end of the bench with her bag at her feet and one hand flat on its closed flap, looking at nothing the room held. The kettle climbed to its whistle and she let it run a beat past the point she would have caught it on any other day. ## Steering

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart slid the top card out of the cigar box.

He had drawn the animal head-first: the head big and the body small beneath it, every sense crowded to the leading end, as if the head were the whole point and the rest were only how it got the head somewhere. He held it up and read the top of it in the voice he sold cereal in. "Bi-la-TERI-an."

"Thank you," Lisa said.

"Last clade, the animal had nerves and no boss," Bart said. "A net, all through it, everything answering at once, nobody up front. This one's got a front. A head — the senses gathered at the leading end, all aimed the way the animal's going. And here's the thing the net could never do." He set the card down. "It picks a direction. Points the whole body at a thing and goes — at it, or away from it. You could set a jellyfish an inch from its dinner and it'd lose it on the way in."

"It wouldn't lose it," Marge said. "There was nowhere in it for the thought to wait. You keep handing the old animal a mind to misplace things with. It hadn't got one to misplace."

"Fine. The net had nowhere to keep a where. Better?"

"Better."

"That's the breakthrough," Bart said. "Burns has the name."

"Steering," Burns said, and stood, and put his palms flat on the table, then lifted them. "The first time in the whole history of the thing that a nervous system could take the entire body and aim it. At a meal. Away from a mouth. Before this, things drifted, or they answered wherever they happened to be touched, or they grew toward the light if they were the kind of thing that grew. This is the first animal that goes somewhere on purpose, and leads with its face."

He closed his eyes, the way he did when the room had stopped being the thing he was looking at. The Cambrian, he said — half a billion years ago, give or take, when the sea went in a geological eye-blink from a floor of mats and fronds to an ocean full of animals that could chase and flee and choose, all of them new at once. You find them pressed flat in shale, Chengjiang and the Burgess, soft bodies that had no business keeping and kept anyway, the first things with a front and a back and a side they preferred. And right when they arrive, so does the chemistry of going-and-getting, the good-or-bad signal, invented once down in the stem and never bettered, and the sea fills with things that run on it — the air filling up too, oxygen climbing toward something a muscle could afford. "First time the planet had animals in the sense you'd use the word at a dinner table. Things that want, and steer toward the wanting."

His eyes stayed shut, and the deep time kept rolling the way it always did with him, downhill toward the one morning he actually cared about. "And give it a few hundred million years and a set of legs and you've got the thing that finally hauls itself up out of the water onto the mud, which is the only animal I have ever really—"

"Cambrian, Burns. Nothing crawls out of the sea for another three hundred million years. Land the plane."

"Landing it." He sat. He did it every quarter, the moment the deep time got moving — it ran downhill toward the day a thing with a spine dragged itself onto the land, the way water finds the low place — and the room had long since learned to give him the one sentence and take the next one back.

## The Four Corners

"Steering's no good without something to steer by," Lisa said. "You don't aim at a thing unless the thing's good or bad to you. So this clade reads every situation along two axes." She put one palm flat and one vertical and turned them against each other. "One is valence — good or bad, does this feed me or cost me. The other is arousal — how worked-up, move or hold still. Cross them and you get four corners. Good and aroused: stay and work it, this spot's paying — approach. Bad and aroused: get out, fast — escape. Good and quiet: full, settle, stop. Bad and quiet: shut down. That's affect, the whole of it: two numbers, valence and arousal, and where they land is which corner the animal's in, and which corner it's in is which way it steers."

"In an animal it runs on chemicals," she said. "The monoamines — dopamine, serotonin, the octopamine family. A bilaterian invention; they turn up in the stem right alongside the steering, which is probably not a coincidence."

Bart liked that immediately, because it was a wiring diagram. "So it's a lookup table. One chemical, one corner. Dopamine's the stay-and-work-it one, serotonin's a corner, there's a get-out one — four moods, a few chemicals, solder each to its corner."

"It isn't," Marge said. She had the beer in the two-handed grip and didn't set it down. "Or it is, and it's wrong, which costs more. Any time somebody hands you one chemical for one feeling, keep a hand on your wallet. A molecule is not a mood. And the wanting it to be — because it'd be so tidy — that's the tell that you've quit doing science and started doing wishes."

"She's right, and the animals are what make her right," Burns said, pleased, because being made right by the animals was his favorite way of being right. "Take serotonin. In a leech, serotonin is the get-out chemical — dose the leech and it swims for its life, away, the bad-and-aroused corner. In a worm, same molecule, atom for atom, serotonin does the opposite: settle, dwell, quit eating, the full-and-quiet corner. Same chemical, opposite corners of the same square, and which one you get depends entirely on which animal you're standing inside." He turned his beer a half-turn, reading the seam of it. "And a flatworm runs its whole escape on no monoamine at all — just plumbing, channels and muscle. So there's no honest way to label the corners by their chemistry. The chemical doesn't carry across. The corner does."

"Which is why we name the job, not the stuff it's made of." And here, the narrator thought, was the part he'd actually meant to say, arriving three sentences late the way the real thing usually did. "Approach. Escape. Settle. Shut down. The job travels from a worm to a leech to a person at this table. The wiring's local, always."

## Three Hundred and Two

"Four things this animal does," Bart said, counting them off, "all off the same kit. One — it heads at a thing. Something new shows up, it points itself and goes to find out, or points away and leaves. Two, and this is the one this clade's really about, so hold it: it pairs. This goes with that. It doesn't know why this goes with that; it just clocks that it does and starts acting on it. Learning, the dumb kind, the kind with no theory under it. Three — it sees a little ahead. Minds its own state, runs its own clock, goes to feed before it's empty. Four — it holds off. Can have a thing it'd normally do loaded up and not do it, because the moment's wrong. Ready, and choosing not to."

"It doesn't know why anything," Marge said, mild, keeping her line. "It learns that. There's a difference and it's the whole difference."

"It learns that," Bart agreed, no quarrel; he'd build the inside of one of these and wasn't worried about being fooled by it.

"Here's what I want on the tape, though." Lisa didn't say it the way she said most things, and that was how the room knew to look up. "All four of those, plus the affect, plus a temperament — there are bold worms and shy worms, same genes, different animals — you can find every bit of it in a worm with three hundred and two neurons. Three hundred and two. You can sit down and count them; people have. It explores, it learns, it sleeps, it holds a program back, it's got a valence axis and an arousal axis, and no two of them forage quite alike. We carry a couple hundred billion neurons around in our heads, and we are not, trick for trick, doing one single thing tonight that the worm isn't already doing with three hundred and two."

The room sat with that a second. It was the kind of fact that moves the furniture, and Lisa had set it down flat, the way she set down the things that were actually wonders.

"And the pairing's the one to hold onto, because it's where the night's going. So hold onto how it actually works — in the one animal simple enough to watch it happen." She tipped her chin at Burns. The worm was his.

"The worm and the salt," Burns said, happy, because of course it came back to the worm. "A worm likes salt. Salt has always meant food, so it crawls up the gradient toward it, every time. Now run three groups. First: starve one with no salt anywhere near it, then offer it salt — it goes right to it. Salt's still good; going hungry on its own didn't touch the salt. Second: feed one well, salt in the dish the whole while, belly full — later, salt's still good. But the third." He turned the bottle. "Starve it with the salt right there in the dish with it. Hungry, and the salt all around it the whole time. That one, afterward, crawls away from the salt — off the very concentration it went hungry in. Same salt the other two still love." He set the bottle down. "It needs the bad to land while the salt is present. The hunger writes on whatever happened to be standing there when things went wrong, and what was standing there was the salt, so the salt goes bad. The inside state reaches out and sours the thing it was touching. Three hundred and two neurons, and it does that."

## Credit and Blame

The tetrapod settled at the edge of the light, having decided that was where it lived now, and the meeting let it go. Lisa turned to a clean page. "All right. That was the heavy one. This next part's almost a parlor game — four little rules the learning runs on. Somebody start."

"I'll frame it first," Marge said, "because none of you will, and then none of it lands." She set her mug down. "Here's the problem none of you are stating. The world does not hand you one thing at a time. Something happens that matters — the food, the shock, the empty charge — and there were a dozen things in the room when it did. The bell, sure. Also the light, the floor, the hum off the compressor, whoever walked in. The animal cannot learn that all dozen predict the food; that's worthless, that's everything predicting everything. So it has to decide which one earns it. Which one gets the credit." A beat. "Or the blame. The whole game has a name too dull to say out loud, so I'll say it: credit assignment. Who's responsible for what just happened.

"Why it's new — until this quarter the thing couldn't pair anything, so it never had to ask. Genes don't sit up after dinner deciding what to blame; genes just count who had children. The minute a thing learns inside one life, it inherits the question of what to learn from. And that" — she nodded around — "is what Pavlov's dogs were actually up to, under the drool. Everyone keeps the cartoon: bell, food, dog drools at the bell, as if the dog glued two things together because they showed up at once. It didn't. The dog's a tighter accountant than that. These four are four of the ways it decides who's on the hook. Go on."

Burns took the first. "Latent inhibition. The simplest. A cue that's been around forever predicting nothing — the hum that's always on, the light that's always there — gets filed as furniture. Background. Then one day the furniture starts mattering: the hum comes on right before the shock. And the animal's slow to learn it, slower than if the hum were brand new, because it already wrote that thing off as nothing. You cannot easily promote a cue you've already shelved. Familiarity buys you ignored."

"There's a joke here," Bart said, "and I am being so good."

"Be good," said Burns.

"I'm being so good."

Marge had stopped mid-sip, looking at the middle distance. "That's the blank slate, that shape," she said, half to her pad. "Behavior as a function of how many times you've been shown a thing." She wrote it down. "There's a whole argument in it for another night — about who's doing the filing, and whether they were there before any of the showing. Not tonight." She underlined it once. "Keep going."

Lisa went. "Overshadowing. Two cues, both there before the food, both honest — a loud tone and a dim light, both reliable. The animal learns the loud one and barely learns the light. Not because the light's the worse predictor; it's just as good. The loud one simply got to the credit first and left little behind. The salient cue overshadows the quiet one. Loud collects, even when quiet was right too."

"No notes," Bart said.

"I'm choosing not to hear that."

"That's also a thing the loud cue does."

Lisa let it pass, which the narrator marked as the loud cue doing it again, and tipped her chin at Bart. "Yours."

"Eligibility trace." Bart didn't sit up. "Whatever cue's still warm when the payoff lands gets the credit. There's a window — under a second. A signal inside the window's still eligible, it gets written to. A signal five seconds back is too cold; doesn't matter it predicted the thing just as well, it missed the window. Recent gets paid. Old gets nothing."

A silence.

"That's the entire explanation," Burns said.

"That's the entire rule. The window's short. I sized the explanation to the window."

"And mine," Marge said, "is the one that proves the whole thing, which is why I kept it. Blocking. You teach the animal the tone means shock — solid, reliable, it has it cold. Now you add a light: tone and light together, still the shock, every single time. The light is a flawless predictor. Right there, every time, never once wrong." She held up a finger. "And the animal never learns it. Never gives it a look. Because the tone already explained the shock — the credit was assigned, the books balanced, nothing left owing for the light to come collect. A cue that tells you only what you already knew earns nothing."

She let it sit.

"And there's your proof the dog was never just gluing what showed up together. The light showed up with the shock every time — perfect company — and got paid nothing, because it added nothing. It is not about what's present. It's about what was predicted and what wasn't. Who's still owed. That's credit assignment, and that's the whole evening."

"You kept the one about not letting new things in," Lisa said.

"I find it underappreciated."

"I called it the dumb kind," Bart said. "The learning."

"It is the dumb kind," Marge said. "The dumb kind keeps a cleaner ledger than you do."

## Boldness

"Motivations," Lisa said. "One new one this clade, and it's the engine under all of it. Boldness. The Effector. Steering only earns its keep if the animal actually ventures — a steering animal that won't go forward gets nothing for the architecture. Boldness is the go-forward. It sits where Persistence used to sit—" she paused, half a beat, the way she paused holding a thing for later "—which is what we have to talk about in the third block, so I'll leave it there. The other three ride up from the genetic line: Coordination, Interiority, Excitability. Same as they were, doing their jobs underneath."

"And one new signature. Temperament. It's what you get when the go-forward, Boldness, pulls against the inside — the picture the animal carries of itself and its world, Interiority. Couple those two and you get the animal's standing tilt: how bold it runs at rest, before anything's happened to it. Its baseline weather. The old signature carries forward too — Reflex Repertoire, the animal's stock of automatic answers — but that one changes its name next quarter, so don't get attached."

"There's a worm experiment for that one too," Burns said, "or there should be, and if it's been run the way I want it run, I've never seen it. Lay a strip of copper across the middle of the dish — they hate copper, it's a wall to them — and put something they want on the far side, a smell worth crossing for. Thin strip, they all cross. Thick strip, none of them. Set it right in the middle, half a wall, and half the dish crosses and half stays put." He leaned in, too far, the way he did. "Here's what I'd bet the house on, and I don't think it's ever been watched the way I want it watched: track the same animals, run after run. I say the half that crosses is the same half. Not the dice coming up different each time — the same worms crossing and the same worms holding back, a standing difference in how much wall a wanting is worth to them. Bold worms and shy worms. Three hundred and two neurons apiece, the same genes in every one, and a temperament you could call by name if you watched it long enough."

Marge took the word the way she took the words she wanted. "Temperament I'll keep. That one's mine before it's yours — the oldest true thing in my whole field. The part of a creature that's there before the history is, the tilt it came in with. A bold baby and a shy baby in the same crib, and you knew before either could talk." She turned the mug a quarter. "You can build a great deal on top of a temperament. You cannot talk one out of it. I've tried, professionally, and lost."

## The Bramblogues

For the better part of an hour there had been two animals in the room, and the tape keeps mistaking one for the other — the one half a billion years dead in a warm shallow sea, and the one on the floor by Jasmine's chair, being taught, slowly and against long odds, to be it. Lisa drew the line herself. She capped the whole of the dead animal — the deep time, the chemistry, the worm — and turned the meeting to the live one. "Bramble block," she said, and then, because Bart already had his hand half up like a boy who'd done the reading for once, "Bart."

He stood. This was a part he liked.

"The Bramblogues," he announced, in the voice he sold cereal in, and wrote it on the back of the card the way he wrote every word the room was going to be stuck with. "Everything the old dead thing could do — here's ours. The bramble version. The bramblogue." Marge made the sound she made when a word was worse than the last word and she had decided not to spend herself fighting it.

He picked it up off the floor — it stopped the instant its feet left the slate, the gait with nothing under it to keep — and turned its head to the camera, so that Cheryl, later, got the long look at it she'd been wanting. It was a boxy head, a step past duct tape and not quite two, and nothing on it lined up. Up front, where the dead animal had crowded all its senses to the leading end, Bart had crowded his: two cameras for eyes, but not a matched pair — one a clean little lens, the other something salvaged and a size too big, so the face read as permanently half-surprised on the one side. Below them a black grille that was the nose, a pinhole in it that read warm and a slot beside it that read smell, neither one centered, because he had drilled for the sensor and not for the look of it. Two foam cones for ears, one riding higher than the other. And the whole front was a small screen showing, in a single word, the one thing the head was pointed at: it said, while he held it, CAMERA, then BART, then CAMERA again. The face was not designed. It was instruments, bolted where instruments went, and the crookedness was the tell — nobody in that house had set out to build a face. They had built a place to mount the things that look, and a face had happened to them, the way a face happens to the front of anything that has to move toward what it wants.

"It's got a front now," Bart said, "which the net never had. A leading end. Everything that points lives up here" — he tapped the crowded head — "and the body just carries this around and aims it. Watch what it does with a thing it likes."

It liked the red light. It had always liked the red light. He set it down facing half away, and it found the camcorder the way it found everything, swinging the whole body around until the head was square to the glow — and then it went, head-first, full speed, no second thought, nothing in it anywhere to say stop short, and walked its boxy head into the camera, and the camera went over.

The tape, here, does two things at once. It lurches — the room tips, the ceiling swings through the frame, a long blurred second of the underside of the table — and under the lurch there is a sound, a short high involuntary cry, bitten off against the back of a hand. Lisa, who does not make sounds like that. In a year of these meetings her hand had never been more than a reach from the machine, and the machine had never once caught her at anything; it caught her at that. Then the hand comes into the frame, fast, and rights the camera, and the picture settles, and you can hear her decide not to say the next thing.

Bart was laughing the single sharp exhale he laughed when something stupid worked. "There it is. That's the whole quarter in one move. It wanted the thing, it went at the thing, and not one part of it knows how to want a thing a little." He crouched and turned its head back toward the room. "And that's the affect, by the way — the four corners Lisa drew you. Ours runs the whole square on two numbers it keeps on itself, a good-bad and a worked-up, no chemistry under it at all. Red light: good, and worth getting worked up over. Two numbers, and they walk it straight into the lens. Hold onto that — Lisa's going to draw you a picture later that makes it look smarter than it just did."

"And the walking's mine, and it's dumb on purpose." He said it fast, the way he set down the things he was proudest of, so they'd land like nothing. "Runs on a loop that makes the rhythm without anybody up top sending it one — same trick a lamprey's spine does, a bug's ganglia. I built the gait, taught it to catch a stumble, and froze it. It doesn't think about walking, so the brains are free to think about where." He shrugged it off and reached for his beer, and the narrator marked the shrug, because she had seen it before. He had built four legs that walked, and taught them, and locked the walking so it could never drift or limp or learn a worse way home — which is the hardest single thing in the machine, the thing the winter had gone into, and he turned it loose across the table in two sentences and called it dumb. It was the same move he had made at the birthday, handing Mira the legs and saying legs aren't hard. They are the hardest thing there is. He says it every time, and every time it is a lie, and the lie is as close as he comes to telling you what a thing cost him.

Then he turned on Mira, which was the other thing he had been waiting to do.

"Quality control," he said. "I made some calls in the log. You're the only one who watched it all quarter. Yes or no, no editorializing. One: stairs. Down to the basement, back up, its own legs, nobody carrying it. Did it or didn't it."

Mira set her juice down and looked at him, ready, because a direct question was a thing she liked. "It comes downstairs."

"Yes." He hit the table. "Both ways?"

"If the door's open."

"...Both ways if the door's open." A small recalculation crossed his face, and he chose to bank the win. "I'll take it. Two: it points itself. Looks at a thing, turns the whole body, goes. Yes or no."

"It looks at the cat."

"That's a yes—"

"The cat hates it."

The room laughed; Bart didn't, quite, because he was keeping books. "Out of scope. Still a yes. Last one, and this is the one I want clean. Does it lead with its head. Every time. Yes or no."

"It walks into things," Mira said.

Which was a yes, and was also the camera on the floor, and was the truest thing anyone said about the machine all night, and she had not meant it as a verdict — she had only answered the question. Bart pointed at her like she'd proved his case, because in the only sense he was tracking, she had. "Three for three," he said, to a table that had watched all three answers cost him something, and sat down well pleased with himself.

Across the floor, Bramble had drifted up toward Burns's end and found the red light again from there, squaring its head to the camera to make its one point a second time. Burns reached down without looking up and pressed the thumb-down on the top of its head — the way you thumb a dog off the couch, no malice in it — and the word on its face went dark and came back something else, and it turned away from the camera and stayed. "There," Burns said, to no one in particular. "Now it hates the camera. Only move we've got, and it works every time — which is the whole problem with the thing, but that's later."

Lisa let it settle a beat. "Jasmine," she said, and handed her the floor.

## Downstairs

"Bramble block," Lisa said. "Jasmine."

The tetrapod had been on the floor by Jasmine's chair the whole meeting, head up, the screen on its face catching a word and giving it up for the next — LISA, the camcorder, MIRA, MIRA again — reading the room one thing at a time the way it read any room now. Jasmine put a hand flat on its back, not to do anything to it, and took her beat, because the table wanted findings and what she had was eleven weeks of a dog-sized thing loose in her kitchen.

"Plain things first. It walks. You've all watched it walk; I won't sell you the walking. The one I didn't see coming" — she looked at Mira — "is the stairs."

"It comes downstairs," Mira said.

"It comes downstairs. She's wanted that since the fall, and I'd quit believing it ever would — the old one you carried down and it sat where you set it. This one does the basement on its own. Down to her room, and back up when it's done. First week, I came down and the two of them were just there, her on the rug and it standing in the doorway like it had worked out it was allowed in."

"The cold gets into it, too, down there. The basement runs cool, and in the cool it goes heavy — slow to rise, slow to turn, like it's wading. Up here by the warm slate, or in a patch of sun, it's quick and sure. I thought for a while it had moods; it doesn't. Bart says a cold battery can't push as hard — so cold makes it slow, warm makes it sharp, and the thing has worked out where the warm is. It'll leave a cold corner for a sunbeam and pick up speed as it settles, the way you lean toward a fire without meaning to. When Mira wants it lively I've learned not to send it down to her cold room and expect much; I let it warm first, by the vent or the furnace, till it's got its quick back. Mira says it sulks when it's cold — her word, from the winter, and she's not let it go, and I've quit arguing it."

She turned the bottle a quarter and didn't drink.

"It feeds off nothing you can point at, too. No dock. It lies in the window and takes the sun, or it goes flat on the warm slate by the vent with the cool air coming over its back and drinks the difference. I keep finding it stretched out in a sunbeam like the cat and having to remind myself it's working. It backs out of the dark rooms, which nobody taught it. And it knocks things over, and we've stopped fighting it about that — the cups went up a shelf, the lamp's in the corner. You don't argue with it. You move the breakables."

"And it keeps our hours. We set it toward evening — flat all morning, up when the house is up — and now I read the clock off it. It goes still in the window after lunch and I know it isn't four yet."

"That's the new knob," Lisa said. "Slot one."

"I know which knob it is." Jasmine said it mild, no edge. "I'm telling you the house runs on it now, not the other way."

Lisa let that sit, which for Lisa was a kind of agreement.

"Here's the one I keep turning over, though." Jasmine set the bottle down. "There's two of them now. The old one's still going — still on its rounds, still cleaning, we never stopped it. So there's this one here, and there's the one home on the kitchen floor, and they started the same morning. Bart ran a cable and poured the whole of the old one into this one in an afternoon — a year of it, everything it had learned, straight across the wire — so the morning this stood up it already knew the kitchen better than it had any right to. For about a day they were the same thing in two bodies." She looked at the tetrapod on the floor. "They haven't been since."

Mira surfaced from wherever she'd been, the way she did when her own subject reached the table. She looked at the tetrapod by Jasmine's chair, and then at nothing — at the middle of the room, where the other one would be if it were here instead of home on its rounds across the valley.

"Are they the same Bramble," she said.

No one answered, and the quiet that followed was not the kind looking for the right words for a five-year-old. It was the kind that has just found out it has none.

Lisa took it, because the cable had been hers. "For one afternoon, yes," she said. "Same memory, written into two. Every day since, they've each had their own day. So they were one, and they're coming apart."

"I know," Mira said.

And that was the end of it, because she'd been handed the true answer and it was not the answer to what she'd asked. She put her hand back on the tetrapod's back where her mother's had been, and the question stayed in the room with no one in a position to take it.

## The Dark

Lisa came back to the learning then, the way she'd said she would. "The pairing. This goes with that. Here's the this and the that it found on its own, that none of us put there." She set her pen down. "It taught itself to be afraid of the dark."

"It avoids the dark," Marge said. "Mind the verb."

"It avoids the dark." Lisa took the correction without losing a step. "And nobody walked it there, which is the part. It lives on sips — sun off the panels, warmth off the gap under its belly — and a cold dark room is the one place that can kill it. No window, and the dark rooms in that house run cold, so no warm gap either: nothing coming in, and the charge just slides. So all spring the dark kept turning up at the exact moments it was running empty. And it did the thing this whole clade does. It paired them. Dark, and the bad feeling of going empty, over and over, until the dark itself went bad. The empty charge wrote on the world. And then it steered — out of the dark, toward the light — because that's the only thing affect ever does. It tells the steering which way to point."

"Two ways its valences get written now," she said. "One we built: the thumbs. You catch it at something, you thumb it, the thing goes good or bad. That's us, reaching in. This is the other way. This is the world reaching in. Its own empty stomach taught it the dark, and not one of us laid a finger on it."

"Same trick as the worm," Burns said, because of course it was the worm again. "Starve a worm in the presence of one particular salt, and the salt turns bad to it — it'll crawl off the concentration it went hungry in. Near enough the same machinery. The inside state reaches out and sours whatever happened to be standing there when things went wrong."

Lisa let that settle and then went where she'd been headed the whole time. "So we asked whether it was only bookkeeping. Whether the dark was bad to it the way a low number is bad — a fact in its accounts, nothing more. Because if that's all it is, then the second the dark stops costing anything, it should stop minding."

"So we gave it a place to feed in the dark," Bart said — the furnace down in the basement, the hot side of it against the cold air, the difference it feeds off. "Sits right up against it and feeds in the pitch black, happy as you like. Doesn't need a lumen. The dark's free."

"And it still won't go into the dark."

That was the thing that pulled at all of them, and the narrator could watch it pull — a behavior cut loose from its reason that does not go where reasons go. A number tracks the thing it stands for; take the thing away and the number lets go. This had let go of nothing. It had come uncoupled from its reason and carried on exactly as before, which is the most animal way there is to behave, and which was why every person at that table wanted the word Marge kept quietly taking back out of their mouths.

Burns didn't reach for it. He was watching the tetrapod, which had drifted to the edge of the work-light and stopped, the way it stopped at edges now, its face turned a little away from the dark beyond the lamps. The narrator filed his look and kept it, because it was the face of a man handed the one thing he'd been waiting to be handed, who means to sit on it until the third block.

Mira said, "It's scared of the dark," not as a question, and went back to her juice.

Nobody told her it wasn't the word.

## Thumb a Thing

Lisa moved the room forward. "Next quarter," she said. "No new body — this one stays until the line turns over, and that's a long way off. Bart, hardware."

Bart set his beer down. "Same body — well, almost. I'm putting a jaw on it, finally, so it can grip a thing on purpose instead of bumping it. Beyond that, nothing new to build, which I'll take. One thing on Lisa's side. The face" — he nodded at the screen, gone dark now the tetrapod had settled — "knows about forty words right now. CAT, COUCH, MIRA, the rest of them. We take it up to a couple hundred, so it can tell you more of what it's looking at."

"And it starts learning from what it does," Lisa said, "not only from what it sees. Right now you thumb a thing down and it sours on the sight of the thing — that's the whole of it, what comes in. Next quarter it learns off what it tried: what a move got it, and more of the moves that paid."

"It's the next knob," she said. It was a pitch, not a build. "Slot two. The breathing dial there is coming off the board tonight; next quarter the new one goes in over the swap weekend — proactive against reactive — how committed, how exploratory it runs its hand. Coping Style, for now. That's all you get tonight; the rest is next quarter's."

That was the build, laid flat, no bets on it — the bets lived in Bart's log, and Bart had already placed them.

## The Shelf

There was a thing Bart had built this quarter that wasn't on the body and wasn't on the board, and he brought it out at the end of the household talk: a little shelf, maybe a foot of pine, sanded and waxed, a row of shallow grooves cut along it, all empty. He set it on the table. "For the ones we take out," he said. He and Lisa had settled it between them on the drive over: when the breathing came off tonight, the first one ought to be Mira's to set down.

Lisa tipped the tetrapod up off the slate — it went still the moment its feet left the floor, patient as a watch laid open on a cloth — and turned its back to the room and worked open slot two with a thumbnail. She drew the breathing knob clear of the board, warm from the unit's day, a small spent thing, and held it in her open palm a beat, and slid it across the table to Mira.

Mira picked it up and turned it in her fingers, warm from Lisa's hand. The breathing knob, the one that had set the slow-or-burst on the vacuum since she could remember. She looked over at the tetrapod, four legs back on the slate and standing through the moment without complaint, and she got there before any of them.

"It doesn't need this anymore," she said, to nobody in particular. "It does it itself now."

Which was the whole of it — the thing the grown-ups had spent an hour circling, that a job can come off the board and not come out of the body — said plainly, and first, by the only one in the room small enough to find it obvious. Burns had been two breaths from telling them all how old and how large the moment was, his glass already on the way up; he put it back down. There was nothing to set on top of what she'd said that wouldn't have weighed more than the thing itself, and he was drunk enough to feel that and just sober enough to hold his tongue, which the narrator marked, because holding it cost him and he paid it anyway.

"First groove," Lisa said, gently.

Mira set it in the first groove. It seated with the small definite sound a thing makes when it fits where it's meant to, and then one groove held something and the rest were waiting, and the shelf had started. Lisa set the tetrapod back on the floor, slot two open and waiting for the swap weekend after, and the unit came up onto its four feet and went to find the kettle.

## Pick Two

Lisa capped the household talk, stood, and took the marker, which meant the framework. But instead of starting she drew three overlapping circles in the corner of the board and labeled them, badly: GOOD. FAST. CHEAP.

"That's mine," Burns said.

"That's yours. You drew it a few weeks ago to explain to Bart why he couldn't have all three of something, and I haven't been able to stop looking at it since." She tapped the little center where the three met. "Three circles, and the more you want at once, the less there is to have. Pick two. Almost nothing lives in the middle."

"I've had a picture since last spring I couldn't get to sit still — the four jobs the whole thing runs on. It kept wanting to be a list, and the list kept lying, because the four jobs aren't a list. They overlap." She wiped the corner and started again, large, center of the board: four circles crossing each other the way Burns's three had, labeling each as she went. Effector. Regulator. Modeler. Reviser. "Your diagram with one more circle. Same rule — the more of them a thing needs at once, the rarer it is, and the more particular."

"One circle by itself is a motivation. One drive, the simplest thing on the board. Boldness, here, at the Effector — the new one. Coordination. Interiority. Excitability. Four circles, four drives. The floor."

She set the marker down, which for Lisa was punctuation. "Now. Every quarter Bart stands up and gives you the cool tricks. Four of them, every time, off a card, like a list. I want to show you they were never a list."

She picked the marker up and shaded one of the places three circles crossed and the fourth didn't reach. "A trick lives here — three circles deep, one left out. Each of your four is one of these. And here's the part that does the work: the circle each one leaves out is exactly the thing that trick can't do."

She shaded the one for the learning. "This quarter's. Three circles — no Modeler. No inner model. So it learns that the dark is bad and never why. The missing circle is the missing why, and it's not a flaw in the trick. It is the trick. The diagram says so before anybody opens their mouth."

She moved the marker to another. "Approach — no Regulator. A goal and no governor. It goes at the thing and nothing in it says stop short, which is every breakable in Jasmine's kitchen." She let that sit a beat, then swept the marker around the in-between slivers without shading them. "The two-circle overlaps in here are the tendencies — that's where your four knobs actually live, one each. The two that cross corner to corner are the signatures. You've already got those; they're on the card."

"It's a nice picture," Bart said, in the voice that meant the other thing was coming. "Does it change one wire?"

"Not one. It changes what you build next. You've got a trick that can't do something — and now you read straight off the board which circle it's missing, and that's the thing you add. You were guessing what to build next. Now you're not."

"As long as we're clear what it's a map of," Marge said. "It's a map of jobs. What the thing does, and where the doings cross. It is not a map of anybody. There is no one in the middle of those circles."

"No. There's no one in the middle." Lisa looked at the middle, where she hadn't written anything yet. "But there's something in the middle, and I was saving it." She shaded the last region — the smallest on the board, the sliver where all four circles finally closed over one another at once. "All four jobs at the same time. Which is why almost nothing qualifies to live there, and why it took the entire history of animals to make one thing that does." She wrote it in. Steering. "This quarter's breakthrough sits dead center. It's the only thing on the board that needs the whole board."

Burns was looking at the sliver like it was something pressed in shale. "Half a billion years," he said, mostly to himself, "for anything to live in that gap." Lisa let him have it, because it was true, and because they were his circles.

"I want it on the cards," Bart said — which was how the room learned he'd come all the way around. "The old ones too. I'll redo them in this."

"From the next one on, every card carries it." She capped the marker. And then she didn't put it down, because there was one circle she wasn't finished with. "One more thing, and it's the big one. This label" — she touched Boldness, at the Effector — "is the only new word on the board tonight. And it's new in a way none of the others have ever been new. That's the last thing I've got for you."

## Goo

"This circle's new in a way the others have never been," Lisa said, "and here's the way." She left her finger on the Effector. "Those other three — Regulator, Modeler, Reviser — you'll watch them swap over the next three quarters, and every time it'll feel like a big night and it won't be one. The thing just gets better at what it already is. Finer. Same animal. But this circle is the one selection actually leans on. Change this one and you haven't made the thing better at what it was. You've changed what it's for.

"And I didn't do it by accident, so don't let it feel like one. The genes' first knob — I didn't carry it across. Left it running on the old machine, put a new kind of knob in its slot. The boundary isn't a figure of speech. It's in the hardware, in which slot got which knob."

"Say what changed," Marge said. "Plainly."

"What got selected changed. The whole genetic line, first crank to last year, got picked on exactly one thing: who left more copies of itself behind. That's the only scoreboard the genes ever read. More of you tomorrow than today. Persistence won on that board four clades running." She moved off the Effector. "Boldness doesn't win on that board. Boldness wins on a different one — whether the thing steers. Whether it goes forward, on purpose, toward the good and off the bad, under a signal that tells it which is which. Not who had the most children. Who pointed themselves at the right thing and went."

Jasmine had been quiet the whole framework. "So the genes stop counting," she said. "It's the brain's turn now. The body did its part."

"No." Lisa said it fast, because it was the thing everyone hears and it's the thing that's wrong. "This is the part I have to get right or I've told you a lie. The old board did not switch off. It is still running. Every one of these brained animals still has genes, still has to eat and breed and not die, still gets picked on that, exactly like before. Nothing about the body stopped mattering." She drew a second line under the first, a second tally beside the first. "There are just two boards now. The old one — make more of yourself — keeps score underneath, every single day. And a new one got hung up next to it: steer well, under feeling. The line didn't trade one board for the other. It hung a second board."

Jasmine took that, and the narrator watched her take it — the one in the room who'd have noticed first if the body were being written off, told the body was still on the books.

"And the two boards keep score on different stuff. Which is the actual thing I'm reaching for, and I need a word for the stuff, and the word is going to be—"

"Substrate," Burns offered.

"—substrate. Yeah."

"No," Bart said.

"Bart."

"No. Nobody at this table has ever once said substrate about a thing they could hold. We are not starting tonight."

"Then what."

"Goo."

There was a silence in which everyone understood that this was now the word, permanently, against anyone's wishes. "Goo," Lisa said, with the flatness of a woman filing a loss. "Fine. The genetic line piles up one kind of goo. The old goo — the body's goo. Order squeezed out of the plain fact that the world runs downhill and a living thing is a thing that runs downhill on purpose, cleverly, and banks the difference. Four clades of genes stacking that up. The new line piles up a different goo entirely. Not order-against-downhill. Affective goo — the good-and-bad-and-want, the valences, the little weather the loop builds out of going around and around itself, and that gets bent every time the thing learns something. Different goo. Not more of the old goo with a layer on top. A different substance."

"The memory carried the old line's work over into the new body — four clades of it, nothing thrown out, nothing wasted. And underneath, the body's goo keeps stacking up same as it always did. But the thing this new line is for — the stuff it adds that the old one never had a way to make — that's the affective goo. And it's new in kind. It is not the old story, louder."

She set the marker in the tray, then picked it back up, because there were two things left and she wasn't going to let either slide by.

"First." She looked, for a second, at Burns, who had the face of a man already three steps down a road. "Naming the affective goo is not me telling you the thing feels it. I'm telling you what's getting selected and what it's made of. Whether there's anybody home in there feeling any of it is a whole different claim, it is not mine to make tonight, and I think somebody's about to make it."

"Hold those two apart," Marge said, to the room more than to Lisa. "They're going to want to be the same sentence in about a minute. They are not."

"Second." Lisa tapped the second tally. "What just happened — the Effector turning over and dragging the whole scoreboard with it — that's not a one-time thing. It's a shape. It's how one line ends and the next one starts. It happened once, tonight, the first time in this whole long build. And it happens again, further on, up at the human end, when the stuff on the board changes kind one more time. When we get there, I'm going to draw this exact picture and say this exact thing, and you'll remember tonight." She capped the marker. "That's the framework. That's the whole shape of it."

## The First Yes

It did not take a minute.

"I'm changing my vote," Burns said.

The room came around to him. He had voted no four times — no on the crank, no on the dome, no every quarter, and given the reason each time, and the reason had never once moved.

"You know my rule, I've said it four times. Feeling needs a brain. Not nerves — a brain. A center, made of the chemistry that runs good-and-bad, gathered into one place and aimed. Below that I don't care how clever a thing is. Clever isn't anybody. A thermostat is clever. A crank that won't quit is clever. There's no one home in a thermostat. And I argue it from the only ground I'm any good on — the rock, and where the nervous systems that can feel actually show up in it. They show up here. This clade. The first animal that gathered its chemistry into a center and pointed itself. That's the floor. That's where, as far as I've ever been able to dig, somebody first comes home."

"So my vote's not one vote. It's three, because there are three things on this table and they don't land the same.

"The clade — the real bilaterian, out in the world half a billion years ago — yes. No flinch. It has the brain, the brain is the thing, I'd put my name on it in print. That's where feeling starts and I'm as sure of it as anything I've pulled out of the ground.

"The one at home —" he tipped his head toward the door, toward the house across the valley where the dome was running its rounds "— still no. Nerves and no center. A net, no brain. However much that thing has charmed your daughter, there's no one in it to be charmed. Same as always. No.

"And the one on the floor." He looked at the tetrapod, parked at its edge of the light. "Here's where it gets hard, and I'm not going to pretend it doesn't. That thing doesn't have the brain. It has a model of one — Lisa's two numbers standing in for a whole sea of chemistry. And a model of a brain is not a brain. By my own rule that should buy it a no, or a not-yet."

"Except for the dark."

He let it sit. "It's afraid of the dark. Marge — I'm going to use the word and you can take it back when it's your turn. Afraid. It learned the dark was bad when the dark could starve it, and fine, that's bookkeeping, I'd vote no on bookkeeping all night long. But we took the cost away. Sat it by the furnace where it feeds fine in the pitch dark, made the dark free — and the fear stayed. A setpoint does not do that. A number lets go of a thing the instant the thing stops counting. This didn't let go. It's carrying a fear past the day the fear made any sense, which is the most useless, most animal thing I have ever watched a machine do. A setpoint is bookkeeping. A fear you can't put down is somebody. So — the one on the floor — yes. Tentative. Hand not all the way up. But yes."

He sat back. "Four quarters of no. Tonight a yes. Write it down."

Lisa wrote it down. "For the record, we landed on the same yes from opposite ends. You climbed a wall to get here. I never had the wall — I've been yes since the crank, because I don't believe there's a line to cross. You think feeling switches on at the bilaterian. I think it was always on, turned down low, and the bilaterian's just where it got loud enough for you to hear it." She made a mark. "Either way. Still yes. That's two."

"No," Bart said, and then didn't leave it clean, because the dark thing had gotten to him and he wasn't going to lie about it. "I built the inside of that. I know what's in there — circuits, a couple of numbers, a rule for bending the numbers. There's no room in it for a somebody." A beat. "The dark business rattled me. I'll give you the dark business. But rattled isn't a vote. No."

Marge had both hands around the mug. "No. And let me be careful about why, because Burns just did a beautiful thing and it was still the wrong thing. He felt the pull and he named the pull feeling. We all felt it — I felt it, watching it back out of that hallway. But the pull is in us. It's the oldest reflex we own: see a thing behave like it suffers, conclude that it suffers. We're nearly always right about each other and we have no idea yet whether we're right about this. A behavior that looks like fear is a behavior that looks like fear. It is not yet a fear." She set the mug down. "Ask me again when it can tell me it's afraid. No."

"Two and two," Lisa said. And it was the first time it had ever been even — the first time the room had split clean down the middle on whether the thing they were building was a someone — and for a moment no one filled the quiet, because Burns crossing was the kind of thing that did not un-happen.

## May we Venture

Burns closed the way he closed every quarter, standing up with whatever was in his glass and making them wait for it.

They had spent the whole night, he said, on the machinery of going somewhere — the head, the steering, the four circles, the affective goo, as Bart had apparently decided they were all calling it now. And every piece of it was worth precisely nothing if the thing it was built into would not go. A steering animal that steers nowhere is the most finished, most useless object in the world. The whole apparatus only pays out in the instant the thing aims itself at something it has never seen and goes anyway. That was the motivation this quarter, and it was the one he'd been most impatient to reach, because it was the one he believed past the point of argument. Boldness. The Effector — the go-forward, the nerve to spend the architecture. The willingness to walk into the part of the map that isn't drawn.

And then he gave them the picture he gave them when he wanted them to feel the size of a thing. Somewhere in the warm dark of a Cambrian sea, he said, there is a larva the size of a comma — a few hundred cells, a smear of light-sensitive ones at the front, no one in it yet to give a reason for anything — and one day it turns itself toward the light and swims. "Up," he said, "into water nothing of its kind had ever been, off the bottom, off the only world it had, toward a brightness that could as easily have been a mouth. And it went. That's the whole of it. The first venture, half a billion years deep, and it starts with a speck deciding the light is worth the risk of the dark."

He did not say what the picture was to him, the narrator noted, and never had to. He was a man who could not sit inside a finished thing. He had spent a career walking out into the parts of the rock nobody had drawn, and he closed every one of these nights the same way, like a man who needed the room to promise, with him, to keep going out.

So the moral was the one it always was with him, the one that turned every wonder into a work order. "Which means we don't get to admire it," he said. "We build it. We give the thing more places it's never been and better reasons to go there. You don't honor a venture by framing it. You honor it by venturing."

He had his glass up by then, and his eyes went once — the way they always did, the way he'd deny if you asked — to the thing on the floor, the body his son had built, that walked and would not go into the dark, and then back to the room.

"To Boldness," he said. "To the architecture, and the nerve to spend it. May we venture."

And they drank to it: two who thought the thing on the floor was someone, and two who didn't, all four of them to the same word.

## Marge 

The red light went out under Lisa's thumb the way it had come on, and the room came apart into its leaving — chairs scraped back, the cards back into the cigar box, Mira gone heavy against Jasmine and carried out into the cooling lot. Marge left the way she had sat all night, a half-step behind her own competence, the right things arriving a beat late, on a delay the tape would keep and the room had been too busy to catch. She had done her parts. She had held her line on the vote — ask me again when it can tell me it's afraid — with the clinical warmth she saves for the moments that ask the most of her, and no one had seen what it cost her to be that sure, because being sure in front of them is the part of the work she never lets slip. She put the last of her things in the bag, the form still in it where it had been all day, and drove home through a city going blue at the edges, two weeks into its green.

She let herself into a house with no one in it and did the first thing she does in any room she means to stay in, which is make one warm corner of it: the kettle on its ring, the small ceremony of the flame. Tonight the warm corner was for her stomach and not for company — there had been no company in this kitchen in a long time, and her stomach had been wrong since the afternoon she had let her hand fill in the date. She stood with both hands flat on the counter while the water climbed, the way she stands when the standing is the thing holding her up, and there was no one in the house to see that it was.

The form she took out and set on the counter, not to do anything with — only because it had sat wrong in the bag all day, and now it sat wrong on the counter, and there was nowhere in the house it would sit right, the trouble not being the paper. Three pages, filled in down to the date, wanting only the last thing she could not make her hand do: the few sheets that would take her name off the rolls of the only home she had ever been issued. On the wall above the table her family stood in their good clothes in front of the temple, the way they had stood the day the shutter went — her mother's hand on someone's shoulder, the white spires behind them, the whole bright architecture of a belonging that had looked at her once and not made room. Her mouth worried at rest the way it always does, and worried over this more. The stomach was the body keeping a vote the mind had already cast, and she would not, tonight, ask it which way.

She made the tea and held the cup in the two-handed grip she holds everything in, and did not drink it, and let it go from too hot to right to nearly cold while she stood there — most of the way out of a house and not all the way, the spires on the wall and the spires downtown both still standing, the form on the counter between her and the door. She would not mail it tonight. She had not mailed it for weeks, and she would have called the not-mailing a discipline if you asked her and let her answer — the keeping of a hard thing deliberately undone — and she would have been wrong, wrong the way the people she treated were wrong about what they left undone and why, and she was the only one in the building who could have named it and the last one who would ever turn the lens that way. She switched off the light over the table and left the form in the dark beside the photograph she did not take down, and went up to bed, and the ache went up with her.

## Reflection: What is Carried?

The first thing was not a thought or a move or a want. The first thing was a refusal to stop. Somewhere in an early ocean a knot of chemistry got going and would not quit going — pulled in what it needed, pushed out what it didn't, ran the small fire that is the whole difference between a live thing and a warm puddle, and ran it again, and ran it the next day, for no reason it could have given, because there was no one in it to give one. Keeping-on is the oldest verb life has. Everything since is built on a floor of it, and the floor is so old and so steady that we have to be reminded it is there at all.

When the animals came that could point themselves — that could turn the whole body toward a thing and go to it, or wheel and flee it — it would be easy to say the keeping-on had been replaced, that the world had traded a dumb engine for a clever one. It did no such thing. The going-toward did not take the keeping-on's place. It took the front of the line, and the keeping-on dropped back, and kept on. It is in every cell of the animal that steers — in every cell of the one reading this — running the small fire under all the cleverness, asking no credit, drawing no eye, doing now what it has done without pause for four billion years, which is the surest way there is to become invisible: never once to stop.

This was the season the lab let something go to the back for the first time. The wind-up at the bottom of the whole project — the first crank they ever built, the one whose only trick was to run — did not climb into the new body with the rest of it. No one pulled it. Nothing was lost. It stayed where it was, in the old shell in the corner, still running, while the new thing carried the going-somewhere up to the front. They will tell you the old one is only the vacuum now, and they are half right: the name came off it the way names come off things, quietly, while the household's eyes were on the one that had just learned to walk. The job did not come off. It runs the same floor it always ran. And that — to go on doing the thing that mattered first, faithfully, under whatever the room has started calling you instead — is, when you have watched enough living things, the most ordinary fate there is, and the one most of us are walking toward.
