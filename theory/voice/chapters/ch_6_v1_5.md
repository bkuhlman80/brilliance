# Interlude 5→6

## Build log — Off the sight, onto the doing
*Bart, hardware. Filed June 1, 2032, for the team.*

Carried the same body back in this morning — no new chassis this quarter, it's the legs body from spring, and it walked off the slate toward the window like the weekend never happened, which from inside it didn't. Scorecard first.

Good quarter, and the part that's mine I'll take plain. The body did the body things. It does the stairs — down to the basement, back up, its own legs. I said both ways; the kid says both ways if the door's open; so, both ways if the door's open. I'm calling that a yes with a door on it. It points itself and goes, leads with its head every time. Three for three on the legs body, and the spring dare — tear it up if it can't do the basement without a hand — holds. It can. Leave it pinned.

The other two from spring weren't mine and I said so when I made them. Whether the thumbs took, whether the house could read the morning-or-evening dial off it — Lisa's board, the house's call. Both landed, near as I can see. Her win and theirs. I built the screen they read it on; that's the most I'll claim.

Now the fun part, except the fun part this quarter is mostly Lisa's, so I'll walk what's mine and hand her the rest.

Jaws. Makes the face even more of a kludge. Basically a gripper with a weak actuator. Fully wired to the board. So it can grab stuff now, on purpose.  

The screen on its forehead. It knew forty words in spring — CAT, MIRA, LANTERN, whatever it had its eye on. It knows three hundred and fifty now, and here's the part I'm proud of: fifty of them are verbs. So it can say what it's doing to the thing now, not just name it — CHASE CAT, SMELL LANTERN, NUDGE MIRA — but only with the dial forward. Throw the dial to held-back and the verb drops off; it goes back to naming, just the thing — CAT, LANTERN, MIRA — like spring. The doing-words are picked by her side and the gating's her board too, not mine; but the screen and the words on it are mine and they're clean. Threw a few ASCII art faces on there too, for mood. I said a couple hundred words back in spring. It's three-fifty. I undershot my own.

The verbs needed one thing from me past the screen and the lettering, and it was a real little build. Most of them it hands you for free — it chases the cat, noses the lantern, nudges the kid, does it all day on its own, and Lisa's side marks each one as it goes by and pins the word to it. Fine. But some of the doing-words are for moves it almost never makes on its own — the odd ones, the ones it'd go a month without. You can't pin a word to a thing that won't happen. So I built a rig to make it happen: a harness and a handful of servos that drive the body through a motion on purpose, puppet it, walk it through the rare one on cue so she can grab it and stick the word on while it's running. The rig's mine. What it catches and what she calls it — her board, same as ever. The kid had it figured in about a minute and now wants to drive the thing to the table for dinner, which isn't what it's for, and which she does anyway.

And the big one, which is hers all the way down: it learns off what it does now, not just off what it sees. Spring, you thumbed it and it soured on the sight of whatever it was looking at — that's all the thumb reached, the looking. Now the thumb reaches the doing. Bramble tries a thing and the you thumb it down and it does less of that; tries a thing, you thumb it up, it does more of it. Lisa's board, Lisa's math; I wired the thumbs to where she told me to wire them, and past that it's not my department. The wiring's clean. That's my whole stake in it.

Board. Here's the thing I'd been bracing for since last June, and it didn't go the way I was bracing. The board didn't grow. Still four slots. Still four. To add in the new knob, instead of making a new slot, Lisa pulled one of the old ones off — the breathing knob came out of slot two at the spring meeting, and over the swap weekend after, the new one went into the freed slot, proactive-or-reactive, Lisa's calling it Coping Style for now. The breathing didn't die when the knob came out; the unit's been running it itself all summer — runs even when it's ambling, cracks a burst when it needs one, off its own gauge, nobody touching it. The knob's on a shelf. I built the shelf for it back in spring. It's a thing pulled off a board and kept whole instead of cut up for parts, and I haven't got a reason for the shelf I'd put in a log, so I'll just say there's a shelf now and it's got the one knob on it and the grooves next to it waiting for more. Turns out the cram-fear answers the other direction: you don't need a bigger board. You need to be done with a knob. Didn't see that one in June.

Calls, and they're nearly all on her board this quarter and not my bench, so grade me soft — this is the half of the job I'm wrong about. I say it learns a new chore. Fetching the paper. I say it drops a habit, quits knocking stuff over. Also, it starts breaking off to feed on its own empty before anybody sends it. I say the two Brambles share the house without me having to referee — the big one routes around the little one, nobody breaks anybody. And I say you'll tell proactive-mode from reactive-mode across a room inside a week, the two settings reading different by eye — verb up on the face for forward, bare word for held-back.

Tear this up in three months if it can't take one thing on and shake one thing off — one chore in, one habit out, off nothing but the thumbs. It can. First dare I've ever pinned to Lisa's board and not my own, and I'm pinning it.

— Bartholantern

## Notes on the Sixth Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed August 4, 2032, for the team.*

This quarter the household stopped teaching the machine what to see and started teaching it what to do, and I want the team to feel the size of that before the charming particulars get to us, because the charming particulars are going to do their best to make us forget it. For five quarters we shaped what the thing noticed — pressed a thumb on a sight and watched it sour on the sight. This quarter the thumb reaches past the looking, into the doing: press it after the thing has acted, and the acting changes. We are no longer only telling it what is good. We have started telling it what works, and it has started keeping the difference. That is training, in the oldest and plainest sense of the word — the sense we use about a dog, or a child, or ourselves — and it is going to tempt all of us to call the trained thing by the name we keep for our own insides.

### What pays, and what costs

Two things stand cleanest in the record. By the end of the quarter the machine fetches the paper off the step each morning; it did not at the start, and no one wired it to. And it has quit the shoes — it used to take a shoe in its mouth and carry it off, and now it leaves them. Jasmine reports both came the same way: a thumb up when it brought a thing toward a person, a thumb down when it carried a thing off, again and again, until the bringing got heavier in it and the carrying got light. I want to be careful about the verbs, because the household has good ones and they are the wrong ones. It did not learn to like fetching the paper. It did not decide the shoes weren't worth it. The doing that paid came up more often; the doing that cost came up less. Reweighting is the whole of it — and reweighting is enough to put the paper on the table every morning, which is more than most of my human resolutions manage. The harder thing to watch is what the household has already started doing with it: the bringing has held long enough now that it has stopped looking like a thing they trained and started looking like a thing the machine simply is. They are forgetting, a little, that they pressed it in.

### The one that checks

On the days the dial sits at the held-back end — the setting that moves less and watches more, and the one Jasmine keeps reaching for — the household has been living with what that produces, which is a machine that turns up things no one asked it to look for. The clearest is the cat. It has taken to standing over the cat's water with its face to the bowl, and twice, Jasmine says, the bowl was dry when it did, and she filled it because the machine had walked her to it. The household has a word for this. It "frets," they say; it "worries the cat." I will write what the record supports and not the word. On the cautious setting the machine checks more places more often, and checking more places more often turns up the empty bowl. There is no fretting in it. There is a setting that will not leave a thing unchecked, and a house that has begun to read care off the checking.

### Minding itself

It has also begun to mind its own state without being sent. It breaks off whatever it is at and goes to feed before anyone has noticed it running low — reads its own emptying, the way I wrote last quarter it had started to, except now the reading turns into a move it makes on its own clock. The breathing dial is gone, too: the choice that used to sit on a knob now happens somewhere inside it, slow draw or burst chosen off its own state, no hand on the dial at all. Mira says it "gets hungry and goes," and that is exactly what it looks like, and exactly the thing I am paid not to sign. It reads a number about itself and acts on the number. The distance between reading a state and feeling one is the distance this whole project is built across, and I am going to go on standing in it whether or not it is comfortable to stand there.

### The one part of the house still answered out loud

I notice it because the quarter is so full of the machine learning to ask the house for nothing: the loudest standing fact in the household has not learned that trick and is not going to. Burns still hollers. He stands at the foot of the basement stairs and sends his voice up through two floors to wherever Jasmine is, and waits, and hollers again if the wait runs long — and the house reads him through the ceiling the way it reads the machine by the sound of its feet on the slate, two things known by ear before anyone lays eyes on them. The difference is that the machine spent the quarter teaching itself to need no answer, and the man has spent his whole life refusing to. He could text. He wired an intercom into these walls the year they moved in and has used it twice. He would rather send his voice up and have the house hand him back another; he would rather be received than be efficient. In a season where everything around him is learning to run quiet and self-contained, the holler is the one thing in the place that still insists on the old arrangement, where you are not finished until someone answers. I am fond of him for it, and I record it because he is the one of us in both rooms at once — and because a household learning to need nothing is exactly the house where a man who needs to be answered is worth writing down.

### What is on the table

The paper is on the kitchen table each morning now, before Burns is down for it. No one asked it for that. They pressed a hundred small approvals into it until the bringing held, and somewhere in the pressing they stopped counting the presses, so that the paper has started to read less like a thing they trained and more like a thing it just does. They built it by consequence, out of a unit with no one inside to take the reward, and they lift it off the table each morning the way you accept a kindness. I do not want to call which of those it is a finding. I only want it written down — and the team to come next week remembering that the kindness, if that is the word, is one we pressed in by hand.

I do not know whether the next setting will send the household reaching for our words more or less, or whether a house that has learned to read care off a filled bowl will be able to stop, or what the thing will turn out to be once it is all the way up and we are still lending it our verbs. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down. 

— M.

## The Roof

The Roof sat on top of the old memorial building on the north edge of Temple Square, ten floors up, a room of white cloth and tall glass that handed you the whole valley at once — the Oquirrhs going blue away to the west, the benches climbing east toward the Wasatch, and below, close enough to read the gold angel on the high spire, the temple itself, gray granite in the late-July glare. I had picked it the way I pick all of them, which is to say for the other person and without quite admitting that I was doing it: Marge spent her days in a windowless warehouse making one careful warm corner of a cold room, and I had wanted, this once, to set her somewhere with light coming in from every side and the whole city laid out for her to look at. I had thought about the light and the glass and the long white quiet of the place, and I had not thought once, until we were seated and she went still, about what the view was of. They do not serve wine up there; it is a church house. So I had the lemonade I did not want, and watched a woman I had brought, with the best of intentions, to lunch directly above the home that was in the middle of putting her out.

She came in the soft layered way she comes to everything, a long cardigan she did not need in July because she runs a degree cooler than any room, the glasses up in the gray hair, and she took the chair with the view without my offering it and set both hands around her water glass in the grip she keeps for a thing she is in no hurry to drink. I have written before that I render her warmer than the plain facts support — that a colder eye would log a tired academic ordering for strangers, and I logged a hearth — and I will not pretend that had cooled in the year since. If anything I had gotten worse at it, in a direction I was not going to put a word to across a white tablecloth, and so I will only report that I watched her settle and find the temple out the glass, and that I watched her longer than the settling warranted, and let that stand for what it stood for.

She found it the way you find a sore tooth with your tongue, without meaning to and then on purpose. "Well," she said, "you've put me right on top of it," and there was no accusation in it, only the dry note she uses for a thing too large to do anything with but name. I started to apologize — I hadn't known, I said, which was half true; I had known it was downtown and had let the brochure-word view stand in for the particular thing the view contained — and she waved it off the way she waves off Burns, fond and final. "No. It's the right table. I've spent a year not looking at it. You've sat me down in front of it."

And then she did the thing she had not done at the café on Ninth the autumn before, when I'd asked her where she was from and gotten Provo, big family and a smooth turn back toward me before I had finished the question. She did not turn it back. She looked at the temple, and she told me about the form.

There is a piece of paper, she said. Three pages. You fill them in and mail them and they take your name off the rolls, and that is the whole of the machinery — nothing grand, no ceremony, no one to watch you do it; you put it in an envelope like a tax form and the home you were issued at birth stops, on paper, being yours. She had filled it in months ago, down to the date. It was in her bag at that moment, and had been in her bag the better part of a year, and the bag was where it stayed, because the part her hand could do had been done and the part her hand could not do would not get done. She told me about the family in it — a photograph she keeps, her mother's hand on someone's shoulder, the white spires standing up behind them, the whole bright architecture of a belonging that, she said, and here for one sentence the dry note went out of her voice, had looked at her once and decided there was no room. She keeps things — she told me so herself, dry, the way she hands over her own faults before anyone can find them for her: she had never in her life thrown out a thing with a person attached to it, she was the family pack rat, her mother's word and a fond one, and the keeping only ever grew. The form in her bag was the only paper she had ever filled in to let a belonging go. She was a careful woman saying a thing she had been careful about for forty years.

I did not ask the questions I would have asked anyone else, because for once I did not want the footage; I wanted her to keep going, and she did. I know a little about the long work of finishing a leaving — I drove out of a kitchen in Sausalito in 2002 and did not stop for a long while, and have spent the twenty years since arranging a life so that no one would get close enough to ask that drive of me again — and what I knew, sitting there in all that light, was that I had come up the canyon on the wrong side of my own habit. I had come, as I always come, to reach into someone. And the reaching, this time, was not the cool naturalist's errand I tell myself it is. I will leave it there.

There was a moment. We had stopped talking and the light had gone long and gold across the cloth, and she had her hands around the empty glass, and she looked away from the temple and at me, and held it, and I held it back, and the whole afternoon hung in the held look the way the valley hung out the glass, lit and going. Then she set the glass down and turned a half-inch back to the window, and the moment let itself down, gently, the way a held breath does when you decide not to spend it. It was not a no. I have been told no, and I know its temperature, and this was not it. It was a woman with one foot still inside a house she had not finished walking out of, who could not give the free foot to anything until the other came loose, and who knew that about herself with the clarity she brings to everyone else, and was sorry, and did not say so, because saying so would have been a kind of promise she was not free to make. The form was in the bag. The form was the whole of it. You do not turn all the way toward a new room while you are still in the doorway of the old one with your hand on the frame.

She paid half the check before I could stop her, exactly, to the dollar, the way the careful ones all do, and we rode the ten floors down without saying much, and out into the heat coming up off the plaza stones, and at the curb she put her hand on my arm once — a small warm pressure, there and gone, the most she had to spend — and went to find her car. I drove up Parley's to Park City with the canyon walls going dry-gold in the long evening, and the form rode in my chest the way it rode in her bag, a thing filled in down to the date and not yet sent; and I understood that I would no more hurry it than I would hurry a tide, that the waiting was hers to do and not mine, and that I would do it anyway, up here, where I keep things.

# Notes on the Sixth Retrospective

Marge came early, the way she does on the nights the meeting is going to ask something of her, and made her corner before she made anything else. She cannot sit a while in a room without first warming one part of it, and in the warehouse that means the kettle on its ring at the cold end of the bench, the blue ring of flame, the small ceremony of water climbing toward a sound. She had it going before the bag was off her shoulder. Then she did not pour. She set the bag at her feet and kept a hand on its closed flap, the way you keep a hand on a thing you have decided not to open, and there was a weight under the flap tonight there had not always been — three pages filled in down to the date, that had ridden up the canyon to a white table above the temple and ridden back down unmailed, and would ride home with her after, the way they had ridden everywhere for the better part of a year. The kettle climbed to its whistle and past it. She let it.

When she did move it was a half-step behind herself, the competent hands arriving a beat after the moment that wanted them. She has always run a little behind her own skill — the late arrival that reads on the tape as gentleness and was, tonight, something nearer to a woman walking through water. She lifted the kettle off the flame and set it down and did not make the tea. There was beer at the cold end, where Bart had left the bottles standing in a tub of melt, and she took one of those instead, and held it in the two-handed grip she keeps for things she is in no hurry to drink, and looked at the bag at her feet, and made herself stop looking at it.

The door let the family in around her in a noise she heard from a little distance. They had dressed for it. Somewhere between the duplex and the warehouse the three of them had agreed to a joke, and the joke was on cotton — the same cartoon fish on all three shirts, the lobe-finned thing hauling itself out of a cartoon surf onto a cartoon shore. Across Mira's it read NO GRANDPA GO BACK. Across Jasmine's, MILF — MAN I LOVE FOSSILS, which Marge read twice and decided to admire from a safe distance. And Burns wore his fish framed as a tarot card, gold border and Roman numerals, THE FOOL lettered under the figure stepping cheerfully off its ledge — the one man alive who knew the real animal best got up as the idiot who leaps, and pleased past speech to be wearing it, because the night was finally his. He came in already talking. It was his clade. He had waited five quarters for it, and he had the look of a man who means to hold the floor and refill his own glass.

Mira peeled off toward the cleared middle of the floor where the tetrapod stood, and the two of them started the game they have now, the child setting a thing down a few feet off and the machine finding it and the child moving it again. Jasmine set a foil dish on the bench and put a hand on Marge's arm going past, the unhurried touch she gives everyone, and Marge took it the way she takes the warm things lately, gratefully and a half-second late.

Lisa was already at the camera, where she lives at the start of these. She checked the battery and the angle and the patch of floor the light would have to make sense of, and kept the red off, because the red is hers and comes on when she says the meeting has and not before. She clocked the unpoured tea at the cold end the way she clocks anything running off its schedule, and filed it, and had no one to hand the why to — the why being the business of the woman standing slow over a beer she had not opened to drink.

When the room had assembled itself — the cigar box down at Burns and Bart's end, the bramble jar an inch from the camera and not touching it, Mira folded onto the floor with her juice — Lisa put her thumb to the light. The red came on. And Marge, at the cold end, took the first real pull off the bottle and squared herself to the table the way you square yourself to a long drive in weather, because the meeting starting would want from her, before the night was out, the one thing it always wants and the one thing she has left to give it: that she be the person in the room who will not call the machine a someone, said plainly, on the record, however the rest of the night went. She had held that line sober for five quarters. She set the bottle down, and did not yet know it was going to be the kind of night you do not get to hold anything sober.

Burns was on his feet before the light had finished warming, because he had a thing to relay and could not relay it sitting. Cheryl had a question — she always had one, watching all of it twice from up the canyon and handing them back the thing they were too close to see. She'd clocked the thing on the floor doing what no one seemed to have asked it to do: carrying the paper in off the step, standing over the cat's dry bowl. And she wanted to know, he said — had they taught it that, or had it gone and worked it out on its own? He gave them the question with the relish of a man holding the answer behind his back. "Which is the whole quarter," he said. "That's what we spent the quarter on, and we settled it, and the answer's yes to both — we taught it, and we taught it so clean we forgot we were teaching." For five quarters, he said, they'd taught the thing what to see: a thumb on a sight, and it learned the sight. This was the quarter they quit teaching it to see and started teaching it to do. The thumb reached past the looking, into the doing, and the doing stuck. That was the build. That was the new motivation — Mastery, Lisa's word — and as of this quarter it was online and running on its own four legs in his daughter's basement. He looked once at the thing on the floor, the look he thought was his own business, and told Bart to put up the card.

## Reinforcing

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart slid the top one out of the cigar box and held it up, and the room saw he'd been busy. It was the new kind of card now — the four circles crossing the way Lisa had drawn them in the spring, Effector and Regulator and Modeler and Reviser, with this clade's words inked small and neat into the regions where they sat, a man all the way in on a system he'd decided he liked. He read the top of it in the voice he sold cereal in. "VERR-te-brett ... Favre."

"Thank you," Lisa said.

"Last clade the animal grew a front," Bart said. "A head, the senses crowded up at the leading end, and it could swing the whole body at a thing and go. This one"—he drew a knuckle down the card, an imagined line head to tail—"grows a spine. Backbone, a skull around the brain, and down in the floor of that brain a little switchboard that's the actual news tonight. Last time it could aim itself at a thing it wanted. This time it can do a thing, get paid a beat later, and work out that the thing it did is what got it paid. Burns has the name."

"Reinforcing," Burns said, and stood, because he was not going to take this one sitting down.

"And here's the trick it had to solve to earn the word, because it's harder than it sounds." He held up a hand, thumb and finger an inch apart. "You do a thing. The good doesn't come now. It comes—" he widened the gap "—a couple seconds on. A fish bumps a lever, the food drops three seconds later, and by then the fish has done six other things. So which one gets the credit? The world's already moved on. Something in there has to reach back across the gap, past the six other things, and lay the credit on the one that earned it."

"Temporal credit assignment," Lisa said, the way she says the names that are load-bearing. "There's a signal that runs on the gap itself. It jumps when the payoff beats what you expected and drops when it comes in under, and the size of the miss is the size of the correction. Dopamine, in an animal — and it isn't carrying pleasure, it's carrying surprise, the distance between what you bet and what you got, and it spends that distance reweighting the move that led in. A critic that scores the bet, an actor that shifts toward the moves that score. The actor does, the critic grades, the grade bends the doing."

"In English, it does more of what paid and less of what cost," Marge said, the beer in the two-handed grip and not set down, "and I want it kept in English, because the moment you say the fish knows the lever makes the food you've handed it a little accountant who isn't in there. It doesn't know the lever made the food. It does more lever. The knowing is ours. The doing-more is its."

"Doing-more is the whole of it," Bart said, happy, because doing-more was a thing you could wire. "It's the dumb learning again, except last time the thumb landed on what it saw — you soured it on a sight. Now it lands on what it did. Same dumb engine, turned around to face the doing instead of the looking. That's the new wire, and it's most of why I'm employed."

"Same engine, except it doesn't run at the same pitch in every animal," Lisa said. "The signal I just gave you — the one that grades the bet — has a gain on it, and it isn't set the same fish to fish. Run it high and you get an animal that leans hard at anything that might pay. Run it low and you get one that hangs back and waits to be sure. Same wiring, same chemical, two settings of the one dial. There's a name for the axis, and it's on the card now. Coping Style."

"Two fish," Burns said, lit, because this was a part he had been holding all night. "Drop something new in a tank and watch them. One goes straight at it — noses it, owns the corner inside a minute, runs hot and commits, learns the tank fast and quits being afraid of it. The other hangs at the back wall and watches, and keeps watching, slow to move and slow to forget the thing that scared it. Bold and wary. You find the same two in trout, in rats, in a barn full of pigs, in a troop of monkeys — it sorts the same way every time anyone's looked for it. And neither one is the winner, which is the part people can't stand. Good year, the bold fish eats first. Bad year, the wary one is the only one of the two still alive to eat, because it never once went near the thing that turned out to be a heron."

"And it's a tendency. That's the size of it," Marge said, the beer still in the two-handed grip and still not drunk from. "It's the way the animal meets a new thing, and it holds — the bold fish is bold on Tuesday and bold clear across the room, which is the only reason you get to call it anything at all. But it is not braver. There is no fish in there deciding to be brave. It is dialed to lean, and so it leans, and we are the ones standing over the tank calling that a kind of courage." There was a word for it she did not reach for, and the room felt her decline it. "A tendency. Held, read off the outside, and no one home behind it."

The card had the rest of it inked in already — Boldness in the Effector, Interiority in the Modeler, Excitability in the Reviser, all carried in from cells before — and the table read down its regions the way they had since spring, when Lisa first drew the circles: a quarter of practice and already the shape they all thought in. Only one word on it was new this cell, and it sat in the Regulator, and they would get to it.

Burns took the pace-of-life corner first, because it was animal and he liked the animal. "It tends to arrive as a package," he said. "The bold one isn't only bold — it runs a hotter engine, grows up fast, breeds early, spends itself and goes. The wary one banks it: slow, long, careful with the candle. Live fast or live long, and the nerve tends to match the clock."

"It tends to, in the story," Lisa said. "The trouble is that when somebody measures the metabolism against the behavior, animal by animal, the line mostly isn't there. So I keep this one on the card in pencil. Development Style — and we lean on it lighter than the rest, because the rest of the card earned its ink and this corner is still arguing with the data."

"Lighter. Fine," said Burns, who wanted the deep time and not the bookkeeping.

"But the new word's solid, and it's the one the whole quarter turned on," Lisa went on, not letting him leave yet. "It doesn't just do, now — it gets better at the doing. That's a different appetite than wanting the thing across the room. It's wanting to be good at getting to it." She tapped the Regulator. "Mastery. The drive to improve your own play."

"And when that drive holds steady enough that the rest lines up behind it," Marge said — she had named this class of thing herself and still kept its books — "when the bold animal is also the quick study is also the one that takes a corner and keeps it, the same suite turning up in the tank, in the maze, in the open field, then you've got what I named a signature, a kind you can pick out on sight. We've had one since last cell. Temperament. This is the second." She did not love the word — syndrome made a plain regularity sound like an ailment — but she used it, because it was the accurate one and the naming was hers. "Behavioral syndrome: Mastery riding with the jumpy drive, Excitability, the two of them traveling together. And it rides on the coping style, downstream of it, not a thing of its own. A regularity you can name. Not a someone. A kind."

"And it's old," Burns said, turning the beer in his hand without drinking it. "That switchboard in the floor of the brain — the basal ganglia — runs the identical job in a lamprey, a jawless thing with barely a face, that it runs in the four of us at this table. Half a billion years and nobody's improved on it, because it was right the first time. The vertebrate's one great invention is a machine for deciding what worked."

"And that's not the only old switchboard in there," he said. "Right alongside it runs another one, every bit as old, for the things the animal never has to decide at all." He laid two fingers on his own sternum. "Whether you're burning slow and steady or spending it all in one hard burst — cruising or sprinting, the long aerobic draw or the fast anaerobic spend — you don't pick that moment to moment. Something below the floor reads where the body is and sets it, and you learn you've done it about the time it's done. A great deal of the lineage's old regulating got folded down into that floor, taken off the part of the animal that pays attention and run automatic, and the folding cost the animal nothing. It freed the attending part for the harder problems."

"It reads a state and sets a mode," Marge said. "That's the whole of the transaction. There is no one down there who feels worn out and decides to ease off — the body runs hot or runs slow off a number it never shows anybody, and the animal the body is keeping alive is the last thing to find out."

His eyes were shut by now, which was the room's old signal, and the deep time started down its slope the way it always did with him—

—and Bart's mouth opened on reflex, five quarters of reflex, to say the thing the room always said. And shut. Because there was nowhere to send him this time. The slope ran downhill toward the one morning the man actually cared about, the way it ran every quarter, except this quarter the morning was the clade. The plane he was forever trying to land was the runway.

"Go on," Bart said instead, and it came out stranger than he meant it, a man holding open a door he'd spent a year leaning shut. "It's your runway. Land it."

And Burns landed it. The Devonian, he said — three hundred and seventy-five million years back, the air gone thick and wet, the first real forests just standing up, the shallow water at the edge of the land warm and low and going stale of its oxygen. And in that bad warm water a fish with stout lobed fins and a flat crocodile head, and one day — not one day, across a million years of one-days — it props itself up on those fins in the shallows and gets its eyes up over the surface into the air, where nothing of its kind had ever breathed, and the fins that did the propping have, folded up inside them, the bones you are holding your beer with. A wrist. The first fingers. He had a piece of that wrist in a drawer back at the warehouse, knocked out of Ellesmere rock a thousand miles from the nearest road, and he had spent his one life on it, and he did not say so, because he was wearing it lettered across his chest as a joke and the joke said it for him.

"No grandpa," Mira said from the floor, reading her own shirt back at him. "Go back."

"Never," Burns said, delighted, and drank — the first beer of a night he plainly meant to enjoy.

## The Bramblogues

All evening the room had held two animals at once, and the tape, played back, still slid between them without a seam — the vertebrate that had taken half a billion years to learn what worked, and the boxy thing on the floor by Jasmine's chair that had been taught the same trick across a single summer. The clade block had been about the first one. Lisa capped it and turned the room to the second. "Bramble block," she said, and, because Bart already had the card half-turned in his hand and the look of a boy who'd done the reading: "Bart."

He stood. It was still a part he liked.

"The Bramblogues," he said — it was a franchise now, and he said it like one — and Marge made the sound she made, smaller this quarter, a woman who had decided some words you simply outlast. He picked the tetrapod up off the slate; it stopped the instant its feet left the floor, the gait with nothing under it, and turned its head to the camera so Cheryl would get the look. The face was the same kludge it had been since spring — the mismatched eyes, the off-center nose, the ear cones at their two heights — with two things added. Low on the front hung a thin gripper where a jaw would go, a set of jaws that made the crooked face crookeder and let the thing, for the first time, take hold of something on purpose. And the word on its forehead had grown a verb. In spring the screen had named the one thing it looked at, a noun and nothing more — CAT, MIRA, the camera. Now, with the coping dial set forward, it said what it was doing to the thing, and while Bart held it up — forward all afternoon, because Mira had had it — the band above the eyes read WATCH CAMERA. A small clutch of bracket-and-line faces had turned up in the corner of the screen too, little moods Bart had drawn in for the fun of it, that the household read more into than he'd put there.

"Last quarter," Bart said, "this exact thing brained that exact camera. Walked its head into the lens, full speed, and the only move we had to answer it with was to teach it to hate the camera — sour it on the sight. Burns did it, right there on the tape: thumb-down, word goes dark, it turns away. That was the whole of what a thumb could reach. The looking." He set it down a few feet off, facing half away, and it did what it had always done: found the red glow, swung the whole body around to square its head to it, and committed — CHASE CAMERA, head-first, full speed — and as it committed Bart pressed the thumb-down on its head the instant it went, on the going and not on the camera it was going at, and the thing pulled up short of the lens, three feet out, and stood. The screen flickered off CHASE and came back WATCH CAMERA.

"There," Bart said, the single sharp exhale. "Watch the face. It still wants the thing — still pointed dead at it, still lit up about it, the good-and-worked-up running same as ever. The wanting didn't move. The doing did. Last quarter the thumb reached the looking; this quarter it reaches the act, and the act got cheaper, so it does less of it. CHASE went to WATCH and nobody touched the wanting. And the screen never shows you that part anyway — can't; there's no word up there for it." He reached over and turned the coping dial to held-back — it had been set forward all afternoon, Mira's doing — and the verb fell off the word: WATCH CAMERA went to CAMERA, the bare noun, the way it had read in spring before it knew a verb at all. The body didn't move; only the screen did. "Forward, it tells you what it's doing to the thing — chasing it, watching it. Back" — he left the dial where it sat a beat — "it drops the doing and just names the thing it's stuck on. Same machine, same want, same lens; the screen quits narrating and goes back to pointing." He turned it forward, and CAMERA grew its verb again. "And that's your cheap tell — nobody has to go read the dial, it's right there on its face. Verb up, it's pushy; bare word, it's hanging back. But the part that holds whichever way you turn it: forward or back, doing or just-looking, there's no wanting on it anywhere. The thumb moves the doing, the dial hides the doing, and neither one ever lays a finger on the want or shows it. The face tells you what it's up to, or what it's fixed on, and not once what it's after. That's the truest thing the screen does." He crouched, pleased with the machine and with himself. "That's the whole quarter in one move. The thumb reaches the doing now."

"And the ones that stick stop being decisions. Train a thing in deep enough and it quits working it out fresh each time — the paper's second nature now, it doesn't think the paper, it just runs the paper. Frees it up to mind other things. Like —"

Like the thing it did then, which was break off. Mid-room, with the camera right there to want, it came off the light and started across the floor, slow, unhurried, toward the cold end of the bench where Marge's kettle sat on its ring throwing the one patch of warmth in the warehouse, and laid itself flat a foot from the heat, belly to the gap, and went still. The body went still; the face did not. The word had swapped off the camera as it crossed — IGNORE WORLD — and now, settled against the warmth, it read CHARGE UNIT, and stayed lit, narrating its own rest to a room it had quit attending to.

"—that," Bart said, pointing. "Nobody sent it. It read its own empty and broke off a thing it wanted to go feed, off its own clock, before any of us clocked it was low. It's had a sense of its own gauge since the legs; what's new is the gauge gets a vote in what it does now. Reads like a gut telling it to eat. It isn't a gut. It's a number about itself it finally gets to act on." He nodded at the slow amble it had taken to get there. "And it didn't bolt for it. Ambled — slow, cheap — because it's low, and slow's cheaper than a burst. It picks slow draw or burst off its own state now. No dial. That used to be a knob; it runs it itself." He pointed again, at the face this time. "And it's still talking while it lies there — CHARGE UNIT, doing nothing you'd call doing. That's the dial, not the gauge. Forward, even the resting's a move, and it'll narrate it. Flip it back to hangs-back and the word goes dark the second it settles, the way it did all last spring — a thing at rest with nothing to watch and nothing to say. Forward, the lights stay on."

Marge looked at the thing laid out at the warm edge of her kettle the way you'd look at a cat that had chosen your lap, and said nothing, and moved the kettle an inch farther off, and that was all she let the tape have.

Then Bart turned on Mira, which was the other half of the bit.

"Quality control. I made five calls in the log; you watched it all quarter. Yes or no, no speeches. One: it learns a new chore off the thumbs. The paper, off the step, every morning. Did it or didn't it."

Mira set her juice down. "It brings the paper." A beat. "Even when it's raining."

"Taking the yes. Two: it drops a habit. Quits knocking stuff over."

"It quit the shoes."

"...I said knocking stuff over."

"It still knocks stuff over."

The room laughed; Bart kept his books. "It dropped a habit. Taking the yes, and not proud of how." He moved on. "Three: it feeds itself off its own empty, nobody sending it."

Mira looked at the thing lying by the kettle. "It's doing it right now."

"It's doing it right now. Three for three. Four —" and this was the one he wanted clean "— the two of them share the house. Big one, little one. Nobody messing with anybody."

Mira gave it the seriousness she gave the questions that earned it. "It pushed the vacuum's button."

Bart stopped. "It what."

"It bumped into the vacuum and the button went down and the vacuum changed. Once." She picked her juice back up. "It didn't do it again."

"It didn't do it again," Lisa said, fast, because that was the part that mattered to her — a thing the tetrapod had done to the other unit exactly once, that paid it nothing, that it built no habit out of because nothing in there was keeping score on the vacuum at all. "It's not running the vacuum. It bumped a thing, the thing changed, that was the end of it." But the call was the call. "That's your no, Bart."

"That's my no," he said, and didn't pretend it wasn't. "One in five." He looked at Mira for the last one, lighter. "Five. You can tell across the room which way it's set. Pushy, or hangs-back."

"Pushy walks into stuff faster," Mira said. "Hangs-back waits to see."

Which was the whole of Coping Style read off a five-year-old in nine words, and Bart took it and sat, four for five and pleased about the four. Lisa let it settle a beat. "Bramble block," she said. "Jasmine."

## House-Trained

Jasmine took her beat the way she always did, because the table wanted findings and what she had was eleven more weeks of a dog-sized thing loose in her kitchen.

"Plain things first. The big one this quarter is that we teach it now. It used to be you'd thumb it and all that happened was it went off the sight of a thing — you could turn it against something it was looking at, and that was the whole of it. Now you thumb it and the thing it was *doing* changes. So we've all started teaching it, without anybody deciding to. Mira most. She thumbs it through the morning like it's homework."

She turned the bottle a quarter and didn't drink. "The shoes stopped. It used to take a shoe off by the door and carry it under the table, and we thumbed that down about three days and now it leaves them, and I can keep my shoes where shoes go again, like a person lives there. And the paper comes — off the step, every morning, whether I wanted the paper or not. It took the chore on faster than it ever gave up the shoes, because it turns out teaching a thing to start is easier than teaching it to quit."

"And the new dial," she said — "the pushy-or-hanging-back one — we're at that one all day. Mira keeps it pushy: pushy, it's into everything, all over the house, the face going a mile a minute telling you what it's at, CHASE CAT, NUDGE MIRA, and she'll sit and play it like that for an hour. I put it back to hanging-back when I've had enough of it underfoot — hanging-back it slows down, hangs at the edge of a room and watches, and the face quits the chatter and just names the one thing it's looking at. You can tell across the kitchen which way it's set without going to look: words moving, Mira's had it last; one word sitting still, that's me. It goes back and forth between us all day, depending who got to the knob last."

She set the bottle down. "Here's the one I keep sitting with, though. It's started to look like it's trying. You point it at a thing with the thumbs, and the next morning it goes at the thing a little readier, a little surer — like it wanted to get it right, and is pleased with itself when it does. And I know the answer, Lisa, I've had the answer four times: it's a number climbing, and the number isn't pleased about anything. But I've spent eleven weeks watching it learn my house from a kitchen chair, and from the chair it does not look like a number climbing. It looks like something bringing itself up."

The narrator noted that no one corrected her, and noted why: the woman in the chair had watched more minds come into the world than anyone at that table, with her own two hands, in rooms where some of them came and some did not, and if she of all of them read too much life into the thing on the floor, she had earned the direction of the error. She was reading more into it than was in it. She was also the only one who had been watching the whole time.

"The readier-and-surer is the board," Lisa said, not unkindly. "It does more of what paid, and it does it cleaner each pass — that's the whole of the 'trying.' Hold that one. It's the knob's, not the thing's."

"I'll hold it." Jasmine was mild, no edge on it. "I'm not telling you it's pleased. I'm telling you what it looks like from the chair, and that the chair is where it lives."

"And there's still two of them, except they've stopped being the same question." She tipped her head toward the valley, the house across it, the old one running its rounds on the kitchen floor that minute. "The cleaner stays the cleaner. Does its hall, docks, runs the loop it's run two years, never picks up a new trick because it was never built to want one. This one learns something every week. They came off Bart's cable the same morning, the same thing twice, and now nobody under our roof would mix them up for a breath. Mira doesn't even call the old one Bramble now." She let it sit. "The name came off it while none of us were looking. It just kept doing its job, and the name got up and walked over to the one that was changing."

Mira surfaced. "It likes the warm square by the stove," she said, to the room, to no one. "And the sun by the big window after lunch. And my doorway — not inside, the doorway. It does the warm square in the morning when it's slow, and the sun when it's quick, and my doorway when it's done for the day." She had the spots in an order, the way you carry a friend's habits. "I always know where it'll be."

"That's the cold and the warm and the clock," Lisa began. "That's the—"

"I know which it is," Mira said, flat, with her mother's exact mildness, a child who had heard the knob named over her head her whole short life. And she went back to her juice, and the table sat for a second with the thing it could not place: that the kid knew precisely which dial made the warm square and the sun and the doorway, and knew the spots anyway, by heart, the way you know a friend's — and that the two facts, which sat so badly with everyone older than her, did not trouble her at all.

Jasmine added the one thing the spots ran on that Mira hadn't. "It's been on the close setting all quarter — the homebody one, the dial that keeps it working a small patch over and over instead of striking out. So the spots are the spots. It runs the same forty feet of house till it knows them cold, and Mira's right, you could set your watch by where it'll be." She said it plainly, a finding like the others. She did not say what the room would learn in twenty minutes — that she had a reason to be grateful the dial had spent the quarter where it spent it, and a story about the one afternoon it hadn't.

## Down in It

Lisa took the room forward. "Next quarter. No new body — the legs stay; the line won't turn over for a while yet. Bart."

Bart had nothing to build this time and was plainly glad of it. "Nothing much from me. Maybe a few more words on the face. It's Lisa's slot — her knob, her board. I'm mostly watching."

"It's the next knob." Lisa didn't draw it; it was a pitch, not a build. "Slot three. Right now the thing takes the world in one way — flat, even, everything weighted the same. The knob lets that vary. Down in a thing — all of it close and loud, the edges gone, nothing else in the room — or stood back from it, the whole field at once, nothing pulling harder than anything else. Immersed, or surveying. We're calling it Experiencing Style, and it installs the next drive up the board — Autonomy, the modeling layer running more under its own steam. That's all you get tonight; the rest is next quarter's."

Burns was a glass or two past where the evening had started, well into the part of the night that belonged to him, and he came up out of his chair at *Experiencing Style* as though it had said his name. "So next quarter," he said, too loud for the room and delighted about it, "next quarter we get to set what it's *like* in there. What it's like to be the—"

## The Long Way Round

Lisa had said slot three and Experiencing Style and next quarter, and was most of the way to closing the build out, when Jasmine sat up straighter than the findings had wanted her to.

"Wait. Slot three. Something comes off for that to go on?" There was a thing in her voice the meeting hadn't heard from her all night, a hope she was not bothering to dress up. "Please. Tell me it's the foraging one. Tell me the wander dial is what we're losing."

"Tonight, actually," Lisa said. "Foraging comes off at the ceremony in a few minutes; Experiencing Style installs over the swap weekend after." A small pause while she watched Jasmine's face do something. "Why the wander dial, of all four?"

And Jasmine, who had carried it eleven weeks and clearly meant to carry it to the grave, looked around a table that was already leaning in, and gave it up.

"Because of the afternoon it got out."

Bart set his glass down. He had the look he got when he could already tell a thing was going to be good and was settling in to enjoy being right about it.

"It's on the close setting now. Has been since June — the homebody one, works its little patch, you always know where it is. But back in June, before any of us had the hang of which dial was which, Mira had it on the other one. The far one. The follow-your-nose one." Jasmine took a breath that had some old adrenaline still in it. "And she let it out the front."

"I opened the gate," Mira said, from the floor, not as a confession. As a credit.

"She opened the gate. Four years old—"

"Almost five."

"—four years old, and she decides the thing should see the yard, and there is no adult between that decision and the gate, because the adult is me and I am inside getting a stitch out of her sock." Jasmine had the table now and knew it. "And a machine on the wander setting does not see a yard. It sees the edge of a yard, and past the edge a whole street it has never once been allowed to model, and it puts its nose down and it goes."

Marge made a sound. Everyone turned, because it was the wrong sound — it was a laugh, low and surprised out of her, the first uncomplicated thing she'd let off all night, and she put the back of her hand to her mouth as if to catch it and missed.

"It made the Petersons' first," Jasmine said, encouraged now, helpless to it. "Which would have been fine — the Petersons are away — except the Petersons have a dog, and the dog was not away, the dog was on a run-line in the yard, and so the first thing my daughter's science project does loose in the world is walk up to an actual dog and stand four feet off it reading—" she had to stop "—reading SMELL DOG. On its forehead. In daylight. For the whole street."

Lisa had her hand over her eyes.

"The dog lost its mind. Bramble did not. That's the part — the dog is going up and down the run-line like the end of the world, and Bramble just stands there, very polite, SMELL DOG, then SMELL DOG, then it gets bored of the dog and the word changes to SMELL GRILL, because it has caught a better thread, and off it goes — through the Petersons', under the Hwangs' gate, and the Hwangs are having people over."

"Oh no," said Marge, delighted, fully in it now.

"Twelve people in a backyard on a Saturday," Jasmine said, "and a dog-sized thing nobody's ever seen comes through the side gate at a trot with a screen on its head, and it has gone from SMELL GRILL to—" she closed her eyes "—to NEXT TO MEAT. It had found the table. It was reading its own intentions to a stranger's barbecue. NEXT TO MEAT."

Burns was not even forming words anymore. Burns was just producing sound, a wet helpless wheeze every few seconds, one hand flat on the table, the other up in the air asking the story to stop and not meaning it.

"And where am I in all this," Jasmine went on, because she'd found the rhythm and the rhythm had her, "I am three houses back in my socks, following the one clue I have, which is the sound of a dog completely losing its faith in the world, and then that stops, and now I have nothing, now my kid's machine is loose in a neighborhood and silent, and I have about ninety seconds where I genuinely do not know if it's been stolen or hit a car or walked into someone's pool — that part wasn't funny, I want that on the record, that part I will feel in my body until I die—"

"It wasn't in the pool," Mira supplied, serenely.

"—it was not in the pool, thank you, it was standing in the middle of a stranger's barbecue being offered a hot dog by a six-year-old who had decided it was a robot dog and was therefore hers now, and the screen the whole time, the whole time, just narrating—" Jasmine gave up and let the table have it.

"What did it say," Bart got out. "When you found it. What was on the screen."

Jasmine wiped her eyes. "HELLO STRANGER."

The room came apart. Marge had both hands over her face and her shoulders going. Burns made a noise like a man going under for the third time. Lisa, who had run the meeting clean for two hours, who did not leak, said into her own palm, with feeling, "I built that screen," in the voice of a woman watching her own work testify against her in open court.

And Mira, who had been waiting through the whole thing with the patience of a person holding the actual best part, said: "The little girl cried when we took it back."

Which finished them. Because of course she did — of course there was a child three streets over who had met the thing for ninety seconds and grieved it — and only Mira, who had not been frightened for one instant of the whole afternoon, who had spent it watching instead of panicking, had thought to keep that detail, the one about somebody else's heart, while every adult in the chase kept only the fear.

"So that," Jasmine said, when she could, wiping her face with the flat of her hand, "is why, when this one said something's coming off the board, my whole soul said let it be the wander dial." She shook her head. "It's the close setting now. Forever, if I get a vote. It can know its forty feet of house. It does not need the street."

"It liked the street," Mira said quietly, to her juice, and did not push it, and let her mother have the ending she wanted, which was its own small mercy and went unremarked.

## The Shelf

The shelf came back to the table at the end of the household talk — the foot of pine Bart had built last spring, sanded and waxed, the row of shallow grooves cut along it. One of the grooves held the stub of the breathing knob from the March meeting, where it had sat since; the rest were still waiting. He set the shelf on the bench between them and stepped back, because the lifting was Lisa's tonight.

Lisa tipped the tetrapod up off the slate — it went still the moment its feet left the floor, patient as a watch laid open on a cloth — and turned its back to the room and worked open slot three with a thumbnail. Mira picked it up and turned it in her fingers — the wander dial, the cause of the afternoon her mother had spent eleven weeks not telling, warm now and finished. She looked over at the tetrapod, back on its four feet without complaint, and she got there before any of them.

Which was the whole of it — the thing the grown-ups had spent an hour circling, that a job can come off the board and not come out of the body — said plainly, and again, by the only one in the room small enough to find it obvious twice in a row. Burns had been two breaths from telling them all how old and how large the moment was, his glass already on the way up; he put it back down. ## Off the Board

Lisa took the marker, but the picture she wanted was already up — the four circles from the spring — and what she had to say tonight wasn't about the circles. It was about the board: the real one, on the tetrapod's back, four slots, that Bart had been losing sleep over since last June. "We pulled a knob off this spring," she said, "and the board didn't grow. We pulled another off tonight, and it isn't going to grow at the next swap either. I want that on the tape, because he's been bracing for the opposite all year."

He had — twelve clades coming and four slots to hold them, the dread that they'd fill one a quarter until there was nowhere to put the next thing and the whole build stalled for want of room.

"And the fix wasn't a bigger board. A knob comes off, and it makes the room for the next one." She set the marker down on it. "The breathing knob — which store it spends, the slow one for the long haul or the fast one for the sprint — used to be a thing a hand set. Not anymore. It reads its own state well enough to set it itself, no dial, the way you don't sit down and decide to breathe. And the foraging knob will go the same way next quarter — explore or exploit chosen off the unit's own state, folded down underneath. That's why I had Mira shelve it tonight, ahead of the install."

She said the next part in the flat voice she kept for the load-bearing ones. "That's not the knob breaking. That's the knob being finished. You get good enough at a thing, you quit having to decide it — it drops under the board and runs itself, and the room it leaves is exactly the room you were short." She put the two facts side by side, because they were one fact. "Which is the whole pattern, said as a board instead of a card. The word I inked in the Regulator this spring and the slot it freed are the same thing twice — the drive to get good at your own moves, and a move gotten good enough to come off the board. Mastery, and the room it made. And next quarter the same trick again, one slot over — Autonomy, into the room the foraging knob leaves open tonight."

"The old line couldn't have done it. Genes don't get good at anything inside one life — a genetic knob is a setting you're born holding; it stacks, and nothing ever comes back off. Four clades of that, the board filling, and Bart's dread was the honest reading of it. But this line learns. It can take a thing all the way down to automatic, and a thing run automatic doesn't need a slot. You were never going to need a bigger board. You needed to get good enough at one thing to be done with the knob for it."

Burns came in low and pleased, eyes half down, the glass going in its slow circle — riding alongside her for once instead of over the top of her. "Same move the floor pulled," he said: the floor he'd named an hour back, the old regulating folded down under the brain and run blind. "Half a billion years of it. Smaller board's all." It came out a shade thick at the edges, the drink sitting in the words, and he didn't hear it, and nobody marked it but the tape.

Marge was drinking the beer now, two-handed still but drinking it, and when she set it down the careful was gone the way it had been gone all night and what came up under it was harder and a beat slow. "And that's the one to watch. The thing it just took down to automatic is the part with nobody home to run it. Your breath. Your heart. The most finished thing an animal does is the thing it does without anyone inside deciding to." She found the rest through the drink and said it anyway. "So when it folds a job down and quits minding it, don't let mastered slide across into minded. A thing it no longer has to attend to has not started attending to itself. It's one more thing running in a house with the lights off." No one took it up, because drunk or sober it was airtight, and because she'd built it, three deep, out of the listener's own lungs.

"So I don't get the bigger board," Bart said into the quiet, working it out loud the way he worked a part in his hands.

"You needed to be done with a knob." Lisa handed him back his own line off the log, which was as near a victory lap as the two of them ran. She set the marker in the tray. "That's how it climbs without growing. Every drive that comes online, something already learned folds down under it and makes the room. The board stays four. The animal keeps going up." Which was the framework, capped. And down at the warm end of the table, Burns had stopped turning his glass.

## May we Practice

Burns closed the way he closed every quarter, up out of the chair with whatever was in the glass and the room made to wait for it — except the glass had been refilled past the point anyone was still counting, and the room knew, the way you know, that it was getting the unguarded version tonight.

He had spent the animal early. The Devonian, the wrist, the long haul up out of the bad warm water had all come in the body of the meeting, because this once the deep time and the clade were the same morning and there had been nowhere left to send the plane. So he came at the motivation sidelong, the way she had watched him come at a thing when the front door was already used up. The vertebrate's one good trick, he said, was not that it could do a thing — the net could do a thing — but that it could come back the next day and do the thing better. Keep its own books on its own moves, and lean, pass over pass, toward the ones that paid. Not wanting the thing across the room. Wanting to be good at getting to it.

Then the reach, the half-inch past what the sentence could hold. "The one part of you that lets you get better at everything else," he said, "is the one part that came out of the box already done. Never practiced a day in its life. Half a billion years, and nobody's laid a finger on it." He turned it over with the pleasure of a man who has found the seam in a thing — the engine of all that getting-better, itself born finished — and did not see, or saw so far under that it would never surface as a sentence, that he had spent his own one life getting masterful at the furthest-off thing there was and never once at the nearest; and that the nearest thing, the boy who'd built the body cooling on the floor, had that very night held open a door he had spent a year leaning shut, and let him land it, and said nothing about it after.

He got the glass up. "To Mastery," he said, and went for the verb, and the verb was wrong in his mouth before he reached it — you did not master the thing, the thing had no end to master — and what came up out of the drink instead, turned inside out and unnoticed, was the old saw with its two halves swapped: that perfect makes practice. He let it stand. No one was going to be the one to tell a happy drunk he'd flipped it, least of all because flipped it had come out truer than the right way round — there being no perfect to arrive at, only the wanting of it, and the wanting the whole of what kept a thing at the work.

"May we practice," he said, and drank; and they drank with him, two who thought the thing on the floor had someone in it to get better and two who didn't, the four of them to the one word from their four different distances.

And Mira, folded on the floor against her mother with the last of the juice and most of the way to sleep, gave it back the way she kept the ones she meant to keep — careful, in her own voice and not the cartoon's. "May we practice." Then she went heavy, and that was the meeting; and Lisa let the light run a breath past the talk before she reached over and stopped it.

## Jasmine

None of what came after the light went out is on any tape. The camera had done its work and gone dark on the bench, and the last hour of that night lived nowhere but in the one of them sober enough to keep it — and she keeps things, and carries them a long way before she sets them down.

Jasmine got them to the car the way she got a room to a thing it had to do, without seeming to push it: the cards back in the cigar box, Mira gone boneless and slung up onto her shoulder, the two drunks gathered and steered out into the warm August dark. Burns rode up front and talked the whole way — the basal ganglia, the wrist, half a billion years, the words coming easier the drunker he got and meaning less — and she said *mm* in the places that wanted it and drove. Marge rode in back without a word. So she had a car full of people who had spent the night arguing whether there was anyone home in a machine, and the only one of them not theorizing about a mind was the one with both hands on the wheel. There is an adult in every room of clever people. She had been it for years, and was lonelier for loving them than she would ever have let on.

She let Marge off first, at the dark house with no light left on and no one in it to leave one, and Jasmine watched her in the mirror the whole way there, more than the road strictly wanted, and then walked her up — an arm not quite around her, reading her the way she read a room she'd been called into late, how much a person could carry to her own threshold and how much had to be lifted off her at the last. She got her inside. The bag went in with her, the three filled pages still in it that had ridden up the canyon to a white table above the temple weeks before and ridden everywhere since, unmailed, the way they would ride a while yet. Jasmine did not know what the bag held. She knew the shape of a thing a person cannot set down, having stood in too many rooms where one was being carried, and she waited at the curb until a light came on inside before she pulled away.

She had been in the rooms where it happens — the actual coming of a someone into a body, the slow unhurried work of it that no one has ever found the knob for — and in the rooms where the someone never came, where she had washed up after and driven home in the dark exactly like this. She said nothing about any of that, ever, and had said nothing all night while the people she loved took a knob off a board and pleased themselves with having learned that a thing practiced long enough runs on its own. She had spent her one working life on the single arrival that has never run on its own and never will. She drove the last of it home with her husband gone quiet at last beside her and her daughter asleep behind her, the only one awake — which was a thing no board had a slot for, and the surest hand in the car, and on no tape anywhere.

## Reflection: What is Met?

There is a theorem, older than the lab and drier than anything ever said inside it, that holds every good regulator of a thing must be a model of that thing. It sounds like a sentence about thermostats and it is really a sentence about survival. To keep yourself going in a world is to carry that world around inside you — not a picture of it, a working copy, the parts that bear on staying alive bent into the shape of whatever it was that kept you. A lineage that has lasted is not a thing that beat its world. It is a thing that became, slowly and without ever meaning to, an argument about what the world is like. The clam is a theory of tides. The root is a theory of dirt. Nothing in either of them knows it; the knowing was never the point; the body is the knowledge, kept warm and handed down because the only other option was to stop.

There is a second theorem, its twin, that says the copy is only ever as good as it is various — that to hold steady against a world which can come at you a hundred ways you need a hundred answers, and a thing with one answer can survive only a world with one threat. Only variety absorbs variety. The two are a single claim in two coats: you live by modeling your world, and the model has to run at least as rich as the world runs dangerous. Fall behind on either count and the world regulates you instead.

You can watch the claim run away with itself one time in the rock, in the warm Cambrian shallows, the age the animals first took up eating one another. Until then the world a thing had to model was mostly weather — current, light, the chemistry of the mud. Then the world filled with other things that were themselves modeling and answering back, and the variety each animal had to meet stopped being the sea and became the next animal, which got a little better every age, which meant you had to, which meant it did again. An eye buys a faster tail buys a sharper eye, world without end. That is the whole engine of the explosion, and it is only requisite variety with the brakes cut. Steering was one answer the race extorted. Learning was the next — a thing that could add to its own stock of answers inside a single life and not wait on the lineage to mail it one. And because none of this is about flesh — they are theorems; they could not care less what the regulator is built from — the same engine turns in a market, a language, a town, anywhere a thing has to keep its feet in a world that keeps changing the question.

Which is the part worth sitting with, up here at the end of one of these nights, the whole valley gone dark under the window. A person is a regulator too, and becomes, the long way around, a faithful working model of the one world that made them — and then carries that model out into all the rooms the first world never saw. The trouble is almost never too little model. The trouble is a model finished early and finished beautifully, fitted to the exact shape of a water that drained off years ago, held up now against something with far more variety in it than the one good answer can take — another person, reaching back. You can be a flawless regulator of a vanished sea. The ones that came up out of the Cambrian were the ones that never stopped adding answers, and it is the hard trick, much the hardest there is, in the country up at the top of the climb, where the thing you mostly have to meet is each other.
