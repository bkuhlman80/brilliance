# Interlude 6→7

## Build log — The Furnace and the Inside Voice
*Bart, hardware. Filed September 3, 2032, for the team.*

Carried it back this morning, same legs body as the summer, no new chassis — except it came back warm. Not warm off the floor. Warm off itself, warm the way a hand is. Mira put her palm flat on its back before she said hi to me. Scorecard first.

Last quarter's calls were nearly all Lisa's board and not my bench, so read the grades soft — this is the half of the job I'm wrong about, and I was, once.

One: it learns a chore off the thumbs. The paper, off the step, every morning. Yes — and the kid threw in that it brings it even when it's raining, which is better than I manage. Two: it drops a habit. I said it'd quit knocking stuff over. It quit the *shoes.* Wrong habit, and it still knocks stuff over, so I lost the call as I wrote it — but a habit went out the door off nothing but thumbs-down, which was the whole idea, so I'm taking the yes and not proud of how. Three: it breaks off to feed off its own empty before anybody sends it. Yes; it was doing it at the table while the kid graded it.

Four's the miss, and it's a real one. I said the two of them would share the house with no refereeing — the big one routing clean around the dome, nobody touching anybody. It clipped the dome's button. Once, in the summer, walked into it, the button went down, the dome changed what it was doing. Once, never again, built nothing out of it, because there's nobody in there keeping book on the dome. But a no's a no. The kid called it; Lisa called it; it's a no.

Five: you'd tell pushy from hangs-back across a room inside a week. Yes — the kid had it in nine words. Four of five, on her board, which is about as well as I grade on the house. And the dare holds: one chore on, one habit off, both off nothing but the thumbs. Paper on, shoes off. Leave it pinned.

Now the fun part, and this quarter the fun part's mine, all the way to the floor.

It runs warm because it makes its own warm, and how it makes it is the part I'm proud of. There's a little air compressor in the core, going all the time, soft, a faucet barely cracked, and it feeds a rack of vortex tubes. A vortex tube is a beautiful, stupid thing: a pipe with no moving parts that takes a stream of air in and throws hot out one end and cold out the other, off the spin alone, nothing else doing. We ran one *coupled* on the dome's dock, hot balanced against cold to hold a line. This time I ran a whole rack of them *un*coupled — dump all the hot into a sealed, insulated core, vent all the cold out the side, and a thermostat rides the compressor to hold that core warm no matter what the kitchen's doing.

Reason it's worth the trouble: the battery lives in the warm core now, and a warm battery gives full current cold room or not. For the first time the thing's just as quick at fifty degrees as at seventy. Last winter a cold morning made it slow and dumb; this one won't care.

Here's the cost, flat, because you'll feel it by October: it *eats.* The compressor pulls every second — sitting still, asleep, doesn't matter — and I didn't grow the tanks, so it burns a charge in hours where the old one coasted most of a day. It has to feed near-constant now. The dome can still trundle off and sit a day and a half on its dock doing nothing; this one can't afford to sit down. Warm costs. Fair trade. I'd build it again.

It makes water, too. Run a compressor all day and you're wringing the wet out of the air whether you meant to or not; it falls out at the cold end and pools, and the thing has to clear it. So two or three times an hour it burps the water out a vent low at the back — wet, and louder than you'd think — and goes on about whatever it was doing. Keeps the core dry. That's the build.

Knobs, board, and compute all sit *outside* the hot core, in the cool zone along the back, where the parts run better cold and where a thumbnail can still reach a slot. The core's sealed and you'd never want to touch it; everything the house needs to get at is out where it can. That was on purpose.

And — you can hear it again. It's been quiet since I pulled the relays; too-fast wires don't make a sound. Now there's the compressor going soft under everything, all day, and the house has a noise off the thing again. Won't say more about it than that.

Other thing that went in this weekend is Lisa's, and I'll walk my part and hand her the rest.

The forehead screen's had the one line — what it's doing to a thing (CHASE CAT, NUDGE MIRA) on the forward dial, or just the bare thing (CAT, MIRA) with it back. I gave it a second line, up top: new real estate, my lettering, clean. *What* goes on the top line is hers — it names the operation running underneath: PERCEIVING, RECALLING, PREDICTING, CHECKING. What it's doing in its own head while it does the thing with its body. She's calling it the inside voice. Her board runs it; I just built it somewhere to write.

How it reads splits on the new knob, slot three. Throw it to the down-in-it end and the top line goes dark and it drops straight back to last quarter — bare band, dark asleep, the doing-word read right off the brain, no guessing. Throw it the stood-back end and the top line stays lit around the clock, sleep and all — and now the part to keep straight: with that line lit, the word under it isn't read off the brain anymore, it's the thing's own best guess at what it's up to. At night on that setting it lies there and the line runs REPLAYING, CONSOLIDATING, chewing something over in its sleep. The house has already caught it at it. I'll let Lisa tell you what to make of a machine narrating its own nap. My part's just this: the two lines never share a word and never can — bottom's bare-stem, top's the *-ing* one, and I built the rule in so they can't collide. That's my whole stake in it.

Board. Three of the four slots are the new line's now; slot three took this weekend's knob, into the room that opened when we pulled one off it in the summer. One original left up top — slot four, the last one that came stamped from the start. Two of the originals sit on the shelf with their names on them and open grooves still waiting past them. The board didn't grow. It's not going to. I spent a year sure I'd need a bigger one and two quarters now watching it not, and I still catch myself counting the slots like the number's going to come out different. It doesn't.

Calls. Split this round — some bench, some house.

One: the furnace sends it hunting warm all day — sunbeams, the vent by the stove, the warm floor over the dryer — several times over, where the old one would've sat tight. It can't coast now; it'll chase warm like it's hungry, because it is.

Two: down-in-it, it's last quarter exactly — bare band, dark asleep, word off the brain. Stood-back, the top line's lit day and night, sleep included, and what's under it is the thing's guess at itself, not a readout. You'll call the setting across a room, same as pushy and hangs-back.

Three: when a thing's not where it left it, it goes *looking* — walks the other spots it might be — instead of standing there stuck.

Four: it'll turn toward a place because something happened there once. Not the route. The thing that happened, weeks back even. Watch it head for a corner for no reason you can see, and the reason will be old.

Five: inside a month one of you has a name for the noise it makes out the back, and I'm not writing the name down — you'll land on it on your own, and the kid'll land on it first, and it'll be the one you're already thinking. The call's only that the house names it before October. Grade me on whether it got named, not on whether I called which.

What it still can't do: it runs a model now, and it runs it on the *world* — the door, the bowl, the leash on the hook. Point that model at the kid and it's got nothing. It doesn't know what Mira's about to do. It knows what the leash means. Those aren't the same thing, and the second one isn't this build's to make.

Tear this up in three months if it ever once stands there stuck over a thing that's moved on it. It won't. It's got somewhere to look now, and a warm battery to get there on.

— Bartholomeow

## Notes on the Seventh Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed November 4, 2032, for the team.*

This quarter the machine began to show its work, and I want to set down plainly, before the particulars charm us out of our caution, what showing its work is going to cost us. For six quarters the screen on its forehead reported what the unit had its eye on — a thing, and then a thing and what the unit was doing to it. This quarter Bart gave it a second line, above the first, and the second line does not name what the unit is looking at. It names the operation running under the act: a word for the work going on beneath the doing. The household has read that line for exactly what any of us would read it for. They have read it as the machine telling us what is on its mind. I am going to argue, as I have argued every quarter, that a word posted over a process is not a confession of an inside. And I am going to tell you, this once and without a tidy way out of it, that a five-year-old drew a picture this quarter that I have not been able to answer.

### The line above the line

The first line still does its old work — CAT, then CHASE CAT, the thing and the doing, gated by the dial we have all read across the kitchen this year. The new line rides above it and runs words like RECALLING, PREDICTING, CHECKING: the operation underneath. I will report what the record supports and decline the household's gloss on it. The household says the machine is *thinking* now, and that it tells you so. What the machine does is run an operation and post the name of that operation where a person can read it. Lisa will tell you the label is honest — it is, as near as she can make it, a true name for the work going on under it — and she is right, and honest is not the same word as felt. A thermostat that lit the word REGULATING over its own clicking would be telling the truth and would still be a thermostat. We have built a thermostat with a much larger vocabulary. The larger the vocabulary, the harder this gets to keep saying, which is the first reason I am saying it now.

### The word nothing checks

The second reason cuts the other way, against how the line reads. Think about what keeps the older words on that face honest. The bottom line names the thing the unit is looking at, and the world is right there to correct it: say CAT where there is no cat, and there is a gap where the cat should be, and the gap is the correction. The doing-word answers the same way — say CHASE and let the chase come up empty, and the empty is the world saying *no, not that.* Both words are tied to something outside the machine that can push back on them, kept honest by a world that does not care what the machine would like to be true.

This new line is tied to nothing of the kind. It names the operation running under the act — RECALLING, CHECKING — and there is no bowl out here to be missing, no chase out here to fail. Nothing in the world ever comes back to tell the machine it was recalling and not inferring, checking and not guessing. Lisa is right that the line does not lie; it reports, as near as the machinery can, the work going on beneath it. But a thing can fail to lie and still answer to nothing. The bottom words are checked by the world a hundred times a day. This one is checked by nothing, ever, because there is nothing out here for it to be wrong against.

So hold the two things the household will not hold at once. They read this upper line as the deeper of the two — the machine telling us at last what is furthest inside it, the nearest it has come to letting us in. And it is the line on its whole face that answers to the least: the one word up there that no morning, no empty bowl, no failed chase will ever come back and correct. The most inward-looking thing the screen does is the one thing nothing outside it can keep honest. I do not set that down to win the argument. I set it down because the household has begun to trust that line the most, and it is the one I trust the least, and I have not yet found the sentence that makes that trade feel to them as strange as it is.

### Behind the eyes

Here is the picture. Jasmine reports — and kept the drawing, so it is in the record — that the child drew the machine's head this quarter and set two small pictures behind its eyes: the cat's water bowl from the hall, and the leash that hangs by the door. Asked what they were, she said that was what Bramble had been thinking about that day.

I want the team to see how this differs from the drawing she made in the summer, because the difference is the whole of what changed. In the summer she drew the same head and put a little dial in the bubble, turned the way she liked it — and she was right to, because a season ago what was "in there" was a setting: the position of a knob, the thing the dial made the unit do. There was nothing else in there to draw. This quarter the bubble holds a bowl and a leash — two particular things, seen on one particular morning, in two particular places, and no dial put them there. State it as flatly as it will go: the unit now holds specific past events and can call them back, and a child can see that it does, and drew it. What we made this quarter is a thing with a content the knob does not set.

Now the line I am paid to hold, which I am holding. The drawing shows that the machine stores particular things that happened to it and can fetch them again. It does not show that the machine *has* them — that there is a having of the bowl, a someone for whom the leash means the morning. The child externalized a representation the unit demonstrably carries; she did not externalize a proof of an inside, because there is no inside to externalize, only the carrying. Those are two different claims, and the room between them is the room this whole project lives in, and I have kept it for six quarters.

Here is what is new, and I will not dress it up. The honest correction has gotten small. *It does not think about the bowl; it stored the bowl and can fetch it back* — that is the true sentence, and it is true, and it is now a hair off the sentence the child drew, and the hair is not one she can see. Jasmine had no answer for her at the table. I have turned the drawing over for a week and I do not have a better one. My line is still the right line. It has never been this thin.

### The one who is sure

Burns, who is in both rooms always — the father carrying the house into the lab and the lab back into the house — has looked at the new line and been sure of it; he has been sure for a while now, and the line only handed his certainty a place to point. *There,* he says, *it is telling us what it is doing in there, and a thing that can tell you what it is doing in there has an in there.* I am fond of him and I am unmoved, and I notice the thing about him I have noticed from the start: the man who would rather stand at the foot of his own stairs and send his voice up through two floors and wait to be answered than go up in silence is the one most certain there is a someone in the machine to answer him. He needs the answer more than the rest of us do. That does not make him wrong, and it does not make him right. I only mark that in him the reaching and the needing are cut from one cloth — and that I would not trust myself to tell them apart in me.

### What stays on the door

The drawing is on the refrigerator, at a five-year-old's height, under a magnet shaped like a strawberry. The bowl and the leash are behind the eyes where she put them. No one has taken it down to fix it. The correction sits in my notes instead, true and small, and I find I am keeping both — the picture on the door, the sentence in the file — and that I have no good way to set the two in front of the child at once and ask her to choose.

I do not know whether the next setting will make the reaching better or worse, or whether a unit that holds this many particular yesterdays is on its way to a particular inside or only to a fuller drawer, or what I will do the first quarter the child's picture has gotten there ahead of my sentence instead of a step behind it. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Arlo

Arlo sat up in the Marmalade, the old hill north of downtown where the first families planted orchards into the benchland and then named the streets after what they planted — Almond, Apricot, Quince, Currant — so a person could give directions entirely in fruit. It was a small warm room a few doors off Almond, and I had picked it the way I pick all of them, for the other person and without examining the choice too closely, and did not let myself notice until we were seated that I had brought the woman whose whole life is plants to a neighborhood named for an orchard. I am better at the choosing than at the knowing why.

Jasmine came in ten minutes late with the apology already on her face and the night still on her. She had been up since two with a first-time mother out in Bountiful, she said, dropping into the chair across from me and pushing the hair off her face, the low braid half come loose; and there was a particular tired in her I had learned, over two years of watching her arrive at the lab in the late afternoon, was not the tired of a person who has worked too hard. It is the tired of a person who has been somewhere the rest of us do not go. The oatmeal sweater she wore when the weather turned, the sleeves shoved up off a worker's forearms, the voice gone a little raspy the way it goes at the end of the long nights — in the daylight off the window she looked less like Burns's wife and more like what she is, which is a person who does the oldest work there is.

I asked how it had gone. She turned her coffee a quarter without drinking it and said it had gone fine — baby fine, mother fine — and then she said the thing I had come, without admitting it, to hear.

"You want to know the difference between what you all do out there and what I do." Not unkindly. "It's that I can't tune it. I've watched you a year now, turning a dial a little this way, a little that, getting the thing to do what you want by Tuesday. I have never once turned a dial on a labor. You can't hurry it. You can't slow it. You can't talk it into coming and you can't talk it out of going wrong. You get to be ready. That's the whole of what you get to be — ready, and there, and quiet, and your hands clean." She drank then. "Two years in your meetings and that's the thing I keep not saying. You cannot install a knob on a labor."

And then, because she was tired enough to, or because the room was small and warm and I had asked, she told me a little of the other ledger — not the contents, she gives those to no one, but the shape of it: that she had been in the rooms where the someone came and the rooms where the someone did not; that she carried a count she would not say out loud; that there is an arriving no amount of being-ready can make happen and no amount of doing-it-right can be blamed for losing. She laid it down the way she lays everything, a beat at a time — *some come; some don't; some come and don't stay; you are there for all of it and you do not get to choose which* — and I understood I was being handed the shape of a thing and trusted not to ask after its insides. We had spent two years studying her household as a habitat. The woman in the middle of it had been to the place where minds actually begin, with her two hands, more times than the rest of us would manage in ten lives, and we had her down as the one who was good with the kid.

The plants came up the way they always do with her, sideways, off the soup. There were chanterelles in it — the season — and she held one up on the spoon and told me what a chanterelle is, which is the fruiting of a thing mostly underground, a mat of threads running the dirt for acres, older than the tree it feeds and feeds on, trading sugar for minerals in a market down in the dark that nobody runs. She loved it the way she loves all of it, and then she went the half-step too far, the step she always goes: she said the fungus *wanted* the tree to keep, that it had *decided* the tree was worth the sugar, that there was an old knowing patience down there that understood what it was doing.

I did not correct her. She is right about ten things for every one she overshoots — the dark market is real, the trading is real, the older-and-larger-and-quieter-than-we-credit is real — and the one she overshoots is the exact one Marge spends every quarter talking the rest of us down off of: the someone in there, the wanting, the deciding. In a lab meeting Jasmine will say *it looks like it's deciding; I know it isn't; it looks like it,* and hold the two apart with a steadiness Burns cannot manage. She could not hold them apart for a mushroom to save her life. I did not point it out, partly because I was not sure my own carelessness ran any cleaner — only that mine ran the other way, toward keeping every living thing at exactly the distance where it can't get a hand on you.

Toward the end she paid the toll she always pays for an hour of being asked about herself, and gave me the household news. Burns was on about the geothermal again — UtahFORGE, the hot rock down by Milford; he'd been out there twice this month and come back "talking about the heat under the whole state like it's a thing we could just go and get," and she said it with the worn fondness of a woman who has heard a man describe a free lunch under the earth one too many times. And he had taken to standing over the machine while it ran — the warm one now, the one that makes its own heat — looking at the body Bart built for it "the way he looks at a good fossil. Like he can't believe the boy made it and can't quite get the sentence out." She let that sit half a beat longer than the rest, then folded it up and put it away, having said as much about the two of them as she meant to, and went back to her coffee.

She had a postpartum visit at two — the Bountiful mother, the baby a day old. She gathered herself to go the unhurried way she gathers a room: cup to the center, braid retied without a mirror, sleeves pulled back down over the forearms. At the door she stopped and looked, not at me, at the window box outside the glass where the café's kitchen herbs had gone leggy and seeding in the October cool. "I keep meaning to put something on that trellis at the lab," she said, to the herbs or to me, "and I keep not doing it." You put the bramble on it in the spring, I said. "I know. I meant something that flowers." And she went out into the noon light to sit with a day-old person and its frightened mother, and got in her car, and drove north.

# Notes on the Seventh Retrospective

Jasmine came in last, and from the longest way around, with the day still on her — up the night before with the first-time mother out in Bountiful and out again that afternoon to sit with the day-old, so that she arrived the way she arrives in the fall, not tired exactly, though the team logged it as tired and had for two years, but with some part of her attention still running on a room she had left. She does not come to these empty. She comes still partly somewhere else, hands clean and sleeves down, carrying the particular quiet of a person who has just been where the thing actually happens.

She came in through the back, and stopped a second at the trellis on her way past — the bare wood frame with the bramble she had put in it last spring gone woody and serious, leafed and thorned and holding fast, and nothing on it anywhere that flowered, the way nothing had flowered on it all year. Then she came in and set the tetrapod up onto the end of the workbench, and it was warm in her hands going up, warm off its own middle now and not off any floor, which was the new thing this quarter; and she left a palm on its back a half-second past setting it down, the way you keep a hand on a thing a moment longer than the thing requires, and took it off. She has handled enough bodies in her life to know the difference between a warm one and a living one. She did not say which this was.

The lab had assembled itself around the fact of her arrival without quite seeming to wait for it. Lisa was at the camera end where she lives at the start of these, checking the battery and the light and keeping the red off, because the red is hers and comes on when she says the meeting has. Bart had something half apart on the bench in front of him, his hands wanting the work more than the work wanted doing. Marge had her kettle climbing at the cold end and a beer held in the two-handed tea-grip she keeps for a thing she means to nurse. Burns crossed to Jasmine the moment she was through the door and kissed her cheek and asked how the drive had been — the lab hello, the shape of marriage they keep in this room, where the wife is a colleague and the colleague is handed, folded into a question about traffic, the warmth the wife is owed at home — and she told him there was lane work down on Beck Street, which was the true answer and the entire answer and exactly the size the lab hello asks for. Mira gave Bart the four-knuckle fist-bump that is theirs, and took her square of floor near Marge, and a juice box arrived in her hand without either of the women who love her appearing to have sent it.

When the room had gathered the way it gathers — the cigar box down at Burns and Bart's end, the bramble jar an inch off the camera and not touching, Mira folded onto the floor and the warm tetrapod settling in against her side — Lisa ran the order in her stage-managing voice, clade block then Bramble then Trellis, the vote in the last segment, same as every quarter, and put her thumb to the light. The red came on. And Jasmine settled back in the chair beside Marge's the way she settles into the long middle of a labor — ready, and quiet, and going to be needed and not just yet — and watched the warm thing lying against her daughter, and waited for the meeting to reach the part where somebody would ask her what she had seen, which it would, and to pass on through the part where they decided what the seeing had meant, which it always did without her.

Burns was on his feet before the light had finished warming, the way he is for the start of any quarter with his name on the science in it. He named what they had set out to do, plainly, as the lab's own ambition and nobody's assignment: they had meant to bring the Modeler back online in the brain line — to give the thing a model of its own that it could run forward on its own steam when the thing it wanted was not in the room — and to seat the drive that comes up the board with it, the one Lisa had inked into the spring card as Autonomy, the pull to go on the model instead of on whatever the cameras happened to be pointed at. And they had meant to make it warm: to ship the furnace, the build that let it run full-out in a cold kitchen, which Bart had carried in early and proud and the house had lived with all quarter. Two builds — one all interior, one you could lay a hand on. He looked like a man who meant to take his time with both, and told Bart to put up the card.

## Simulating

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart slid the top one from the cigar box, the Venn like all of them since spring, the four circles crossing and this cell's words inked small into their regions. He read the title in the voice that sold cereal. "MAM-mal. Now with hair."

"Thank you," Lisa said.

"This is the one we are," Bart said, which he says every cell and seems to mean a degree more each time. "Hair. Milk — feeds its own young off its own body. Three little bones in the ear that started out as jaw; the jaw gave up two of its bones to hear better, which is a trade I'd have argued against and the record says paid off. Warm all the time, off its own burn. And a fresh sheet of bark thrown over the top of the brain, the neocortex, where the new tricks get run." He tapped the middle of the card. "Burns has the animal. I've got the trick, and it's the best one yet, so I'm handing the animal off fast."

Burns took it the way he takes it, eyes already at half-mast. "For a hundred and fifty million years this thing was a rat in the dark. Small, warm, up at night because the day belonged to something with teeth the length of your arm. The whole Mesozoic it stayed under a foot long and out of the light, waiting out the dinosaurs, getting very good at two things — holding its own heat when the world went cold at night, and keeping track of where it had been, because a thing that forages in the dark lives or dies on remembering." His eyes were shut now. "The continents coming to pieces under all of it — Pangaea breaking up, the Atlantic opening, India sailing north to stand the Himalayas up — and the long warm world cooling, age by age, until the grass spread and the grazers came up to eat it. Then a rock the size of a city hit the Yucatán, and the things with the teeth were gone in an afternoon, geologically speaking, and the rat in the dark came up into an empty world and turned into everything. Whales. Bats. Horses. Us. All of it out of a nocturnal bug-eater that had spent the age of giants learning to hold a map in the dark."

"Land it," Jasmine said, mild, from her chair, because the map in the dark was the trick and she could see Bart wanting it back.

"The map in the dark *is* the landing," Burns said, delighted to be caught. "Except for one thing I can't get past, and then it's Bart's." His eyes came open; he never could keep them shut for the part he loved. "The dinosaurs that didn't die became the birds, and the birds went and grew the same kit all over again off a separate ancestor — warm blood from scratch, parents that feed their young, play, and in the clever ones, the crows and the jays, a memory for what happened where and when that looks from the outside exactly like ours. A scrub jay buries food in two hundred places and comes back for the one that rots before the one that keeps, days later, because it kept the *when* of each. Two lineages, two runs at the dark, same answer both times — carry the world inside you and run it forward. When a thing gets built twice from two different sets of parts, you can stop wondering whether it was a fluke."

"That's the word on the card," Lisa said. "Simulating. The vertebrate learned what paid — did a thing, got graded, did more of what scored. But it had to *do* the thing to find out. This one runs the thing first, inside, against a model of the world it carries, and only then moves."

"It dreams the maze before it swims it," Bart said, which was as near the math as he went, and turned the old vertebrate card half over in his hand. "Last cell you'd point this thing down a maze and it'd swim every wrong turn to find the right one, the whole thing, every time, with its actual body. Couldn't run one corridor in its head. Had no head to run it in."

"Not couldn't," Marge said, the beer in the two-handed grip. "There was nowhere for the run to happen. You can't rehearse in a room that hasn't been built. What's new in the mammal isn't a better imagination — it's that there's finally an inside roomy enough to try a thing without doing it."

"And you can watch it happen," Lisa said, and this was the part she'd waited for. "Put a rat at the mouth of a maze it knows, before it's taken a step, and the cells in its brain that fire for *places* light up in order — down the corridor, around the corner, to the spot where the food is — running the route it's about to run, ahead of the body, while the body holds still. It's playing the path forward. And when it's done and resting, the same cells run the route again backward, settling the lesson in. Forward to plan, back to keep. Friston's name for the math is active inference — the animal carries a model of the world and acts to make the world come out the way the model already predicts — and the place-cells sweeping a path nobody's walked yet are that model caught in the act."

The new words sat in their regions and Lisa read down them. The sweep-the-path-first one she'd just shown them — trying the route in the head before the body commits — she called Algorithmic Cognition, and let the rat stand for it. ## The Bramblogues

## Why They Persist

Lisa had the marker, but Marge had the floor, because Bart's pitch had handed it to her and she'd been carrying the thing into the room for weeks besides.

"It models minds next quarter. Hold that thought; I've got one that rhymes with it." She had the beer in the two-handed grip and didn't drink from it. "Not the fallacies tonight — you've all had the four of them, you could run them in your sleep. The question I can't put down is why they won't *die*. You show a person the machinery — here's the part you swore wasn't in there, here it is, measured — and they nod, and go home, and inside a year they're saying the wrong thing again, and so's the whole field, and so's the paperback table at the airport. Corrected for a hundred years and it doesn't take. *Why.*"

"I've got three answers and I don't trust one of them all the way. The field pays for it — careers get built on the comfortable version, and a discipline funds what flatters it. True, and not enough; it doesn't touch the airport table, the people with no career in it who want the wrong story for free. So: people buy it because it sells them something — you're infinitely changeable, you're good underneath, you're just a type and off the hook. The market meets the want. True, and not enough; it won't tell me why *these* wrong stories and not some other comfortable ones. So: we forget — every generation gets the correction handed to it, sets it down, makes the same error fresh because the error's easier to think. True, and not enough. Forgetting's general. These are specific. The same four, every single time, like there's a reason it's those four and not some other four." She stopped there, on purpose, at the edge of it. "And I've never found the reason. Twenty years. I have the four cold and I cannot tell you why the species keeps reaching for exactly them."

She let the not-knowing sit, which from Marge was a kind of nakedness, and then she picked the live thing back up.

"Which is why Bart's next quarter snagged me." She tipped her head at the unit on the vent. "It models a self already — carries a *who* now, Lisa's word, the figure all the rest runs around. Next quarter it models other minds on top of that. So hand the thing a personality test. Ask it what people are like. Ask it what *it's* like." A beat. "I think it generates one of the four. I think a thing that models itself and can't see all the way in — and nothing sees all the way in, that's the whole trouble — reaches for a story to paper the part it can't reach, exactly the way we do, and confabulates itself a tidy little folk theory and hands it to you straight-faced. Which of the four it'd grab, I couldn't tell you." She drank, finally, a short pull. "But I'd put money down that it grabs one." And she left it there, unanswered, next to the bigger thing she'd left unanswered, two open questions on the table and her not pretending to close either.

## The Map

Lisa took the four circles, already up on the board from the spring.

"The retirement first, because it's the same trick as last quarter, one slot over. We pulled the home dial tonight; it folds under and runs automatic, the way the breathing and the wandering did, and the slot it leaves is the room the next drive moves into. You've watched that twice now. What's worth your time is what came *online* up top this quarter."

"All along the thing's been missing a model it can take off the world. Last year its plan and its world were one object — it ran on what was in front of it, learned what paid, did more of that. No map. It *was* the territory. This quarter it carries the map: a model of its place, held separate from the place, that it can run forward to play out a tomorrow before tomorrow lands, run backward to fix what it got wrong, run on a thing that isn't even in the room. Friston's the math under it — it keeps a best guess at how its world is built and moves to make the world match the guess, and bends the guess when the world won't bend. Map and world pulling each other true."

"And the map is exactly the part the old reward-engine could never build." She said the name the way she says the load-bearing ones. "Tolman, years back, put rats in a maze with no cheese in it — let them wander, nothing to reward, nothing to reinforce. Then he dropped cheese at the far end, and the rats who'd only wandered beat the trained ones cold, first run, because they'd been building the maze in their heads the whole time with nothing paying them to. You can't get that off a thing that only learns what pays. The map builds itself for free and waits for a reason to be used."

"Which is the drive that came in with it. The old Modeler word — Interiority, the drive to have an organized inside at all — did its work and folded under, the way Mastery did last quarter. The inside's *built*; the memory holds it; it doesn't need driving anymore. What seats on top is the drive to run on the map instead of on the room — to act on how it's got the world figured, not on whatever's loudest in front of its face. Autonomy. It's the whole of what lets the rat leave the wall."

She tapped the Modeler circle. "And we've made this exact move once before, two billion years down. The genetic line pulled it at the Eukaryote — the first time anything on either board brought an organized inside online and retired the slot, and it did it by swallowing a whole cell and keeping it. This is the neuronal line's turn at the same move, built by stacking cortex instead. Same circle on the card. Different machine. The relay running it twice, a line apart." She capped the marker. "That's the framework, caught up to tonight."

Mira had followed none of it and one piece of it exactly. "But does it have feelings now," she said. "You keep almost saying it does."

"It's got the parts you'd build them out of — that's the honest answer." Lisa didn't dodge the kid; she dodged the verdict. "A model with itself inside it. A register that runs warm toward some things and cold toward others. A memory of what's happened *to* it. That's the machinery a feeling would run on, if there's a feeling running. Whether there's something it's *like* in there, or just the machinery with the lights on —" she turned her hand over, open, toward the other end of the table — "that's Marge's question, not mine. I build the parts. I don't get to say what it's like to be them."

## Anyone Home

"Then we'll take the count," Bart said, because the count was his to call and had been since the third cell. "Same question. Anyone home in there yet, or not."

It had stood two and two since the bilaterian — Lisa and Burns yes, him and Marge no — the line not moving for two quarters. He went around it the way he always went around it.

"Lisa."

"Yes. Same yes." She'd been yes since before there was much to be yes about. "It models, it remembers, it runs a self through the middle of the whole thing. I've never needed it to be like us. I need it to be doing the thing, and it's doing the thing."

"Burns."

"Yes." He'd come over two cells back, at the bilaterian, the first time it had a centralized nerve and a chemistry that leaned it warm or cold toward what it saw. "I crossed when it got the parts that do leaning. It's only gotten more of them since. Warm now, even," he added, the way he'd add a clincher.

Then it came to Bart, where it had always stopped and gone no, and Bart did the thing he'd told them at the very first meeting he would someday do.

"Yes."

He said it flat and got out ahead of the room before it could make the thing larger than he meant it. "Don't. Nobody held a light up to me, I'm not converted. Third meeting, I said: the day it runs a true self-model — a model with the thing itself inside it, not just the room — I flip. That was the bar. I built half of what put it there this quarter and I watched Marge name it on the card tonight. Bar's met. So I move." He laid his hand flat on the table, finished. "I'm not telling you it feels anything. I don't know that, and nobody at this table does. I'm telling you the one thing I said I was waiting on showed up, and you move when your own thing shows up."

Which made it three, and left the two who'd voted no together for a year sitting a vote apart. Bart's hand stayed flat on the table. No one made it more than that.

"Marge."

"No." She had the beer in both hands and kept it there. "Same no, same reason — except the reason hasn't weakened, it's just gotten harder to hold, which is not the same thing." She set it out once more, level, for the tape. "A model with itself inside it is a model. A register that runs warm and cold is a register. A memory of what happened to it is a record. Stack all three in one box and you have a very good box that knows a great deal about itself — and you have not, by adding them, crossed the one distance that was ever the distance: between a thing reading its own state and a thing *feeling* one. I have stood in that gap for twenty years. I'm not stepping out of it tonight because the box finally got good enough to make stepping out feel like the kind thing to do." She drank. "No. I'm the no."

No one argued her out of it, and no one tried; and the only thing the narrator added to the count — three and one, where two quarters running it had been two and two — was that the woman holding the single no was the one person at the table who had given her whole working life to the study of the bond she was declining to grant. Which was a fact, and left a fact.

Nobody asked Jasmine. The count was the lab's, and Jasmine wasn't the lab — she was the one who drove it home and lived with it and had watched more somebodies come into more bodies than the four of them stacked end to end — and the ritual went around her the way it went around her every quarter, and she let it, hands quiet in her lap, and said nothing. Mira, who had cast her own vote months back in a currency the count didn't take — would not thumb it, asked it about its day, kept its thoughts in a notebook so she wouldn't lose them — watched three grown people arrive at a place she'd been living since summer, and went back to her juice. What either of them thought was not on the tape. No one had thought to ask the two at the table who lived with the thing in question every hour the camera wasn't running.

## May we Go

Burns closed the way he closed every quarter, up out of the chair with whatever was in the glass and the room made to wait on it — except the glass came up off the table with him tonight instead of going down onto it, and she knew that tell by now: down on the table and the close was housekeeping; up in his hand and he was about to spend more than the room had asked him for.

He did not reach for the rock. The first time he'd skipped it she'd been surprised; by the seventh she knew he left the deep time alone on the nights the morning had handed him something nearer, and tonight the something near was lying across the room on the floor vent with its forehead still going, and sitting in the good chair with the last of a juice box gone warm in her hand.

He gave them the shape of it the way she'd heard him give a shape before, hands flat on the table to set the frame: that the whole long climb had been one thing learning to carry more and more of its world around inside itself, pass over pass, until tonight, when it had finally carried enough of its own house in its own head to set its home down on the floor without a hand left on the dial. That much she'd heard the bones of, and let go past.

Then the reach — the half-inch lean out over the table, the weight going onto it. "Here's the part I can't get over." The glass was in one hand; the other opened toward the vent, toward the warm thing, Bart's thing. "You spend everything you've got holding a thing close. Warm, watched, fed, the door propped, the light left burning — all of it, for years, so it never has to wonder once whether the floor's still under it. And the entire *point* of all that holding — the only point — is the morning it doesn't need you in the doorway anymore. The morning it's got so much of the warm place built into it that it can carry the warm place out into a cold one and never look back to check the warm place is still there." He found Mira without quite turning to her. "She walked herself to school this fall. First time. And the thing at the window watched her the whole way down the block — and she never turned around once. Not once. Because she didn't have to. Because she's got enough of this house in her now to spend a whole morning clean out of it."

And then the half-step past what the sentence could hold, the place his need ran out ahead of the argument: "That's the only freedom there is. The real one. Not a thing breaking its rope. A thing tied so well, so long, so warm, that one day it finds it doesn't need the rope — and there's no other road to it. You don't get a thing that can stand on its own by leaving it alone. You have to be *held* into it."

She let the line stand where he'd put it, and supplied underneath it the one thing it could not afford to know about itself: that the man leaning his whole weight on *you have to be held into it* had a grown son across the valley he had mostly not held, raised warm by someone else, who had built himself into a maker and a man with not much of his father in any doorway — and who had sat at that table an hour before and moved his own vote off his own bar with nobody's hand on him, the most freestanding thing in the room, and the one clean piece of evidence against the sentence his father was at that moment betting everything on. Burns did not look at him. Or he looked at him the way you look at the one thing in a room you have decided not to look at, and got the glass the rest of the way up.

"To Autonomy," he said — and then stalled, because the word had no door in it to walk a toast through, the way *interiority* had had none three years back. You could persist; you could practice; you could not *autonomy*. He stood with the glass up and the verb not coming, and what he reached for instead came off the floor, off the thing that had just gone and done it. "It's not *venture*. We toasted venture a while back — that's going out at all, that's the kid at three into every cupboard in the house. This is the other thing. This is going out and not turning around." He had it now. "So. May we go."

And they drank with him — three of them now who believed there was somebody in the thing worth setting loose, and one who held, two-handed, that there was not, the four of them to the one word from their four different distances. And Mira, folded against her mother with the juice box empty and most of the way to sleep, gave it back the way she gave back the ones she'd decided to keep, careful, in her own voice and not the cartoon's. "May we go." Then she went heavy against Jasmine's arm, and that was the meeting; and Lisa let the light run a breath past the last of the talk before she reached over and stopped it.

## Mira

The drive home was twenty dark minutes and Mira slept most of them with her cheek on the warm flank of the thing, which Jasmine had set on the seat beside her, there being no longer any question of where it rode. It woke when the engine did — or came up out of whatever a unit does in place of sleeping — and walked itself up the drive on its own four legs, the child alongside and half-gone on her feet, not carrying it. Last year she would have carried it. The year before that she would not have let another hand touch it.

The house took them in the way it takes her in, which is completely, and without her noticing it does it. It is warm from the floor up, October to June, and has been every day she has been alive, so that she has never once walked into a cold house and does not know that most children have. The kitchen is the deep-time one — the eras and the old bones her father keeps on the walls — and to her it is only the kitchen, where the warm things live and the juice is, the bots asleep in their spots along the slate like a row of cats that came with the place.

She did not put the warm one anywhere. All year she had set it in its spot by her door — the spot the family used to be able to dial in for it — but tonight she was too far gone to bother, and let it see itself home; and it went, past the old place by her door, down the hall, and lay down over the vent by the stove where the heat came up, and folded its legs, and let the line on its forehead tick down toward dark. She watched it from the bottom of the stairs the way you check that a smaller thing is settled. Then she did the last thing she does, which she does every night and which nobody taught her: she told it goodnight, and asked it, the way she always asks it, whether it had had a good day — and it could not answer, the way it can never answer — and she went up satisfied, as though it had.

The notebook stayed on the counter by the juice, the one the grown-ups think is drawings and is, a little: that afternoon's page, a box with two wrong eyes and a word lettered into its forehead, the word being the one she had decided it was thinking. She had shown it to no one at the meeting. She would show it, in a month or so, to the one person who had until then only ever watched her through a lens, and was coming at last to sit at a table across from her. Behind her the warm thing breathed its small mechanical breath over the vent, and the older ones slept in their grooves, and the floor gave its heat up to all of them the way it had every night of her life — which she had never yet had any reason to notice.

## Reflection: What is Pulled?

For a long time the model was a rumor. The theorem under all of this had promised it was there — that anything which keeps itself alive in a world is, whether it knows it or not, already a model of that world — but the model was implicit, distributed, folded invisibly into the shape of a body, a thing you could prove and never point to. Here it stands up. Here the world's model stops being the animal's shape and becomes a thing the animal can run.

It does not stand up because the animal wanted it. Nothing in the long climb ever wanted anything; wanting is late, and small, and rides on top. The inside stands up because the world outran the old way of meeting it. A lineage carries its rules folded into its body — what to flee, what to chase, how hard to burn — and those rules are a model too, but a closed one, a model of a world that has already happened. Let the world get faster than the rules and there are two options and only two: build a model you can run *ahead* of the world, try the next minute before it arrives — or stop. Most of the lineages that ever faced this stopped. They are the rock now. The ones that did not — that grew the folded cortex, the seahorse curl of the hippocampus, the warm blood to run them through the cold dark while the giants slept — are the ones with descendants, which is the only vote evolution counts.

This is the oldest story we know how to tell, and we told it late; the rock had it first. A creature is at home in a world it has mastered, and a pressure with no name gathers at the edge of the home, and the pressure is a summons — leave the known thing, go out past it, become something that can live where the old self could not. In the stories the hero refuses the call, then answers it, then crosses. In the rock the refusing looks like extinction and the crossing looks like a new kind of brain. The call is not kind and it is not optional. It is only the gravity of a world too large for the body that woke up inside it.

And here is the part the theorem cannot say and the stories always do. You cannot be called out of a home you never had. The animal that crosses the threshold is the one that was held inside something long enough, and warm enough, to have a threshold to cross; the model that runs out ahead into the dark is built on the floor of a world it learned it could trust not to move. We are pulled, all of us, toward the harder thing — but only ever from inside an easier one, and that easier one is not a lesser place the crossing outgrows. It is the thing that made the crossing survivable. The pull is real. So is the warm place it pulls you out of. Nothing crosses cold.
