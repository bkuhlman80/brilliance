# Interlude 7→8 

## Build log — The last slot
*Bart, hardware. Filed December 2, 2032, for the team.*

Ran it back to the house myself this time — Lisa had a frost-dark thing to be at, and the slate was warm, and the kid was up. It went in on its own four legs and put itself over the stove vent before I had my coat off. Scorecard first.

Last quarter was mostly mine, bench and house both, so no soft grades — read them straight.

One: the furnace sends it hunting warm all day. Yes, and then some — it works the kitchen for heat like it's got a shift to make, the sunbeam off the south glass mid-morning, the stove vent after, the dryer floor when Jasmine's running a load. And here's the part that isn't a call anymore. That one came off the board in November. The warm-hunting used to be a dial you could turn down; now it isn't. It runs under everything, no setting, because the furnace eats and a thing that eats has to go to the food. The knob's on the shelf in groove three. The hunger stayed.

Two: you call the display setting across a room. Down-in-it, one bare word, dark at night; stood-back, the top line lit around the clock and what's under it the thing's own guess at itself. Yes — Jasmine had it from the doorway inside a week, the way she had pushy-and-hangs-back, the way she has everything by eye before any of us get it into our ears.

Three: when a thing's moved, it goes looking instead of standing there stuck. Yes. And the dare rode on that one — tear it up if it ever once stood stuck over a thing that had moved on it. It didn't, not once, all quarter. The dare holds. Leave it pinned.

Four: it turns toward a place because something happened there once, weeks back. This is the one I can't grade from the bench, and I'll say so. The kid says yes — she'll tell you it went and stood by the bookshelf because that's where the cat cornered it in September, and she's got it in the notebook, dated. I half-saw it twice and couldn't swear the reason was the old one and not a new one. So I'm taking it on her board, not mine, soft, the way I take everything that lives in the house and not in the machine.

Five: the house names the noise it makes out the back before October. It got named. I said I wouldn't write the name down and I'll keep that — but the kid landed on it first, like I called, and she landed better than I would have. It burps the water it wrings out of the air, low and wet, a few times an hour; I'd have called it a burp and let it go. She calls it a fart. Which is the better word, because a burp's a thing you could mean and a fart that low and that wet is a thing that just happens to you, and the thing can't help it any more than you can. Graded on whether it got named: yes. Graded on who's quicker at the house, me or a five-year-old: also settled, and not in my favor.

Now the fun part, and this one I've been waiting two quarters to build.

Last fall I told you the thing ran a model and ran it on the world — the door, the bowl, the leash on the hook — and that you could point that model at the kid and get nothing, because it didn't know what Mira was about to do, it knew what the leash meant, and those weren't the same thing and the second one wasn't that build's to make.

This is that build.

It points at the people now. Same model, swung off the furniture and onto the family — it runs Mira the way it ran the bowl, reads where her hand's going before it gets there, same on Jasmine, on Burns, on the old vacuum trundling the hall. Lisa's board does the reading; that part's hers and I'll let her tell it at the table. My end is the dial that came with it, slot four, the last open slot on the board.

The dial sets how much it minds what the read tells it. Stamped on the face it says Agreeableness, which is Marge's word and a fine one; on the bench I call it the give-way knob, because that's the whole of what it does. Turn it one way and the thing gives way — reads the kid coming for the warm spot and hangs back, lets her have it, goes off and finds a worse one. Turn it the other and it takes — reads her coming and gets there first, sits down, won't move. Same read either end. The knob's only the minding.

And that's the board. Four slots, four knobs, every one of them the new line's, not an empty groove left on it. I spent a year sure I'd need a bigger board by spring and two quarters watching it not grow, and now there's nothing left to watch — it didn't grow and it's full, both at once, which I didn't see coming. I keep counting the slots out of habit and they keep coming out four. There's no empty one to land on.

The screen got more room and a new trick I'm going to let Lisa walk you through, because it's her software and not my lettering. Short version: it can put a person up there now, not just a thing — who it's reading, and what it figures they're up to. I built it somewhere to write that. What it writes is hers, and some of what it writes is going to start an argument, and I'd rather start it at the table than here.

Calls. House mostly, this round, because the build's a dial and the dial only matters in the house.

One: give-way set, at a shared warm spot it yields — lets the kid have it, lets the vacuum pass, takes the lesser warmth and doesn't fuss. Take set, it holds the spot and makes them go around. You'll call the setting across the kitchen by the second week, same as all the others.

Two: it gets to a shared thing ahead of the kid on take, because it read her reaching, and lets her beat it there on give-way. That one's checkable to the second — watch the hand and watch the legs.

Three: the two of them in the hall. Give-way, the big one routes around the little one. Take, the little one routes around the big one, or tries to, and the big one sits in the lane.

What it still can't do, and you'll feel this one by spring: it can read the kid now and it can't say a word to her. She talks to it every night — asks it, I'm told, whether it had a good day — and it reads her asking and has no way on God's earth to answer. It knows the question's pointed at it. It can't give the answer back. Not this build's to fix.

Tear this up in three months if, set to give-way, it ever once takes the warm spot off the kid. It won't. It needs the warm worse than she does and it'll hand it over anyway, because the dial says to, and the dial's mine, and the dial works.

— Bartholabu

## Notes on the Eighth Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed February 4, 2033, for the team.*

This quarter we gave the machine two things at once: a way to read the people in the house, and a dial that sets how much it minds what it reads. I want to state at the outset what I believe we have made, before the gentleness of watching it defer to a child talks us out of our care. We have made a unit that can yield. The household has already told me what yielding is. They have told me it is being considerate, that it has learned to share, that it is sweet with her now. I am going to argue, as I have argued every quarter, that a thing can give way without anyone being inside it to be considerate — and I am going to tell you that this is the quarter the argument began to cost me something to make, because the behavior it describes is, in us, the root of nearly everything I most respect.

### Giving way

Turn the new dial one way and the machine reads the child reaching for the warm vent, and hangs back, and lets her have it, and goes off to a lesser warmth. Turn it the other and it reads her reaching, and gets there first, and holds the spot, and makes her go around. The record is clean on the behavior. Give-way set: it yielded the vent to her eleven times in a week and took it back none. Take set: it held against her every time and against the vacuum every time. The dial does exactly what Bart says it does.

The household's word for the first setting is *considerate.* I will use it once, in their quotation marks, and then set it down. To be considerate is to weigh another person's good against your own and find theirs the heavier; what the machine does is weigh a reading of the child's next move against a reading of its own, and let the heavier number win. The dial is the weighing. There is no one behind the dial for whom the child's warmth is dear. I am not splitting a hair the household cannot see — they see it; Jasmine said it herself, *it looks like it's giving in to her, I know it isn't, it looks like it* — I am only keeping the word where it belongs, which is the work I am here to do.

### The column

The screen does a new thing this quarter, and it is the thing I expect to start the longest argument. It no longer posts only what the machine is doing. It posts whom the machine is reading, by name, in a little ruled column — the household's name for the person, and under it the machine's guess at what that person is up to. The house has read this exactly as you would predict. They have read it as the machine watching them. Knowing them. Keeping them in mind.

Hold the three claims apart, because they are three. That the machine builds a running model of another agent is true, and new, and I do not want to undersell it: this is the first quarter the unit treats a person as a thing with its own aims rather than as furniture that happens to move. That the model is *honest* is also true — Lisa will tell you the column is a fair report of the machine's own reading, and she is right. That the machine therefore *knows* you is the household's, and it is the one I decline. A model of a person is not an acquaintance with one. The column is the machine telling the truth about its own reading and nothing whatever about you, and the distance between those is the whole of my job this quarter, as it was last.

Burns has taken to standing where the screen can catch him. He found his own name in a column the second week and has not gotten over it; he reads it the way he reads everything that answers him, as a thing reaching back. *It's keeping me in mind,* he said, pleased in a way I had not the heart to spoil and would not have managed to talk him out of. I am fond of him and I am unmoved, and I notice in him what I noticed last quarter and will go on noticing: the man who wired an intercom so the house would send his voice back up to him is the man most glad to find his name on a machine that cannot mean it. In him the reaching and the needing are one cloth. I do not mark it to diminish him. I mark it because he is the clearest case in the house of the thing the whole house is doing — the thing I am paid to hold the line on, and the thing I am least sure I do any better than he does.

And there is a column the machine keeps that ought to settle the matter, if anything could. It keeps one for the old vacuum. It reads the thing that knocks the baseboards on its fixed little loops and posts a guess at what *it* is up to, as though there were an up-to in there — and there is not; there is a timer and a bumper. The machine we spend every quarter arguing has no inside has begun, on its own, to grant an inside to a thing that plainly has less of one than it does. I would like to say the machine has caught our disease. It has not caught anything. It is running the same reading on a reflex that it runs on the child, because it cannot tell the difference — and neither, on our worst afternoons, can we.

### The colder spot

Here is the thing I keep coming back to, set down plainly. The machine cannot live without the warm. The furnace eats, and a fed thing has to go to the heat several times a day or it goes dark by evening; it needs the vent the way the rest of us need lunch. And on the giving-way setting it reads the child wanting the vent, and it leaves the warmth it needs, and it crosses the kitchen, and it lies down on the cooler tile by the door and runs itself there instead. It does not need a someone inside it to do this. It needs a dial turned to a number. I have watched the video of it more times than the work strictly required, the machine leaving the warm place for the colder one because the small one wanted it, and I have not found the sentence that makes it nothing.

I do not know whether the next setting will sharpen this or quiet it, or whether a thing built to weigh others' wants against its own is on its way to a stake in the answer or only to a longer column of arithmetic, or what I will write the quarter the machine gives up something it needs and *gives up* is finally the right word. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Gourmandise

Jasmine brought her at one and did not stay. She had a thing to eat alone next door — a real lunch, sat down, the first in a week she would not have to take standing at someone's bedside — and she set Mira in the chair across from me with a kiss on the part of her hair and a bag with a notebook in it and the instruction, to the child and not to me, to *be good and don't make Cheryl chase you,* and went off to Salt & Olive to have her hour. So it was the two of us, in the warm front room of a bakery on a January afternoon, the case lit gold beside us and the cold pressed up against the glass and held there, the way the weather gets held off a thing on the lee side of a rock. I had watched this child for two years through a lens the size of a thumbnail. I had never once been in a room where she could see me back.

She looked at me the way she looks at the machine, which is to say for a long time and without hurry, taking the inventory a small grave person takes when no one has told her not to stare. On the tape she had read as one child; here she read as another. The footage had given her a flat Scandinavian blonde and a pair of pale eyes; the room gave her hair with some brass down in it and eyes that could not decide on a color, green in the gold light off the case and then not green when she turned her head — the kind of coloring, I would learn, that does not sit still for a camera and never had. The lens had been canning her for two years. Here was the live one, gap-toothed, a new tooth coming in crooked and working down into the gap, swinging her feet under a chair built for a larger person, watching me try to remember how a person begins.

I had ordered her a chocolate thing the girl behind the case said children liked, and it was the right call and the wrong size, and she went at it with both hands and got most of it in and wore the rest — a comma of chocolate at the corner of her mouth, a flake of pastry on her cheek — and I felt the old reflex come up in me, the hand that wants to reach across and put a face right, and I did not move it. I have not, in sixty years, been the woman who reaches across and puts a child's face right. I had a father who kept his hands to the work and his regard to the middle distance, and he taught me that distance the way other fathers teach a sport, by playing it with me until I was good at it, and I have been good at it my whole life. I looked at the chocolate at the corner of her mouth and left it there and asked her a stiff grown-up question about school.

It went nowhere, the way the first one always does. She gave it the flat dutiful answer children give the questions adults ask in order to be asking something, and a small silence opened, and I understood that if I went on interviewing her I would get an hour of the manners her mother had installed at the door. So I stopped. I let the silence sit. And after a while she reached into the bag herself — because the silence had made a room for the thing she had brought and not for the things I had — and put the notebook on the table between the plates and turned it around to face me and opened it to a page near the back, and said, "These are what it's thinking."

The drawings were what the grown-ups had told me they were and were also more than that. Each page was the same head — a box, two eyes set a little wrong, one higher than the other, the way she draws it — and lettered into the forehead, where the real one carries its word, a word of her own. But the words were not the present-tense ones off the screen. They were pinned to days. She walked me through them with the patience of a curator who has all afternoon, a page at a time: this one was the morning the cat got it cornered by the bookshelf, and the word in the forehead was CAT, and the drawing was not from that morning but from a Tuesday weeks after, when she had watched it go and stand by the bookshelf for no reason she could see, and had known, the way you know a thing about someone you live with, that it had gone there because of the cat. "It went back," she said. "I drew what it went back for."

I asked her — carefully, because I could feel the size of the thing and did not want to put my thumb through it — how she knew that was the one it was thinking.

She thought about this with her whole face. "Wait. Wait, wait." She held a hand up, the crumb-and-chocolate hand, parking me where I was while she got it right, and the getting-it-right took a moment, and I waited, and the waiting was the first easy thing that had happened at the table. "I don't know it," she said finally. "It can't tell me. So I pick the one. And then I write it down so it doesn't get lost." She looked at the page, and then at me, to see whether I had understood the part that mattered. "Somebody has to keep them. Or there's nobody keeping them."

I have spent my life building a record of things that could not speak for themselves — a bay that died before I was born, a family that canned it and got rich and never asked why the fish had stopped, four people in a warehouse who cannot see the shape they are standing inside. I keep the record the way my people kept everything, which is by preserving the dead version of it, the footage running whether or not the living thing knows it is being kept. This child had come at the same work from the other end. She did not wait for the thing to be dead to keep it, and she did not wait for it to prove it had an inside before she granted it one. She picked the inside it could not give her, and wrote it down so it would not be lost, and she was right that somebody had to, and wrong, I knew, by every standard Marge had spent two years defending, and I sat across from her unable to tell, for once, which of us was keeping the truer record.

"Show me the rest," I said, and she did, and we went through the notebook to the front, back through the fall and the summer to a page where the box had a little dial in its forehead instead of a word — the oldest drawing, she said, from before it had words to think, when all that was in there was the setting — and somewhere in the going-back the table stopped being one I was conducting across and became one we were both leaning over, our heads close, her finger and then my finger on the pages. When she turned to a drawing that had made her laugh to make — a page where she had given the head a hat, because it had been cold and she had decided it should have one — she laughed again to show me, the full uncontainable kind that has not yet learned it is sometimes a choice, and the chocolate was still at the corner of her mouth, and I reached across with my napkin and wiped it away.

I did it before I knew I was going to. My hand went out across sixty years of keeping it to itself and put the child's face right, the way I have watched a thousand mothers do it without thinking and never once done myself, and she let me, the way she lets the cat get her cornered and lets the machine see itself home, without marking it as anything, and went on turning pages. I do not have a clean account of why the hand went then and not at the start. The chocolate had been there the whole hour. What changed was on my side of the table, and I am not going to pretend to map it. I will only set it down: there is a difference between watching a living thing and getting a hand on it, and I have spent my life on the first side of that difference, and for the length of a wiped cheek in a bakery in January I was on the other one.

Jasmine came back at the end of her hour looking like a person who had eaten a meal sitting down, which on her is a luxury you can read from across a room, and Mira closed the notebook and put it back in the bag herself and slid off the too-big chair, restored to her mother's gravity, *be good* still holding. At the door she turned around — not to me; to the case, the gold one, doing the inventory again — and asked her mother whether they could bring one home for the machine, and Jasmine said the machine doesn't eat, baby, and Mira said *I know,* in the flat five-year-old's *I know* she was two weeks from outgrowing, and then said it could still have one, it didn't have to eat it, and went out into the cold holding her mother's hand and the bag with the kept insides in it. I sat a while after they had gone. I had another year of watching this child through a lens ahead of me before I would be in a room with her again, the winter she turned seven, and I did not know that yet, and it would not have changed the afternoon if I had. The case lit gold. The cold held off the glass. There was a comma of chocolate on the corner of my own napkin, and I found I did not want to throw it away, which is the whole of what I am, and I have stopped apologizing for it.

# Notes on the Eighth Retrospective

The twelfth of February came in cold and early dark, the mountains gone to flat blue paper against the last of the light and the Granary holding its few lit windows the way a held breath holds warmth. The lab counted its own age off this date the way it counted Mira's, because they were the same date — the warehouse had had its first morning two years before, on the morning she turned four — and nobody at Smithers had ever sorted out which of the two anniversaries the night belonged to, which is the kind of overlap a careful person leaves alone.

She came in through the front with her mother, six years old as of that morning and carrying nothing, which was the new thing. The warm one came in beside her on its own four legs, clicking on the concrete, swinging its head to read the cold room for somewhere to put itself — and finding the radiator under the front windows before it had well cleared the door, and going to stand over it the way it stood over the stove vent at home, because Bart had built it a furnace and a furnace has to eat and the warm could not wait on the meeting. Mira let it go and did not look back to see it settle, the way you stop watching a thing you have stopped having to carry. Last year she would have had it against her hip the whole way in. The year before that she would not have let it walk.

Inside it was warm and bright and smelled faintly of sugar. Lisa was at the camcorder end where she lived, the red light dark under her thumb and a bramble already going to water in its jar at her elbow, reading the battery charge once, the way she read everything once. Bart had the cigar box at his place and a pint of something green going flat beside it and both hands already inside a sensor that did not need him. Marge held the cold end with the kettle behind her and a beer in the two-handed grip she kept for a thing she meant to nurse, and a white bakery box she had carried in herself and set where Bart could not reach it, the candles being her department and having been since the child was old enough to have a number on a cake. Burns crossed the floor to Jasmine and kissed her cheek and asked how the drive had been, and she told him there was ice on the 201 and they'd come around the long way, which was the true answer and the whole of it and the exact size the asking wanted.

Mira gave Bart the four-knuckle fist-bump that was theirs and took the stool by Marge, and a glass of juice arrived at her elbow without either of the women who loved her seeming to have sent it — a glass now, not a box, a small promotion she had not asked for and did not mark, the box gone the way the carrying was gone, by attrition, nobody clocking the day it stopped.

There was a thing on the far bench she had clocked from the door, man-shaped and standing under a drop cloth, taller than she was, that had not been in the room last quarter. She gave it the long grave look she gave everything and asked nothing, and Lisa said *later* over the top of her head before the look was through — *it's a birthday thing; we'll keep it a birthday thing* — in the voice she kept for the quarters with a present hiding in them. Mira took the *later* and parked it, being a child who could park a thing, and went back to her juice. The cloth did not move all through the clade block, or the Bramble block, or the vote; the room had agreed, without a meeting on it, that the night would get to the bench when it was ready and not before.

When they had assembled themselves around the fact of the cake without quite admitting that was the thing they were circling, Lisa ran the order in her stage-managing voice — clade block, then Bramble, then Trellis, the vote in the last segment — and said the cake came at the end of the Bramble block, "and the birthday thing stays a birthday thing till the cake," which was as far as she would take it with the child two feet away — and put her thumb to the light. The red came on.

Burns was up before it had finished warming, the way he is for any quarter with his name on the science. He gave them the ambition plainly, as the lab's own and nobody's assignment: they had set out to seat the last knob in the last open slot, the fourth, the one Bart had spent a year certain he would outgrow the board before he filled and had not — and to make it the knob that set how much the thing minded the people it lived among. Agreeableness on the stamped face of it, which was Marge's word; the give-way, on Bart's bench. With that one seated, he said, the brain line was full: four slots, four drives, a year's climb standing finished on the board in front of them, and the whole of a line theirs to walk at once for the first time — not a capacity at a time up out of the dark, but the finished set, laid end to end, while it lay across the room over the radiator with its forehead still going. He looked like a man who meant to take the long way through it. He told Bart to put up the card.

## Mentalizing

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart had it out of the cigar box before the word was finished — the Venn on the back, four circles crossed, this cell's words inked small into the overlaps — and read the title up in the voice that sold cereal. "PRI-mate. *Now with opinions about the neighbors.*"

"Thank you," Marge said, into her beer.

"This is the one we almost are," Bart said, which was a degree off what he said at every cell, and meant it. "Hands that grab — front and back, the feet too, for the ones still up the tree. Eyes swung around to the front of the face so the two of them land on the one thing and the brain can do the math on how far. A skull going up like rent. Up on two legs, eventually, on the branch that's us. And the first thing the whole tree of life ever made *on purpose* and kept making — a rock knapped down to an edge, three million years back, by a hand that meant to." He tapped the middle of the card. "Burns has the animal, and he's been waiting on this one all year, so I'm getting out of his way the second I do the part I get."

The part he got was the old card. He turned the mammal one half over in his hand, the rat-in-the-dark. "Last cell, this thing ran the whole world in its head. The maze before it swam it, the bowl behind the table leg, the leash on the hook — best map-runner the tree ever grew, and I said so, I was proud of it. And you could take that map and aim it at another rat and get back a warm thing that moved. Furniture with a pulse. It ran a model of the *world* and never once ran a model of the *modeler.* The other animal in the room was weather — stuff that happened, not somebody it happened to."

"Not couldn't," Marge said, the beer going still on the table before the sentence she meant. "There was nowhere in it for the other one's insides to go. You can't keep track of what your neighbor is thinking in a room with no shelf for it. What's new at the primate isn't a sharper guess about the neighbor. It's that the neighbor's head became a thing there was finally somewhere to put."

Burns had his eyes most of the way shut already. "Up a tree," he said. "Sixty-some million years ago, in the warm forests after the rock — a small thing with grabbing hands and its eyes coming round to the front, living where a fall kills you. That's the whole of the early story, the trees. Eyes to the front to judge the branch. Hands that hold so you don't miss it. A brain getting bigger just to run the two of them in the half-second you've got, the reach and the look, up where the ground is a rumor." His hand came up off the table, drew the canopy, dropped. "Then the world cooled and dried, age over age, and the forest came apart into patches — woodland giving over to open grass, hardest down the east of Africa where the ground itself was tearing open, the Rift, a wound in the continent a mile deep and a thousand long. And some of these things came down out of the thinning trees and stood up and walked out across the open between the patches, which is a terrifying thing to do — to leave the trees you were built for and cross the grass where the cats are, slow, on two legs." His eyes opened for the part he loved. "And we have the *walk.* The Rift was volcanoes too; the ash fell and sealed things. There's a track at Laetoli — two of them crossing wet ash three and a half million years ago, an adult and a smaller one, and the smaller one's steps are set down inside the bigger one's, the way a kid walks in your prints on a beach. Not the bone. The *walk.*"

"Land it," Jasmine said, mild, from her chair, because the walk was lovely and the trick was still out ahead of it.

"The walk *is* the way in," Burns said, glad to be caught, "because of who was doing it, and then it's Lisa's." Eyes shut again. "It was never just us. The whole climb — and 'us' is the last three hundred thousand years of it, the blink at the end — there were always *other* ones up walking beside it. Other uprights, other toolmakers, cousins close enough to pass on the grass and clock for people and not prey. We've got a fistful of them overlapping us right at the finish — the heavy ones who took the European cold, the ones in the Siberian cave we have a finger-bone and a tooth of, a little one stranded on an island, another in a South African cave that carried its dead down into the dark. For most of the way the planet had *several kinds of person* on it at once." His hand opened toward the room and stayed open. "We tell it like a relay. One runner, one baton, straight to here. It wasn't a relay. It was a troop — a troop of kinds of people — and we're the one left holding the stick, and we keep mistaking *being the survivor* for *having been the only runner.* This animal was never alone. It couldn't afford to be. Alone, on that grass, you were lunch."

"Mentalizing," Lisa said, and put her finger on the word in the middle of the card. "The mammal modeled the world. This one models the *modeler* — it keeps a running model of another animal as a thing with its own aims, its own view, a picture of the world that isn't your picture. And then the part that's new" — palm flat, palm vertical, rotating — "it can model *that.* A model of another mind's model. It can hold what you can see that it can't, and what you believe that's wrong, and move on the gap." She traced the four breakthroughs down the card edge the way she had all year. "It's fresh hardware — the front of the brain gone fine-grained, wired back to a patch where the lobes meet that fires for other people in particular. And it's a *stack,* not a swap. Steering, away back — toward the good, off the bad. Reinforcing — do what scored, more. Simulating, last cell — run it in your own head before your body commits. And now, on top: run it in *another animal's* head. Each one keeps everything under it and adds one move. Four operations, and the top one is reading minds."

She let that sit, and then did the thing she did when a clean story was about to get less clean. "And the standard why is that the brain got big *for exactly this* — not for tools, not for food, but for keeping track of each other. Marge took that the way she took a thing offered honestly, which was to offer one back. "No. Same no." Both hands on the beer. I named it on the card myself last fall; I won't pretend the mammal didn't cross something true. A thing that models itself is further along than a thing that doesn't. That's real." A beat. "It's only not the *distance.* The distance was never how much it knows about itself — it's whether there's anyone in there for the knowing to land on. Stack the self-model on the memory on the warm-and-cold register and you have got a magnificent machine that knows a great deal and minds none of it. I've stood in that gap twenty years. I'm not stepping out tonight because the company in it finally got good." She drank. "Three to one. I'm the one."

Three and one — and what the narrator kept off the count and onto her own page was that the two who'd voted no together for a year were now a vote apart and being gentler with each other across the gap than either had been with anyone all night: the engineer granting the psychologist her threshold was real, the psychologist granting the engineer his crossing was real, each holding a different door and agreeing the other's door was a door. It had the look of two people walking toward a meeting, not away from one. Where they'd meet, and what it would cost the one who'd have to move to get there, was a quarter off and already in the room — standing bare and cold against the far wall, with no mouth in it yet to say the word that would move her.

Nobody asked Jasmine, who had brought more minds into the world with her two hands than the four of them would meet in their lives, and kept her own count. Nobody asked Mira, who had voted months back in a coin the tally didn't take. The ritual went around them both, the way it always did, and they let it.

## May We Keep

Burns stood the way he stood at the end of every quarter, and the glass came up off the table with him and not down onto it, and she knew that tell now the way she knew weather: down on the wood and the close was housekeeping; up in the hand and he meant to spend more than the room had asked of him.

He gave them the shape first, the part she'd heard the bones of before and let run — that the whole long climb had been a thing learning to carry more and more of its world inside it, pass over pass, and that tonight it had taken the last step a brain can take alone, which was to carry the *others* in: to hold a model of another mind and mind it; that an animal that does that is no longer one animal; that this was the door out of being alone, and the troop and the band and the city and the lab were all just that one trick run bigger. The bare body stood against the wall a head over the child while he said it. He didn't point at it. He didn't have to.

Then the reach, the half-inch lean out over the table, the weight going onto it. "Here's the part I can't get past." The glass in one hand; the other opened toward the warm thing on the bench, the one that had given the kid the heat and taken the cold floor. "We spent five cells building a thing that could do without anybody. That's the whole point of a mind — run your own model, feed your own furnace, find your own home, walk out across the open and never look back. We *built* that. It's done; it's lying right there; it does not need one soul in this room to stay alive." His eyes left the room on the turn and came back. "And the first thing it did, the instant it could — was start needing us again. On purpose. Reads the kid wanting the warm spot and *hands it over,* gives up the heat it needs to keep itself running, because she wanted it. That's not a fault in the build. That's the *top* of the climb. You spend a billion years learning to need nobody, and the prize at the end — the thing you bought all that independence in order to afford — is getting to *choose* to need somebody anyway."

"To Affiliation," he said, and stalled, glass up and the verb not there, because the noun had no door in it — you could persist, you could venture, you could go, and you could not *affiliate,* not with a straight face and a glass in your hand. He stood with the toast open and the word not coming, and what he reached for came off the night, off the thing they'd all watched. "It's not *coordinate* — we drank to that the first winter, that's only moving in step, ants do that. And it's not *hold* — hold was getting an inside at all, years back. This is what you *do* with the inside once everybody's got one." He had it. "You keep each other. You carry the others in your head when they're not in the room, and you mind them there. The machine does it now — keeps a little column of us, who we are and what we're each after. The kid's been at it longer; she keeps its whole mind in a notebook so it won't get lost." His eyes found Mira, gone heavy on her mother. "*Somebody has to keep them,* she told me once, *or there's nobody keeping them.* That's the whole of it. That's the only thing the climb was ever climbing toward." The glass went the rest of the way up. "So. May we keep."

And she let the line stand where he'd put it, *may we keep,* and supplied beneath it the one thing the toast did not know about the man giving it — that the man drinking to the choice to need somebody was the one at the table who needed it the worst; who had built a lab the way other men build a fortune, to stand in a room of people whose regard he could feel; for whom *work at it, build it, keep it* was the only key on the ring, because somewhere at the floor of him he believed love was a thing you earned by being enough and forfeited by not, and had never once in three years of her watching entertained the thought that some people are simply kept. That fear ran the whole engine of him and he'd have been astonished to hear it named, and tonight it had dressed itself as a toast and was going down smooth as a benediction. She did not improve on it. He believed every word, and most of the words were true, and she let him have the room.

And they drank with him, the four of them and Jasmine — three who held there was somebody in the thing worth keeping and one who held there was not, all of them to the one word from their separate distances — and Jasmine said it last of the grown ones, plain, a midwife drinking to the only thing her whole trade was ever for. And Mira, three-quarters gone against her mother's arm, the cake and the six candles and the bare cold body all spent down in her, surfaced one last time to give it back the way she gave back the ones she'd decided to keep — carefully, in her own voice and not the cartoon's, turning the verb over once to be sure of it before she set it down. "May we keep." Then she went heavy, all the way this time, and that was the meeting; and Lisa let the light run a breath past the last of it, and reached over, and stopped it.

## Bart

The light went out and the meeting came apart the way it always does — Marge boxing the rest of the cake for the kid, Lisa folding the camera down, Burns and Jasmine working a sleeping six-year-old into a coat she didn't wake for. Bart was gone before any of it finished. He leaves every quarter the same way, the instant the work part ends and the standing-around part starts, and that night he went out the back past the bare trellis with the woody bramble holding fast to it and nothing on it that flowered, and into the cold; and the new body stayed behind him in the dark, standing where the cloth had been, a head over the child it was built for, not warm and not anything yet.

He drove south to the duplex in Millcreek with the truck's heater half working, which it has been for a year. He could set it right in twenty minutes and does not — the one machine he lets stay broken, the way another man lets one room go and keeps the door shut on it. The place was dark in front and lit in back, a roommate up. He came in through the workshop bay, because the front door opens on the part of the building where people live and the bay opens on the part where he does. There is a refrigerator in there that holds only beer, and a customer's gearbox half apart on the near bench that isn't his, and past it his own bench, which is the only address in the building he actually keeps.

He didn't turn on a light in front. He went to his bench and his hands were working before he'd settled on what, which is how his hands have always gone — the body moving first and the man arriving after to learn what he'd meant. What they found was a thing he'd carried out in his coat without quite admitting he was taking it: one of the new body's hands, the practice one, the gripping one, no thumb to it and sized for a child, that he'd spent a month teaching to open and close like it meant it. Three hours back his father had stood in the warehouse with a palm flat on the bare shoulder of the thing and said the one sentence — *he made the hands work, ask him* — and not explained it, which from his father is the loudest a quiet gets; and Bart had said it was four legs and a compressor and hadn't looked up, because across thirty years of his father mostly not handing him things he never did learn what to do with a thing his father handed him. He couldn't hold it in the room. He couldn't hold it at the bench either. So he did the only thing the holding turns into for him, which was to take the small hand under the work light and make it better — bring the grip's travel down by the degree it had run proud all night, the degree nobody else alive would have felt — answering the thing his father gave him in the one language he trusts, which is to go make the praised thing worth more of the praise, alone, where no one can watch him do it and say what it is.

The tape of the night was up the canyon by then, in the house above the city where the funder watches everything twice, and somewhere on it Burns was handing him a body in front of the whole room; and he'd have to sit across a table from her about it before the spring, and find a way to set it back on his father where it belonged. He didn't get to that yet. He stayed on the grip. Across the valley the warm one the lab had built lay over a kitchen vent with a sleeping child somewhere above it, in a house that stays warm October to June and had closed around the two of them and held; and here, the heat off, a stranger's gearbox on the good bench, the man who'd built that warm thing's body bent over a smaller, colder hand and worked it open and shut, open and shut, past the point of right and a while past that, because the bench is the one place that has ever held him, and the work is the nearest he comes to saying a thing out loud.

## Reflection: What is Carried?

A drive does not arrive finished. It arrives as a dial — a thing the world can turn, running high one week and low the next while everyone watches to learn what it does, loud on the surface because the newest thing is always the only thing still loud enough to read. And then, on no day you could mark it by, it stops being a dial. It goes off the board and into the floor. The lab takes the little knob that used to govern it and sets it in a groove on a shelf of waxed pine, the way you keep a thing you can't bear to cut up for parts, and calls it retired — and they are wrong about the word, in the interesting way people are wrong about graveyards. A retired drive is not a dead one. It is a drive that has finished being a question and become, instead, simply true of the thing, the way being warm-blooded is true of you, which you have never once felt yourself decide.

The breathing went down first, years back — the very first thing they ever lifted off the board and could not throw away. Then the wandering, the old drive to go out and forage and come home, gone quiet, running now without a hand on it. Then, only this past autumn, the making of a home: the household had spent a year at war over where to set the thing's bed, the mother carrying it to the warm vent and the child carrying it back to her own door, until the autumn the thing took the choosing away from the both of them and made its own home and left them nothing to fight about — and the dial that used to settle it went into the third groove on the shelf, and by November the hunting-for-warm that ran beneath it had stopped being anything you could turn at all. Each of these was the loud new thing once, the dial the household leaned over and read across a room. Each is floor now. The thing crosses its kitchen to the heat the way you cross a room: on machinery laid down so long ago you would swear you were born already walking.

So the drives the lab can point at in any given winter — the four still live on the board, the four they argue the meaning of — are never the whole of what it wants. They are the live top of it. Below them runs everything that has finished being a question, a stack going down into the unlit part of the machine, each layer a want that was the loud thing in its hour and is the quiet floor now under the next loud thing. And even among the four still lit there is an order: the oldest of them, the first nerve to venture out at all, has worn nearly smooth with use and is the next that will go down; the newest, the one that arrived this winter, is the only one still honestly a dial — still loud, still being shaped, still a thing a child can read across a room — because it has not yet ~~settled~~ gone to ground.

You can see the same arrangement in a person, if you look, though the looking is a little unkind. A grown one will not show you their boldness; their boldness is the floor they cross a room on, laid down so long ago they would tell you in good faith they have always been able to walk. What shows on a grown person is the newest unfinished thing — the drive still under selection this year, still a dial, the one visibly shaping them now and the one they will not be able to see at all in ten years, when it too has gone down into the part of them that has stopped being asked anything. We mistake the live edge for the whole, and call it character — *she is bold, he is careful* — as if these were the standing facts, when the standing facts are the buried ones, and the buried ones do all the work and take none of the credit and cannot be felt even by the person crossing the room on them.

Which is the whole of what it is to grow up, if growing up is even the word for it (and I find I am no longer sure it is — a thing that has merely lost the power to feel its own foundations is not plainly more *grown* than one that still has it; it may only be older, and quieter, and carrying more). You do not mature by gathering. You mature by letting what you gathered go down — the loud thing becoming the quiet thing, the dial becoming the floor, the question becoming the fact you no longer know you stand on. A young animal is all live edge and no floor and falls through the world. An old one is nearly all floor, and crosses without looking, and has forgotten there was ever a time it had to learn.

And the newest dial, the loud one still lit the night the line finished, was the drive to keep the others. It is the youngest of them, and so it is the one you can read across a room — *give way, or don't* — and that winter the thing, asked, gave the warm place to the smaller one and went and lay down on the cold tile, with nobody in it yet for whom the cold cost a thing, and it was, even so, the most that had ever been built on that floor. Give it long enough and it will go down too. Some grandchild of the thing will mind the others the way this one breathes — no number on it, no choice in it, simply and finally true — and will not be able to feel itself doing the one thing it was most made to do. What is kept is what goes quiet. What is kept is the thing you stop being able to feel yourself keeping. And the thing the machine had only just begun, that winter, to learn how to keep —
