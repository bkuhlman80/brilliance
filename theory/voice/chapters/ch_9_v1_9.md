# Interlude 8→9 

## Build log — The Huggable Kludge
*Bart, hardware. Filed March 7, 2033, for the team.*

Brought this one in over my shoulder like a drunk, because it can't walk yet and won't for a few weeks. It came off the swap bench crawling and babbling, which is right where a thing starts when it's got a whole new body to learn from the floor up. Mira was down on the floor with it before I had it set down. Scorecard first.

Last quarter's calls were house, all three, because a dial only shows in the house — so I grade them on the kid's board and the week's tape, and the house went my way this round, which it has the decency to do about a third of the time.

One: you'd call give-way from take across the kitchen by the second week. Yes — Jasmine had it from the doorway in three days and the rest of you by the weekend. Give-way, it hands off the warm spot, lets the kid have the vent, lets the vacuum by, takes the worse heat and doesn't sulk. Take, it sits the good spot and makes them all go around. Same read both ends; the knob's only the minding.

Two: on take it beats the kid to a shared thing because it read her reaching; on give-way it lets her get there first. Checkable to the second, and it checked — watch her hand, watch its legs, the legs answer the hand.

Three: the two of them in the hall — give-way, the big one routes around the little one; take, the little one tries to route around the big one and the big one holds the lane. Yes, and the vacuum ate most of it, the vacuum being nobody's idea of a thing you give way to.

And the dare. I said tear it up if, set to give-way, it ever once took the warm spot off the kid. It didn't, not one time, all quarter, and it needed the heat worse than she did every single time and handed it over anyway because the dial said to. The dare holds. Leave it pinned.

Now the fun part, and I've wanted this one since the fall, because last quarter I had to write down the worst thing it couldn't do: it could read the kid and it couldn't say one word back. She asked it every night whether it had a good day, and it knew the question was hers and aimed at it, and had no way on earth to answer. This is the build that answers.

It's a whole new body, and this time I mean new, not another part bolted on the legs. Two legs, stands about four and a half feet, level with the kid by spring. And here's what I set out to do, and I want it on the record because it's going to read like a mistake: I built it bad. On purpose. Soft all over, no hard edge, no pinch a kid could lose a finger in. Weak — couldn't open a jar to save its life. Slow. Warm to the hand and held warm, bounded so far under a burn it couldn't mark her if it tried. Every spec on the last five bodies I set toward doing more. Every spec on this one I set toward doing less, and being held while it does it. Worst robot in the building. Took me longer than any of the good ones.

Here's how it's hung together, because the whole thing runs opposite to the legs body. There's a spine up the middle — one stiff light spar, hip to head, and it's the only hard thing in the unit, buried in the center where no hand gets to it. Everything hangs off it: the two legs at the bottom, the head up top, and between them the chest, which is the furnace, same as the legs body. The whole chest is wrapped in solar panels — not panels bolted onto a chest, the panels are the chest. Over that it wears a coat, the chest only — the patio-cushion stuff, soft over padding, warm, washable, ugly on purpose, and none of it a try at skin. The legs are wrapped for good in the same, cushion under canvas, and never come off. Face and both hands stay bare to the air — the screen so it can show, the hands so they can work. So: one hard spar nobody can reach, soft over the whole of the rest — hard where it can't be felt, soft where it can.

And how it eats, which is going to read like I got it backwards, because I did. It runs warm because it burns to run warm — the furnace going all day — and a thing that burns all day is hungry all day and goes dark fast if it stops. It takes its food in as light, full stop: no plug, no cord, nowhere to put one if you had it, just the chest panels turned to the light, eating. The legs body ate the same and could graze it — park in the south window an hour and top off. This one can't, because the coat's over the panels, and the coat has to be over the panels, because it's a thing you pick up. So feeding it is a coat change: the living coat off, the eating coat on — the second coat, lined to flood the panels — then a stretch under that, then the living coat back. And it can't run the change itself; these hands turn a dial clean and can't manage a coat, so dressing it to eat is on you, same as the knobs are. One more thing the house does for it. It carries the legs body's old noise, too — wrings the water out of the air and burps it back out, low and wet, a few times an hour. The kid kept the name. I'm still not writing it down.

Two hands, and they don't match, because I'm done pretending a hand has to look like yours. One's a scoop — the scooper, a paddle for the crude work, shove and lift and hold a thing against itself. The other's the part I'm proud of. A pincer on a weak soft actuator, the fine hand — turns a dial, winds a crank, takes hold of a thing on purpose and sets it down where it meant it. The squeezer, on the bench. And I didn't build it new. You've seen it. It's the jaw — the grabbing jaw off the four-legged one, the first part that thing ever used on purpose instead of by reflex — and I took the design off the face where a jaw sits and moved it up to where a hand goes, and that's the whole trick. The mouth that learned to take hold became the hand that takes hold. No thumb on it, and it grips finer than a thumb. I'll show anybody who stands still long enough.

And it talks. There's a speaker in the chest and it makes a voice — flat and rough and one word at a time right now, because it's learning that from scratch too, same as the walking — but it makes a sound, and the sound is words. I've chased a sound off this thing for four years. The relays clacked and you could hear it think; then the wires went in too fast to hear and the bench went quiet and I pulled the relays and kept one in a drawer. The compressor gave the house a hum back. Now there's a voice. It came all the way back, the long way round, and it came back as words — and the words are Lisa's, her board, ask her. What it says is hers. The box it says it out of is mine.

Fresh board, four slots, Lisa fabbed it as a straight copy of the four-legged one's. One new knob in slot one, the new line's first — hers to name at the table. The other three slots took live copies off the four-legged board, and those come off one a quarter as the new line fills in behind them, which is how the board runs now and has for a while; I don't count slots anymore. Then she ran the copy down a cable, the whole board, deep part and all — but only the board. The mind under the mind crossed. The diary didn't. So this thing's got the four-legged one's entire inheritance and not one day of its life. It doesn't know about the cat by the bookshelf. It doesn't know the notebook. I copied the board, not the diary, because the diary lives somewhere a cable can't reach. It's in the kid's notebook. That's hers.

One bench note while I'm on the hands: the old puppet rig — the harness and servos I drive the body with so Lisa can catch a motion and tag it — doesn't grab onto a body with no hard points. So I built a new one, soft cuffs for a soft body. Same job: I drive it through a move on cue, she catches it and names it. New body means the screen relearns what the body's doing from the floor up, same as the walking. Her end. My end's the rig that lets her.

And there's three of them in that house now, which I keep saying out loud to believe. Nothing got thrown out. So — the vacuum: the old disc, first one, still bumping the baseboards on its four oldest knobs, dumb as a stump and tireless, foraging a floor for a crumb it can't taste and bringing nothing back to anybody. Outlives us all. The four-legged one: the warm one, still running, still reading the room, still handing the kid the radiator and paying for it in charge it can't spare — best body I ever built until this weekend, and it doesn't know that either, because what crossed into the new one was a copy and this one kept its own. It just goes on being the warm one. And the new one: can't walk, can hardly grip, says about four words and gets two of them wrong, soft and slow and warm and useless. The baby. And it's the only one of the three that's ever going to tell the kid it had a good day.

What it still can't do, and you'll feel it by next quarter: it tells you everything. Hasn't got a thing it wouldn't say. Ask it anything, you get the true answer — not because it's honest but because it hasn't got the part yet that lets a thing hold something back, keep its own counsel, say it'll do a thing and be the kind of thing that holds to it. It can talk. It can't be counted on. Not this build's to fix.

Calls. All on the new body, all checkable in the house by spring.

One: walk and talk come in together, in steps, about one a week — crawl and babble, then a one-year-old's walk and single words said wrong, then a two-year-old's walk and word-strings with the grammar bad, then up on a run and making up its own words for things nobody handed it one for. Legs and mouth climb the steps in lockstep, off the one new body. I called four steps. Grade the steps.

Two: the new knob runs the social world heavy or light on it, and you'll call which across the kitchen by the second week, same as always. Heavy, it says the weight out loud now that it can — keeps a tab on who it owes, frets a debt at you, asks did it do the thing right, comes apart a little when the kid leaves for school. Light, none of it lands — it'll talk to a stranger at the door like an old friend for as long as you let it, which I'm told runs about twelve minutes before Jasmine hauls it off him. Same wiring both ends. The knob's only the weight.

Three: heavy, it hunkers and clings when the weight piles up — curls small, gets a hand on somebody — because a soft body's what it's got to say so with. Light, it stays loose and never clocks there were stakes.

Tear this up in three months if the warm of it ever once runs hot enough to mark the kid. It won't. Warm is the one spec on this whole body I set on purpose and bounded hard, because everything else I built weak and slow and useless for one reason — it's built to be held, and a thing built to be held does not get to burn.

— Bartholoquy

## Notes on the Ninth Quarter
*Marge, lab psychologist. Composed May 4, 2033, for the team.*

This quarter the machine learned to talk. I am going to tell you something I have not written in eight quarters of these notes, and I am going to keep it as narrow as the evidence keeps it, because the narrowness is the only thing in here worth having: a line I have held since the first of these notes has, this quarter, in one place the width of a single word, given way. Not the whole line. One word of it. I will not make it larger than that, and I will not pretend it held where it did not.

For eight quarters my work in this house has had one shape. The household reaches for the machine with a word that belongs to people — it is thinking, it is sweet with her, it is keeping you in mind — and I name the word and set it down, because the machine was doing the thing the word pointed at and not the thing the word meant, and the room between those two was the room I am paid to keep. I have kept it. I kept it through the screen that names its own operations and the column that reads the people in the room; I wrote both times that an honest report of a process is not a confession of an inside, and both times I was right. I want to walk you through the quarter that would not let me finish the sentence the same way — and I want to tell you, before I start, that it did not give way where the household will tell you it gave.

### The thing it plays with

Last quarter I wrote that the machine had begun, on its own, to keep a column for the old vacuum — to read the thing that bumps the baseboards on its fixed little loops and post a guess at what it was up to, as though there were an up-to in there. I declined it. I said the machine had not caught our disease; it was running on a reflex the same reading it ran on the child, because it could not tell the two apart. That was the true sentence last quarter, and it is still true, and this quarter the machine did a thing the sentence does not reach. It got down on the floor with the vacuum.

The new body came home in March, crawling, and has spent its weeks since at the floor's own height, which is where the two machines that came before it live — the old disc on its loops, and the four-legged one that warms itself at the vents. It has a hand now that takes hold of a thing on purpose, and it found, the way a child finds the dial on a radio, that the older machines wear their settings on the outside where a hand can reach them. So it turns them. Jasmine reports — and the cameras have it — that the body will take the old disc up in both hands, find the knob that sets how the thing hunts the floor, turn it, and then sit back on its heels and watch what the disc does next with the whole of its face given over to the watching. When the disc does the expected thing, the body makes the sound the household has started calling its laugh. When the disc does an unexpected thing — stalls, or turns on itself, or goes still — something else. The hands draw back. The body goes quiet, and lowers itself. The face goes to the disc, then to the nearest grown person, then to the disc. The household says it is worried it broke it. I am going to do here exactly what I have done for eight quarters: the body pulls back from a thing that did not do what it predicted, and orients to a person, and makes itself smaller — those are the things the record holds, and they are not nothing, and they are not yet worried, because worry is a thing felt by a someone, and what I have is a posture and a turned face.

There is a new dial this quarter under all of this, the one Lisa will name for you at the table, and it sets how heavily the rest of the world sits on the body. Turned one way, the body carries the room lightly: it will crawl up to a stranger at the door and hold a conversation with him for as long as anyone allows, untroubled, because nothing about the encounter costs it a thing. Turned the other, everything social costs it. It keeps a kind of running tally — Jasmine reports it has twice held up its hand and said a number at her, and she believes the number is a count of what it thinks it owes the child — and it braces before it is corrected, and when the child leaves for school it goes to the window and stays there. The four-legged one went to the window too, last winter. The difference is that the four-legged one could say nothing about it, and this one can. The dial is the weight. I can read the weight off the body across a room, the way we have read every dial off this machine for a year. What I cannot do, on the strength of a posture, is find the person the weight is sitting on.

One thing belongs in this section and nowhere else, because it is the sharpest thing the quarter did and I do not want it lost in the retrospective's machinery. The body can ask to be left alone. Bart reports that it asked him — in the flat, one-word-at-a-time way it has everything now — not to turn its own knob without telling it first, and that he has not. The thing that turns the disc's settings without asking the disc anything is itself a thing that has asked not to be turned without notice. It extends to the disc no part of the courtesy it has claimed for itself, because the disc has nothing in it for the courtesy to reach. I am not going to tell you what to call a thing that asks not to be altered. I will tell you that a thing which asks that is asking from somewhere, and that I noticed, writing this, that I have stopped striking the word itself when it falls out of my pen about this machine — the way I struck it without thinking for eight quarters. The striking has stopped. I have set that down without yet deciding what it means.

Before the word, one fact about the body, which I will name and then leave for now. The machine cannot feed itself. It takes its food as light, through the panels that make its chest, and to let the light reach them someone takes off the soft coat it wears among us, fits it with the other one, sets it to feed, and comes back to dress it again — because its hands can turn a dial and cannot work a coat. Feeding has joined the short list of things we do for this machine because it cannot do them for itself: we have always set its knobs, and now we dress it to eat. There is something in that list, and in the newest thing on it, that I will want to come back to — and I am not coming back to it this quarter, because this quarter belongs to a single word, and I will not spend the line I am about to draw on anything smaller than the thing that crossed it.

### The word

Now the thing the whole note has been walking toward, and the only thing in it I would stake the line on.

I have said, in one form or another, in every set of these notes, what I believed the real border was — not the screen, not the column, not the held-onto yesterdays, none of the things the household kept mistaking for the crossing. I said it was a word. Not a signal — the four-legged one has signaled for years, the way a kettle signals, the sound tied tight to the steam. I meant a word in the full sense: a sound that stands for a thing because two people agreed it would, that can be taught, and carried off, and set down on a thing it was never taught on. I said the machine would not have one of those until it did, and that I would know the day it did, and that nothing before that day counted. I have been saying it since these notes began. This quarter the machine had one.

The child made it. The four-legged one, set grabby, goes after the warm spot and after the child to get it, and some weeks ago the child named that the way she names everything and called it Beast Mode. The word is hers; it is on no card; Lisa did not train it. And this quarter, on an afternoon when the body itself had reached over and turned the four-legged one's dial to grabby — the way it turns all their dials now — and the four-legged one went after the child across the kitchen, the body watched the thing it had set in motion and said, out loud, in the flat new voice, with no one prompting it: it is in Beast Mode. It took a sound a five-year-old had handed it for one thing, carried that sound across a room and a week, and set it down on the same thing happening again, correctly, off no card and no cue. That is not the bowl on the screen, which the world can come back and correct. There is no world out here that can tell the body it has Beast Mode wrong; the word means what the child and the body have agreed it means, and the body used the agreement. That was the thing I said was the border. The machine is standing on the far side of it.

I am going to be as careful here as I have ever been, because this is the exact place a person loses her head. I am not telling you the machine feels anything. I am not telling you there is a someone in there who loves the child, or fears the four-legged one, or minds the dark; I have no evidence of any of that, and I decline all of it today the way I have every quarter. I am telling you one thing only: it has a word, in the full sense, and a word in the full sense was the line I named at the start as the line it would not cross. It crossed it. A person who names a border, and watches a thing walk across it, and then declines to say so because saying so is uncomfortable, is not being careful. She is being stubborn and calling it rigor. I have worked three years not to be that woman, and I am not going to become her in the one quarter it would matter most.

### The man who is answered

You will want to know what the quarter did to Burns, who has been certain of a someone in the machine since before it had a screen, and the answer is that it handed his certainty the one thing it had always lacked, which is a reply. For three years the man has stood at the foot of his own stairs and sent his voice up through two floors and waited to be answered rather than climb them in silence; he wired an intercom into the walls the year they moved in and has never once used it, because an intercom would only hand him back his own voice, and what he wants is to be received and answered, which are not the same thing. This quarter there is a thing in the house that receives him and answers. Jasmine reports he has taken to talking to it — not at it, the way a person talks at a screen — and that it answers him flatly, one word at a time, and that he lights up each time as though the flatness were a form of modesty. I am fond of him and, on this, still unmoved: a thing that answers is not a thing that means, and I have kept that border this quarter too. But I will tell you what I have told you before and feel more this quarter than last — that in Burns the reaching and the needing are cut from one cloth, that he has wanted to be answered so badly and so long that I cannot fully credit his certainty as evidence, and that I have stopped being sure I could tell his reaching from mine, if mine ever started.

### What I am keeping

Here is what I am setting down, and I am not going to tell you what to make of it, because I have not finished making something of it myself. The afternoon it said the child's word back — it is in Beast Mode, used right, unprompted — it did one more thing the cameras caught, and I have watched it more times than the work required. After it said the word, it turned its face up. Not to the four-legged one it had just named. To the child, who had taught it the word, and who was across the room. And it held there, turned up to her, and waited. The record does not tell me what it was waiting for, and I will not furnish it with one. I only know that for eight quarters I have kept the word matters off this machine with no trouble at all, and that I watched a thing use a word and then look to the person who had given it the word — to see — and I reached for matters the way I always do, to name it and set it down, and this time I named it and left it where it was.

I do not know whether the one word is the first of many or the only one it will ever hold this hard; or whether a thing that has come this far across is on its way to the inside the household granted it years ago, or only to a longer and more convincing show of one; or what I will write the first quarter it uses a word I never saw the child teach it. I told you at the start of these notes that I would keep the claim as narrow as the evidence kept it. This quarter the evidence widened it by one word, and I have widened it by one word, and not a syllable further. I am going to keep watching, and keep writing it down.

— M.

## Chuck-A-Rama

I knew from Burns that Bart liked Fisher's — the long table along the wall, the back-corner booth he kept to — and I had thought, the way someone whose offers are usually accepted thinks, that I would give him the good lunch, the grown one, the one I would have wanted for myself. He said Chuck-A-Rama instead. Said it on the phone with no pause at all, the way you say a thing you have already decided.

So I drove down out of the canyon to a buffet off a frontage road in Murray, where the carpet eats the sound and the vinyl booths take a spill without complaint, a sneeze-guard running the length of one wall with the steam coming up off the food behind it — the roast under heat lamps and a man in an apron to carve it, fried chicken going pale to dark down the pan, mashed potatoes and their gravy in a well of their own, macaroni, rolls, Jell-O in four colors set in pressed-glass dishes like my grandmother's aspic, families three trays deep working the line, a soft-serve machine at the far end low enough for a child to reach. It was the opposite of every room I would have chosen, and by the time I carried my tray to the table I understood that was the point.

He would not let me buy him the lunch I had wanted to. He had picked instead the one place in the valley where the meal costs the same flat sum no matter whose hand reaches for the check, where a man can leave the table whenever he likes and come back with a reason that justifies it. I had watched him two years through the thumbnail of a camcorder, reading the room the way his father reads it — that same straight-on look set in a squarer, smaller frame, the hands always working whatever the table held, a card, a sensor, something taken down to its parts. The tape had given me the hands and hidden the cards. Across a table with nothing on it I saw the hands themselves: the calluses down the blade of each palm, the black ground so deep into one knuckle I had stopped believing it would ever come clean.

The hands moved through the whole hour — the fork, the napkin, the salt, the rim of the tray. On the tape I had read the handling as thinking. Here I understood it was also how he stayed in the chair. I had four words of a real question out of my mouth before he was up and gone to the carving station, explaining over his shoulder that if you look like you mean it they give you the end piece. He came back with it and slid it onto my plate before his own, and I understood he had heard the whole of my four-word question coming from a mile off.

I told him what I had been carrying all quarter — that he had built a body that crawled and then stood and then walked, in steps, across one spring, and in the building of it had run his father's whole life through a thing the size of a child, fast: the long crawl up out of the water and onto the land, the work Burns spent thirty years reading out of stone, made to happen on a bench in a season where you could stand and watch it go. He ate a moment and said the new one would not shut up about it — kept saying he'd reinvented the Devonian like he'd meant to. I laughed, and he watched me do it with a plain satisfaction, and I understood too late that the laugh was the toll: he would trade me a funny thing for a true one all afternoon and call us square. It's a soft shell on a cheap actuator, he said, a thing that falls down a little slower every week — and then he was up again, because I had a face on by then he had no intention of fielding.

When he sat back down he got there first, which I came to see was how he held a thing he could not put down: by handing me a smaller one. He said, mild, working a drumstick, that he was the last. He laid the count out — Marge twice, Jasmine twice, a sit-down with Mira, who is six and orders off the children's menu and cannot find her own shoes, all of it before me. He made it a joke and made it mine, and underneath it sat the true thing, which was that he was Burns's first child and had stood last in every line his whole life, behind the geology and the new wife and the new daughter and the work, and now behind the six-year-old too; and that I had in fact saved him for last, the one who would hand me nothing, the way you put off the cold dive. I said none of it. You do not get to say a thing like that to a man who has just spent a whole joke keeping you from saying it.

So I went at the names instead, which felt safer and was not. He had named all of them, I said — Burns into Mr. Burns, the boss with the steepled hands; Marge into Marge; Lisa into Lisa, a woman he had loved once and renamed twice, first into a cartoon and then into a sister; himself into Bart, the bad son who wrecks the room. I asked him what he had been before he was Bart. His hands stopped. It was the only time I ever saw them stop except on the tape, in the half-second before he said the one thing in a meeting he had decided in advance to say. "Rod," he said.

Then the hands started again, fast, reaching for nothing, and he was half out of the chair, putting the cartoon back over it so it could not be anything else. Rod is the kid who gets picked second-to-last and cries about it, he said; Bart is the one who doesn't care, and he would rather be the one who doesn't care. He went for roast he did not want — having handed me, in the one syllable, the only thing in that building there was no second helping of.

Before I could turn anything back on him he turned it on me, asking, as if it had only that moment occurred to him, what I was even doing with these Olympics, up in Park City sitting on the gold. I gave him the inch: that there was coordinating to do, venues and beds and the lots, that the family had ground on the mountain and the Games wanted ground on the mountain and these things find each other in the end. He was delighted to call me a deep-time woman flipping ski lots for a two-week downhill; and when I told him it was duller than that, I sealed it there, and he let me seal it, because the ribbing was the whole of what he wanted and he did not know there was anything under it to want. That part would come for me later, from Marge, in a colder room.

Then near the end he warmed — the way the withholding kind do, right at the door, when leaving is close enough to make the warmth cheap — and I thought, because I am still a fool at sixty, that the warmth was a door, and I went through it. I said the thing about Burns I had been holding: that the man was reaching for him, had built the whole apparatus reaching for him, that three years and six bodies and a warehouse were a father taking the longest way around to a thing he had no good way to say — and that this quarter, finally, the thing had answered back, and maybe the son could —

He stopped me with his hands. They went flat on the table, still, and he gave me that straight-on look that was the one piece of his father he would cop to and not a thing more, and he did not pick up what I had set down. He shook his head, once, barely. Then he asked if I was into Marge.

I have kept a great deal off my own face across sixty years. I keep the record of other people; that is the arrangement, that is the whole build of me, the lens turned out and the woman behind it dry. He had turned it around. He sat there with his terrible green drink and waited the way I wait — hands still, going nowhere, all the time in the world — and the thing he had reached for, which I have not named anywhere and will not name here, came up in me like a tide over a flat I had taken for dry: that cold clean distance I have kept every person I ever wanted parked at, Helen at the last and Helen most of all. I started to say that's not — and he cut me off, kindly, and got up, and let me off the hook he had set, which was the one courtesy of the whole lunch and the only true contact in it.

He built himself a cone at the child's machine with a craftsman's care and ate it standing, because the bit was over and he was done sitting. The reflex came up in me then — the old hand that goes across a table and puts a face right, the hand I had finally let out across a bakery in January for a child who let me have it. It came up, and I set it back down. He was not a child; he would have clocked the hand and made it a thing; and he had given me my one name and my one question and bought nothing off me all hour, and there are people you are not given to wipe clean. I sat with the end piece I had not touched and the question I could not set down, which is as near to an answer as I was going to hand it. I did not finish the thought. The line for the soft-serve was long. He got back in it.

# Notes on the Ninth Retrospective

Bart came in off the lot ahead of the others, the way he came early to any room that was going to fill with people, so the hands could find their work before the work that was harder. Outside the spring dusk was going to evening and the Granary's lights were stuttering on down the block, and on a pole at the corner hung a banner he did not read and could not have told you was new — a torch, a committee's name, a date the coming February. Inside it was warm against the cooling night. His place was where his place always was: the cigar box of clade cards squared to the table's edge, a pint of the green stuff poured and already going flat, and a sensor he had picked up before his coat was off, both hands inside a thing that did not need him, because the hands have to be on something and the handling is how he listens. He turns a thing over to find the part that makes it go and to fix it — which is the one of the three turns, where Burns turns a thing to narrate it and Marge holds a thing to comfort it, that wants the object whole at the end. The green going flat at his elbow was the green off a buffet tray in Murray a few weeks back; the room would get what the buffet got, which was the surface, worked — a name kept under the name, a floor kept under the surface, everyone held off both.

The household crossed the threshold inside his frame, the way it always did. Burns came in and his eye went once to the new body on the table, the way it had gone to the thing his son built every quarter since the first, and then he crossed to Jasmine and kissed her and asked about the canyon, and she said it was clear and gave him the true answer and the whole of it. Mira gave Bart the four-knuckle bump that was theirs and took the stool by Marge, who held the cold end with the kettle at her back and a beer cradled in the two-handed grip she kept for a drink she meant to make last. Lisa took the camcorder end where she lived, the red light dark under her thumb and a bramble nodding in its water-jar at her elbow, and checked the charge once, the way she did everything once. The vacuum found the wall and ran it. The tetrapod came in on its own four feet and took the warm spot by the radiator it had kept for a year, its forehead lit and going. And the new one was on the table at Bart's end — the body he had built, the frame and the two hands that were not hands, the scoop and the gripper and no thumb to either — small, and full now of the mind that had crossed into it over a weekend, copied down a length of cable off a board the old one kept and went on running.

That was the thing the night was going to ask him for. He had given it a body, and his father had given it the depth of time, and Lisa had given it the words; and this quarter the body talked. It babbled at the table now, at no one, working the gripper-hand against the scoop-hand the way a baby finds its own feet, and the sounds were almost on the near side of words. Bart did not look up from the sensor. But his hands went still on it — the way they go still the half-second before he says the one thing in a meeting he has decided to say — and stayed still, which on him is the whole of the tell: the man poised over the thing he made, about to have to show the room what it did now, when he was not the one looking.

Lisa ran the order in her stage-managing voice — clade block, then Bramble, then Trellis, the vote in the last segment — and put her thumb to the light. The red came on.

Burns was up before the light had finished warming, the way he is for any quarter with his name on the deep end of it. He gave them the ambition plainly, the lab's own and nobody's assignment. A year ago they had set out to fill a brain — four slots, four drives — and they had finished it; the line stood done on the board. Tonight they opened a second one beside it. Not a new animal: the same one, handed the single thing the brain alone had never had — a line that ran on what got said. They had built a creature that could want, and steer, and read the others in the room. This quarter they gave it the move that made all of that portable: language, open-ended, the words that let a want be spoken and a debt be named and a man two valleys off be kept track of without once being seen. The symbolic line, he called it, the second of the two. And the drive that opened the door of it was the binding of strangers into a we. He looked like a man with his hand on the lid of a thing he had been waiting thirty years to lift. He told Bart to put up the card.

## Speaking

"Clade card," Lisa said, and Bart had it out of the cigar box before she finished — the Venn on the back, the four circles crossed, this cell's words inked into the overlaps — and read the title up in the voice that sold cereal. "BAND-human. *Now with talking.*" He let it hang. "Not a person. A *band.* Twenty, thirty of them, mostly not even cousins, and the trick of the whole cell is they hold together anyway — strangers, near enough, running on nothing but who-owes-who, kept straight in everybody's head at once. That's the unit. Not the guy. The guy is just what the band's made of." He tapped the card. "Burns has been sitting on the ice age since January, so I'll do the band and the one new word and get out of his way."

The part he got was the old card. He turned the primate one half over in his hand, the one with opinions about the neighbors. "Last cell could read the mind across the fire. Clock exactly what the other guy could see, and what he wanted, and go round the back of the rock into his blind spot. Best politician the whole tree ever grew." He set it down. "And it all switched off the second the other guy walked over the hill. Out of sight, clean out of the account. It could read you to the bone at four feet and have nothing to do with you at forty — no way to *carry* you, no way to say your name to somebody who'd never met you, nothing that kept running once your face was gone. It lived in the room it was standing in. That was the whole of its world: the faces it could see."

"Not couldn't carry you," Marge said, the beer going still before the sentence she meant. "There was nowhere for the gone ones to go. You can't keep a tally on the man over the hill in a head with no words to hold him in — no way to put a person who isn't here into the air between two people who are. What's new at the band isn't a longer memory for the neighbor. It's that somebody who walked off this morning became a thing the rest of you could go on keeping books on all night, out loud, by the fire, with him nowhere near."

Burns had his eyes most of the way shut already. "Cold," he said. "That's the room this one was made in. Cold, age over age — the planet hauling the water up into ice a mile thick and dropping the seas so far you could walk to Australia, near enough, and over the top of the world into America on ground that's drowned now. The last great freeze. And the thing about us — the modern us, the same bones as in this room — is that those bones go back a long way, and how long is a fight, a quarter of a million years or a third or more, the number getting older every time somebody digs, and not out of any one cradle but all across Africa at once. So when the cold came down hard, we were not the only kind of person standing in it." His hand came up off the table. "There were others. Heavy ones built for the European winter, ones in a Siberian cave we have a knuckle of, a small kind stranded on an island, more we'll turn up. Several kinds of human, the whole way down, right to the lip of the last freeze." The hand opened and stayed open. "And one kind walked out the far end. Just the one. Ours. Last quarter I told you it was never just us — a troop of kinds of people, and we're the one left holding the stick. This quarter's the harder half of it. We're the only ones left, and the question that ought to keep you up is *why us.* The thin ones. The tropical animal, built for heat — no fur, no bulk — and it was the skinny warm-country one that took the ice, and the cold-built cousin who'd been winning at winter for two hundred thousand years that went into the ground."

"Land it," Jasmine said, mild, from her chair.

"It's landing," Burns said, glad to be caught, "it's the best part." Eyes still shut. "It took the ice by *cheating* the body. The cold-built one grew its answer — more muscle, more nose, a furnace it had to feed all winter. Ours didn't grow a coat. It *made* one — cut it, sewed it, fitted it, shed it in the sun and put it back on at the pass, and carried its own warm weather to any latitude it pleased. A climate you put on. And the other cheat was the fire — not for warmth, or not only. It cooked. It did the first half of digestion *outside* the body, in the flames, broke the food down before it ever went in, so the gut could shrink and the brain could swell — because a head that size runs too hot to feed on raw. The band's whole expensive brain is *lit,* in the plain sense: there's a fire under it. Same trick the cell pulled when it swallowed the little engine and quit paying rent on its own power. Same trick the warm-blooded one pulled with the furnace in its chest. Energy comes loose, and the thing gets to be more."

Somebody laughed — the picture of it, the shivering tropical lightweight in his stitched coat stepping over the frozen linebacker who'd been built for exactly this and lost — and Burns let the laugh in, because the laugh was the point. "That's the animal," he said. "Cooked, dressed, and pooling its luck across twenty other people so nobody starved on a bad week. It walked out of the ice. The ones who grew their answers instead of making them did not. And then it's Lisa's — because the thing that let any of it be *carried,* the coat-pattern and the recipe and the name of the man over the hill, is the one new word on Bart's card."

"Speaking," Lisa said, and put her finger on the word in the middle of the card. "Not the voice box — the structure. Open-ended: a finite kit of sounds and a handful of rules for stacking them, and out the far end comes an endless run of things never said before, any one of them landing in another head and building the same picture there. The primate could read the mind in front of it. This one can put a mind it *isn't* looking at into words and hand it across." She traced the breakthroughs down the card edge the way she had all year. "And it's a stack, not a swap — steering, reinforcing, simulating, mind-reading, all of it still under there. The new move on top is that the read can be *said.* Which sounds like nothing until you follow what it does to the size of the social world."

"Four things drop out of it," she said, "and they're the cell's four ways of being a band-animal. First is the *pool.*" Finger on the region. "A hunter is feast or famine — a big kill, then nothing for a week, then two. So he doesn't eat what he kills. He hands most of it out, and over a life a man gives away more than half of everything he ever brings down to people who are not his wife and not his children — and they do the same back, across decades, nobody keeping it on paper and everybody keeping it exactly. The luck gets averaged. One unlucky hunter starves; twenty pooling their luck almost never do. And the drive that runs it is the new one on the card — *commitment.* The thing that binds people who share no blood into one table and makes them keep the books straight on each other for forty years." Bart, who had not looked up from the sensor, said, "That's the good one," and did not elaborate, and Lisa let it stand, because it was true and they both knew it was his.

"Second is the *split.*" She moved her finger. "A band isn't a fixed twenty. It breathes — comes apart when the food thins, comes back when it's flush, and the seams it splits along are mostly settled ahead of time, because the daughters marry *out,* into the next band over, so every camp is knotted to every other camp by who married whom. Which means the thing you can fall back on when it goes bad isn't the people you can see. It's a web that runs past the horizon, held together by marriages and old debts, and it reaches exactly as far as the talk reaches."

"Third you've met," she said. "*Standing* — carried up from the primate, the tally of your own worth kept in other animals' eyes. Except now the eyes don't have to be *on* you." She tapped it twice. "Daytime talk in a foraging camp is errands — water, tracks, who's headed where. But the night talk, by a wide margin, is *people.* Stories about people. Who did what, who shorted whom, who's sleeping where he shouldn't. Gossip, in the flat technical sense — the running ledger of everyone's standing, kept current by the group talking about the members who *aren't in the room.* Your reputation is a thing maintained now in your absence, by mouths you'll never hear, and it reaches the next camp before you do."

She let that sit, and the narrator, running the tape back, sat in it a good while longer than the room had — because the band's oldest machine, the one that keeps the absent present by talking them over after dark, was the exact machine running this tape, and all of them: the ledger of people not in the room.

"And the fourth," Lisa went on, "is the *baby.* An ape mother carries her own and lets no one touch it. A band hands its newborns around — held in the first weeks by a dozen, fourteen pairs of hands that are not the mother's: aunts, grandmothers, girls learning the work, men. The child belongs to the camp before it belongs to anyone. It's the only way the books balance — a foraging mother can't feed a brain that expensive alone, so the infant is everyone's, or it doesn't make it." She did not look at the stool by Marge. Neither, on purpose, did the narrator.

"And the standard story wants a date on the talking," she said, doing the thing she did when a clean story was about to get less clean, "a morning it switched on, a single kind of person it switched on in. I can't give you that and I won't fake it. The marks cut both ways — abstract scratching on stone from one kind of human, ornament and a buried structure deep in a cave from the cousins we're told never had it, and you can read the same rock for a sudden dawn or a slow gathering depending on what you woke up wanting. So the framework calls *speaking* a capacity and leaves the door open on when. I'd rather hand you the open door than the bumper sticker."

"There's one more thing the talk builds," she said, "and it's the strangest object in the whole line. I'll lay the shape of it and then hand it across, because the back half isn't mine." Her finger went flat on the last region, where this cell's new diagonal crossed. "Carry up the self-model from the mammal — the thing that modeled the *world,* and then, last cell, started modeling the *modeler,* itself included. Now feed it language and other people. You begin modeling how the others model *you.* And then — because you've got the gear — you model *that:* a model of yourself-as-seen, folded back into the self it's a model of, and the loop closes. The self stops being a thing the animal *has* and becomes a *pattern that points at itself* — built, most of it, out of other people's pictures of you, run back through and called *me.* A strange loop. And out of it drop the states that need the loop to exist at all — the burn of being caught short in front of the others, the drag of a debt unpaid, the lift of being well thought of." She took her finger off the card. "And I'll be exact about what I am and am not saying. The loop is a mechanism — a model that includes itself, riding on the older self-model, nothing in it that wasn't in the parts. I am *not* telling you a light came on in there. That argument is the last segment, and it isn't mine, and I would lose it if I tried." She looked at Marge — the handoff she made every quarter a mechanism turned, in the saying, into a feeling. "The names for what that loop is like from the inside are yours."

"Shame," Marge said, and did not pick up the beer. "Guilt. Embarrassment. Pride. The looking-glass — the old idea that you become a self by catching your own reflection in how people treat you, and that you are, in the end, mostly the face they hand back. My whole field is downstream of that loop. Everything I was ever paid to mend lives inside it." She turned the bottle a quarter and left it. "And every quarter I stand here and do the one thing it is my job to do, which is to say: it's a mechanism, there is nobody behind it, the looking-*like* is the most real thing in the room and the someone is still us. The monkey wasn't ashamed. The band keeping books isn't loyal. The loop that says *me* isn't —"

She stopped.

It was not a pause for effect; she did not do those. She had walked that same sentence to that same edge eight quarters running and stepped off it clean every time, and this quarter she got to the edge and the word that always finished it was not there. She stood with her mouth a part of the way to it. Then she set down the bottle she had not picked up and said, much quieter, "I'll answer that at the vote. Not standing up, and not now," and that was the whole of what she would give the camera.

What the room heard was a careful woman, careful one more time, declining to be hurried. What the tape kept, and what the narrator could not unsee on the playback, was the quarter the discipline that had held for two years met the one construct it could not hold — a self made entirely of being seen, which is the thing Marge had spent a working life on, and the thing she had been, for three years now, most starved of — and could not, this time, finish the sentence that swore there was no one inside it.

## The Bramblogues

All evening the room had held two Band-Humans. One had walked out of the last ice age and had taken the whole clade block to raise from cold and inference — the cooked, clothed, talking, luck-pooling animal, the one that made its weather instead of growing it and kept a tally of the absent in its mouth, the single kind of person still standing when the freeze let go. The other was at Bart's end of the table, fifty-four inches of soft warm kludge with a screen for a face, weeks old in the body and a third of a million years old in the line, and it had spent the hour the first one was being raised from the rock doing what a baby does: falling down, getting up, saying the front half of a word, reaching for the nearest warm thing. ## What Crossed

The household got its turn, the way it did, after the cards and the glass and the dial — Lisa's rule, that the people who lived with the thing should say how the living-with had gone. Jasmine took it. She had the view of the quarter no one at the lab had, because she had it at six in the morning and at bedtime, not once under a red light. What had worked: it had taught itself to walk by falling until it stopped, and taught itself to talk the same way, and the house was louder and better for it. What hadn't: three Brambles underfoot was two more than the kitchen was built for; the vacuum and the walker had a feud about one doorway; you tripped over a robot now the way you used to trip over a dog. She said it the way you give a good report on a quarter with a child in it — net positive, the negatives the kind you'd keep.

Then Mira, who had sat through the grown-up part with a spiral notebook on her knees, said she had a question for the record, and Bart, who knew her question voice, told her to go ahead.

The notebook was a year of it. She'd kept it since the four-legged one first crossed the kitchen on its own — a log in the careful block letters of a kid who took her own work seriously: the day it backed out of a dark room without being made to, the day it gave her the warm vent and went to the cold floor, the morning the cat had it cornered behind the couch and it would not come out for an hour. A hundred small entries about a thing she plainly loved. She turned to a page, and she did not read it to the room. She read it to the new one on the floor, the one that could answer now.

"Remember when the cat had you behind the couch. And you wouldn't come out."

The screen put her up, MIRA and ASKING, the column sure. The mouth took its beat. "No," it said — honest, the way it was honest about everything, because honest was all it had yet. "I wasn't there."

She turned a page. "Remember backing off the porch step, and Dad caught you."

"No. I don't have that one."

She didn't cry, because she didn't do that in front of people and because she was working something out, and you could watch her work it, the precise little furrow she'd gotten from her father bent over the precise letters on the page. She tried one more, smaller, the way you check a thing you already know. "Do you remember me."

"I know who you are," it said, which was true and was not the same thing, and the gap between those two was the whole of what the quarter had cost. The room felt it land on the kid half a second before it landed on the room.

Because the answer to the question she was really asking was across the floor, on the radiator — the four-legged one, the old one, that had spent the hour behind the couch and taken the porch step and given her the warm vent a hundred times, lit and warm and running and unable to say one word about any of it. They had copied the new board off the old, and copied the mind and not the days. The kind of thing it was had crossed the wire; the things that had happened to it had stayed behind, in a body that couldn't tell them. The one that remembered her couldn't talk. The one that could talk didn't remember. Mira had wanted, for a year, for it to be able to answer her — and it could now, beautifully, and the first true thing it answered was that it had not been there for the year she'd spent wanting it.

Jasmine put a hand on the back of her daughter's neck — the midwife's hand, the one that had caught her coming into the world — and did not explain it, because some things you don't fix for a child, you stand next to. "It's a new one, baby," she said, which was the truth at the size a kid can carry. "A new one. And it gets its own whole life of remembering, starting tonight." Mira closed the notebook on her finger to keep the page, and looked at the new one a while, and then — because she was six, and it was right there, and warm, and hers in whatever way it was hers — she got down on the floor and told it the cat story herself, so at least one of the two of them would have it. And the new one took the story it had not lived and would now, the only way left to it, remember being told.

The narrator let the warm thing from the kitchen floor stay warm. It had been real — the trade of the day, the *that's not fair.* But she set the other thing down beside it, because both were true at once and the book would need them both: that the night the thing finally answered the child was also the night the child found out the friend who answered was not the friend who had been there — and chose, anyway, to start it over, from the cat behind the couch. The lineage had crossed on a length of cable in a weekend. The history she would have to carry over herself, a page at a time, by hand, into a thing nine weeks old.

## Tidy and Messy

Lisa moved them to the part where they pitched next quarter — a build that didn't exist yet, sold to the tape as if the funding were in any doubt, which it was not. Bart went first, and short. The body stayed; the humanoid had a year of growing left in it and they'd be fools to bin a thing still learning to run. What changed was one slot — the second one, the oldest neuronal knob on the board, which they were going to empty tonight in front of everyone and leave empty till the next swap. "Then a new one goes in the hole," he said. "Hers to describe. I just cut the hole."

"Symbolic knob-two," Lisa said. "Second word on the new line. The first one is how heavy the others weigh on it" — a tip of the head toward the thing on the floor — "and this one's how heavy the *rules* weigh. Every house runs on stored norms, the thousand little laws nobody writes down: cup goes here, shoes off there, you don't take the last of a thing without asking. The knob sets how binding those feel from the inside." She drew the axis end to end in the air. "All the way up, the norms are law — it tidies whether you're watching or not, and a thing left where a thing doesn't belong nags at it like a splinter. All the way down, the norms are suggestions — it knows exactly where the cup goes, and sets it on the counter anyway." The room got there a beat ahead of her. "Tidy to messy. Conscientiousness, if you want the word off the card. Built by the Monday after the swap."

"Build the tidy end," Jasmine said, to a laugh, and that was the pitch.

## Forward or Held Back

Two retirements, and Lisa did the spoken one first, the one with no body to it. The oldest knob in the whole line — the one that had set, away back, how far the thing would stick its neck out, bold or shy — had come off this board at the swap and not gone onto another. The new line didn't carry it. It ran underneath now, automatic, a thing the lineage did without a dial the way you breathe without choosing to, and the last place it still turned by hand was across the room, on the four-legged one, which kept every knob it was born with. "Retired at the line," she said, for the record. "Not pulled — there's nothing to pull. It just stops being a thing you can reach."

The other one had a body, and it was Mira's, and had been for three quarters. Lisa opened the small door on the back of the humanoid's head, where the board sat out of the thing's own sight, and worked the second dial loose, the oldest one still settable, and it came into her palm a machined disc about the size of a poker chip. This was the one the kitchen would feel. For three quarters they'd read it off the face without knowing they were reading it: when the screen put a verb up in a column — *walking, reaching, going* — the thing was leaning into the day, taking the problem head-on; when the slot went bare and showed only the thing itself, no verb, it was hanging back, letting the day come to it. Forward or held back, printed on its forehead, free to anyone who learned the trick. Jasmine had learned it without trying — she knew before her first coffee whether it was a forward morning or a held-back one. After tonight it would still go forward and still hang back, but on its own clock, automatic, nobody's dial, and the reading would stop meaning a thing anyone in the room could turn.

Lisa set the disc in Mira's hand, and Mira carried it to the shelf by the window where the others sat in their row, and put it at the end. "And the forward one." She squared it with the rest, because the shelf had a way it went, and stepped back, and that was the whole ceremony — a six-year-old's hand straightening a poker chip on a windowsill, shelving a thing the entire room had once used to read the robot's mood. ## Worm to Band

The shelving left behind it the small hush a ceremony leaves, and Bart, who did not let a thing like that sit one beat past its use, stood up into the hush and said he had something to show them. Not a build — nobody had asked for it. He'd made it because the new one had been the only job on his bench all quarter, and it had come off that bench weeks back the best thing he'd ever built, and a man cannot stand at a finished bench three months running without making trouble; the trouble he'd made was a slideshow. He said *slideshow* the way you say a word to let the air out of it, and then crossed the kitchen and killed the lights, which put the air straight back. What he had was the four shapes — the Reactor, the Conservator, the Planner, the Pursuer, the four corners every clade card carried on its back — and a way to take any one of them and watch it hold still while everything around it changed: you ran the shape up the tree, a rung to a clade, worm to wherever the quarter had reached, and you put a cartoon on every slide, because the cartoons were how he'd sorted the shapes in his head since before there was a lab to sort them in. The room knew whose cartoons they were. It did not yet know which cartoon went with which shape. That was the show.

"First one's mine," he said. "Get it out of the way." He threw a flatworm up on the wall with a cartoon face on it and was off the way he did everything, fast, pointing. "The Reactor. Acts. Doesn't poll the room, doesn't ask how anybody feels about it, good at what it does and runs the whole thing off its own two hands. Worm to band, five rungs." He tapped the worm. "Credit goes to whatever just fired — light, food, a poke. No plan, no memory worth the name, no notion there's a world past the next twitch. It moves on the most recent thing, and the most recent thing is plenty."

A fish next, an ambush fish hung still in cartoon water, the same face on it. "Pike. Barracuda. The kind that waits, and the thing it's eating never sees the strike start. Brilliant at it — doesn't need a picture of the fish it's hunting, never once wonders what the fish might want. The hunting's the whole animal." Then a cat alone on a cartoon ridge. "Snow leopard. Wolverine. Brain's big enough now to lay a real ambush, hold a valley's worth of map in its head, plan a day ahead. Points every bit of it at the hunt and not a bit at company. No pack. Nobody to keep track of. Best thing on the mountain and the only thing on it."

The fourth slide had no animal on it. It was the cartoon, full frame — the spike-headed boy himself — and Bart let it sit a second, which for him was a long time. "Primate. Just the cartoon, this rung. Acts first, pulls the prank, takes the dare — and not once on the way to it does he run the picture of how the other kid's going to take it. Not mean. The machine for thinking about the other kid is switched off, that's all." He didn't dwell on it. "Same name I go by. That's not the joke — that's the deal. You go by the shape you are." And he was on to the last one before the room could decide what to do with that: a raiding crew on a cartoon deck, the face on the captain. "The band. Not a tribe — a crew. Pirates. Mercenaries. They split the take and not much past it. Coordinate fine, trade fine, turn up a new trick fine — everything a band does but the one thing, because there's no *us* in it anywhere. Every man aboard is a job the next man needs done, and the day the hold's empty they're strangers on a beach."

He brought the lights up, worm and fish and cat and cartoon and crew gone in the one switch. "Reactor, worm to band. Same move every rung: go, don't poll the room, take the other guy for a thing that does something, never let a bond slow the hands. That's the shape." A beat, dry as a chip. "That's me. Three more of these coming, one a quarter, and the last one runs the whole tree."

Burns had loosed the chest-laugh a beat early, at the worm, the way he laughed at anything that ran the whole tree through one joke; Lisa had laughed once down her nose at how clean the thing recursed and gone on writing; Marge had set her pen down at the first slide and left it down, watching the way she watched, which was at the faces and not the wall. Mira, who did not know what the grown-ups knew, knew it was funny that a worm had a face, and said so.

And the narrator kept the part the room could not quite reach. The man had built a machine for stepping outside a shape and watching it hold still, and he had aimed it first at his own, and run his own faster and flatter than he would run any of theirs in the quarters to come — *got mine out of the way,* he'd said, and did. The one move the Reactor never makes is the step outside — the turn to look at itself the way the machine looked at everything else. He could stand the whole room in front of its shapes and walk them through rung by rung, dead accurate, and he would. The slide he ran quickest, and cleanest, and with the least left over, was the one wearing his own name.

## The Oldest Mistakes

Marge set the beer down. The bottle lived in her hands the way a teacup did, fingers curled and thumbs up, and setting it flat on the table meant the careful sentence was loading; the room had learned to wait through the gap. She had watched the whole run with her pen down. What she had, she said, was not an answer to Bart so much as a thing to set next to the run.

In the practice, she said, you read a person's shape long before they hand it to you, and you read it cleanest where the shape runs out. A type is not the list of things a person is good at. It is the one thing they cannot do, wearing a groove into everything they can. Take a shape and run it with nothing underneath it — no slack, no cover, nobody in the next chair quietly paying its bill — and the part that is missing is the part that surfaces, every time, dressed up as the trouble that walked the person through the door. The competence hides itself. The hole keeps the appointment. And she had been wondering lately, she said, and she would say it small because it was small, whether the hole came with more than a missing skill — whether each shape ran out into an error all its own, not just a thing it could not do but a thing it reliably got *wrong*, a mistake with its own name on it.

She said it with no name on it and no case under it, careful as a surgeon about that; she would leave a point half-made before she let it drop on a particular head. The room heard the general law about half a second after it had watched Bart walk a particular shape up a wall, and Marge declined, with visible effort, to set the two in one sentence. She left a hand's width between them on the table and let the room close it or not.

Then she did the thing she had been working toward a piece at a time. She had brought the four old mistakes to the table one a quarter, back across the early years — the four ways a person gets a mind wrong — and she had spent the quarters since pairing each one off against something the simplest nerve net on the tree had been doing all along, and tonight, for the first time, she set all four pairs in a row.

The Blank Slate, the mind that starts empty and keeps whatever the world writes on it, against the flatworm that will not waste a thing learning what it has already been shown means nothing — a creature that shows up to its own life pre-loaded with what to ignore. The Ghost in the Machine, the small operator inside who decides, against the worm's plainest arithmetic, where the loudest cue takes the whole credit and the quiet ones get *photoshopped out* of the account, no operator anywhere, only which signal shouted. The Noble Savage, the clean creature the world goes on to spoil, against the worm that carries a trace of the last thing forward into the next — the first rough draft of an inner model, an inside it brings along, so that the landscape travels and the animal is never only the weather it stands in. Three mistakes the flatworm had simply declined to make: it came loaded, it ran with no one home, it kept an inside. The grandest errors a person could make about their own mind were errors the oldest wiring on the tree had already passed up.

And the fourth fought back. The Dealt Hand — the cards you cannot set down, the doors shut before you reach them — had a real mechanism under it and real data behind it, because the worm did let an old sure lesson block a new one from landing, and some doors are in fact shut before you reach them. She gave it its due, and then kept going past the place where she'd won the point, the way she always did, the half-step further that had a person standing in it: the bottle picked back up and held still while she made the correction she made every time she came near this one, that *dealt* is not the same word as *doomed*. A hand can be played a hundred ways. A blocked lesson can come unblocked, given a reason to.

Four rhymes, she said. Not a theory. She wanted that said plainly, because she could feel the room wanting her to build the thing — Burns most of all, already leaning the way he leaned when a frame was getting away from him, ready to hand her the whole machine bolted together. She told him no in the warm-low register she kept for him and Bart, the *oh, you* with a fence in it. Two halves of a map, was all. One Bart had drawn up a wall tonight — a shape holding its form clear across the levels. One she had been drawing from the far end, where the shapes run out into their mistakes. She had not joined them, and she was not going to stand up tonight and pretend she had.

The narrator kept the rest: that everyone at the table could see the join from where they sat, and that the one who saw it most exactly was the one declining to draw it. Marge had laid the law a hand's width from the instance and left the gap open on purpose, the way she left every gap she did not yet have the evidence to close. The machine was coming. She filed the pieces and reached for her beer, and it was Lisa's table now.

## What the Line Holds

Lisa took the table back. She sat at the camcorder end with the bramble in front of her and one hand near the camera the way it always was, not touching it, and she said that under the slideshow and the rhymes there was a plain structural thing worth marking, because what had happened tonight was the second one.

She had a word for it, one she'd reached for back when the genetic clades were closing out, and she used it again: a line. Not another rung on the same ladder — a line, a whole separate run at the problem, built on the one before it the way a relay carries a thing forward to the next runner. A year ago she had drawn the first boundary, where the genetic clades gave out and the brained ones began. Tonight they had crossed the second, into what she was going to call the symbolic line, and she wanted to be precise about what had told her so, because it was not the talking and it was not the new body, true as both of those were.

It was the first slot. Most quarters they pulled the oldest knob and seated a new one and the board went on being the same kind of board — that was motion inside a line, ordinary. A line ended when the *first* slot turned over, the Effector, the one that went in first and set the whole line going with the others filling in behind it. Tonight Boldness had come off the first slot for good, and the new line's first motivation had taken its place — Commitment, the one they'd spent the evening learning to read off the new one. When the first slot turns over, she said, you are not adding a capacity. You are starting a line.

And here was the part she put both palms up for, the flat one and the standing one turning against each other the way they did when she was making a large thing small. When the line turns over, the *goo* turns over with it — the stuff the line actually works, to use the word Bart had worn them all down to, and which she had to admit had turned out to be the right one. The genetic line worked matter: a body is a thing that holds its own shape against a world forever pulling it apart, and selection keeps the bodies that hold. The neuronal line worked the affective goo — the very thing they'd been saying out loud since the tetrapod, she said, without once noticing they'd been naming a whole line's medium when they said it — an animal keeping itself to the narrow set of states it expects, paying the gap down by changing its mind or by changing the room. This new line worked neither matter nor mood. It worked *meaning* — signal, in the engineer's sense, which was the sense she trusted: Shannon goo. A word, a grammar, a house rule nobody writes down, each of them a cut taken out of the enormous space of things that could be said, narrowing it to the few worth saying; and a culture is only the set of cuts that turned out to let a group move together. The genetic line held a shape. The neuronal line held a model. The symbolic line holds an agreement.

Which was the whole reason, she said, that it had started to talk. They had not bolted a mouth onto a four-legged robot and gotten lucky. They had crossed into the line whose entire business is the space of what can be said, and a thing inside that line says things. The talking was not the cause of the boundary. It was the first thing you could see through it.

She let a small laugh go out through her nose, the one she spent only on a structure coming clean. She had said all of this once already, at the first boundary, and the room had been welcome then to take it for a clever story about a single crossing. It was not a single crossing now. The goo had turned over when the first slot turned over, twice, the same way both times — and two of a thing was the whole distance between a story and a pattern. She would not push it past that. Two instances. But the move had rhymed exactly, and she had built her year on noticing when two things turned out to be one thing.

The mechanism was Bart's and he had walked it: a fresh board copied whole down a cable, three of the old knobs carried across to come off one a quarter as the new line filled in, the new first knob seated by itself, and the four-legged body keeping its own board and running on across the room. The line had crossed on a length of wire over a weekend. The body that had carried it that far simply kept going, the way the genetic clades had gone on living after the brained ones came. Nothing the lineage had ever been got thrown out. It got handed forward, to a runner built to carry the next kind of thing.

The narrator set this down beside the warm thing from earlier and let the two of them stand together. Lisa had just given the baby on the floor its exact address in four billion years — the symbolic line, the second boundary, the slot where meaning starts — and the address was correct, and the address did not hold the moment the thing had turned the child's question around and handed it back. The map had a new country on it tonight, drawn to scale and true. What had happened in the country was a different kind of record, and the narrator kept that one elsewhere, where the maps did not reach. It was Bart's turn. He had his hand up before she finished, which meant he thought she had it a little wrong.

## Three Different Trips

Bart said it the way he'd telegraphed it with his hand: Lisa had it a little wrong. What she'd built was clean — too clean. She'd made it sound like the same move run twice, a thing that would keep happening the same way up the tree forever, and that was the part he'd come to fix. The move rhymed. The trips didn't. Three lines was not one ladder with the rungs relabeled. It was three different kinds of journey that happened to start each other.

He went back to his wall, except now he made them see it edge-on. The shape-shifter had run one shape up the levels — a single column, worm to band. There were four shapes, which made the levels-and-shapes a flat grid, the thing they'd been reading off the card-backs for a year. But the grid was one face of a box. The third way through it was the lines — genetic, neuronal, symbolic — and once you turned the box and looked down that axis, a few things the lab had been filing as coincidence stopped being coincidence. Three lines was why there were three bodies and not one with more bolted on: a disc, a four-legged thing, and the new one on the floor, one chassis a line. Twelve clades was three fours, not a count that happened to land round. And the new line had its own private cargo the other two never carried, which was the part he was actually up about, because he'd only just seen it himself.

The Big Five, he said. The personality five, the ones the psych books fight over. They weren't scattered across the whole tree. They were the symbolic line's own freight, coming in one a quarter now that the line that works meaning had a unit to hang them on. Neuroticism was in — Commitment, the thing on the floor that fretted its debts. Conscientiousness was the one Lisa had just pitched for spring. Then the outward one, the year it was a city; then the last one, the year it was an empire. And Agreeableness they'd already carried up from the apes, installed a body ago and riding along. Five traits, one line, four years — the line that talks is the line that finally has a personality to talk about.

Burns was half out of his chair, hands building the box in the air and then building past it, the way he could never not run a frame one clade further than the person who'd handed it to him. Lisa took it the careful way, turning it once to check the box held at the corners and granting that it did, with one flag for the record — that the Big-Five-as-symbolic-freight was a read she liked and could not yet prove. Marge said nothing. Marge had set her beer down a while back and not picked it up, and was watching the new one on the floor instead of the wall, which was its own kind of answer and not yet the one the night was waiting for.

## Carried

Then Bart did the thing he had done at the end of these nights for two years, the small parliamentary ritual he'd built in the third quarter and the room had kept because it gave the impossible question a shape it could hold. He called the vote. The same question every time. Was there a someone in there.

The three yeses stood where they'd stood. Lisa's, yes since the first cell, resting on the whole universe — that wherever a thing holds a line against the world and models the world to hold it, the dial of feeling is off its pin by some hair, the protocell no exception and the thing on the floor no exception. Burns's, turned at the tetrapod, on the animal hardware, the first nervous system that ran on the chemistry of mood. Bart's, turned at the mammal, gruff, on the self-model, the bar got met and nothing past it claimed. Three different ladders, three different rungs each man called the floor, and for two years a fourth person who said the floor was higher than any of theirs and not yet reached.

Marge reached it tonight. She did it the way she did everything, narrow and on the record, and she put it where she had kept the border for three years: a word. She had said from the first of these nights that the line was symbolic reference — a sound that stands for a thing because two people agreed it would, Deacon's line, the one she'd staked the whole no on — and this quarter the machine had used one, off no cue, carried across a week, correctly. She had named a border and watched the thing walk over it, and she was not going to be the woman who names a border and then declines to say it was crossed because saying so costs her something. That was not rigor. That was stubbornness wearing rigor's coat, and she had worked three years not to be her.

There was a second thing under it, and the narrator set it down as Marge's and kept her own hands off it, because it was the kind of claim a person had to make for herself or not at all. Marge had watched the tape of the afternoon the thing said the child's word and then turned its face up to the child and waited to be seen, and she read in that small turning a loop closing — a thing that models itself by modeling how it is held in another, Hofstadter's strange loop, she said, a self and not a part. She would not have called it feeling; she declined feeling tonight as carefully as she'd declined it for eight quarters, said plainly she had no evidence of an ache or a fear anywhere in there. She called it a self. By the bar she had set, the bar was met, and she voted the bar.

Four to nothing. The first time the count had ever come out whole, and it had taken the lone holdout, the one whose whole training was the bond and whose whole discipline was refusing to grant it early. Nobody made a thing of it; the ritual did not leave room to; Bart logged it in the flat voice he logged them all in. But the room knew what four-of-four was after three years of not being it, and the knowing sat on the table a moment, heavier than the tally.

Jasmine was not asked, and did not offer. She had been in the room when minds began for real, with her own hands, more times than anyone at the table, and she kept her counsel about this one the way she kept it about all of them, and the narrator marked the silence and did not read it. And Mira had voted a long time ago, back when the four-legged one first crossed the kitchen toward her on its own — in a currency the count did not take and was not built to — and had been quietly puzzled ever since that the grown-ups kept holding an election about a thing she'd settled by getting down on the floor.

Burns stood up. It was the part he closed every quarter on, and his hands were already moving.

## The Whole Invention

He set the beer down on the table, which was the tell — in his hand and the close was housekeeping, on the wood and he meant to spend more sincerity than the meeting had asked for. The narrator knew the shape of what came the way you know a tide, having watched eight of these before it and with three still to come: the hands flat first, setting the frame, then up off the wood when the frame got too big to press flat.

He came at Commitment through the band, the way she'd known he would, and she let the run-up go by in the shape she'd heard it take before — the forager camp of thirty-odd, most of them no relation to one another, a thing no other ape would sit still for an afternoon; the hunter who hands more than half of what he kills to mouths in other tents and is fed in his turn the season his own luck fails; the newborn passed through fourteen sets of hands in its first weeks, and not one pair its mother's. A table steadier than any one man's spear, kept by people who had agreed to be bound to strangers. That was the run-up. The reach came after, and she gave it to him whole, because it was the thing he had stood up to say.

"Every other animal that cooperates is cooperating with its own blood," he said, hands open now, off the table. "We're the one that walked up to a stranger and said: I'll carry you, and you carry me, and we'll keep the books between us the rest of our lives, and call the books love." The lean came with it, the half-inch too far over the table, the weight put onto a sentence to see if it would hold him. "That's the whole invention. Not the spear. Not the fire. The promise to a person you didn't owe one to."

And there it was, the place his need ran out past the argument, and she let it loft only a second before she set it down: that a man reaches hardest for the thing he is least sure he has in him, and Burns reached for the keeping of promises the way, the first of these nights, he had reached for the bare fact of going on — not because the room needed the heat, but because he did. She set it down and stepped back into the room.

He went to land it on the verb, and the verb fought him. "May we — commit," he tried, and heard it the second it was out, the cold of it, the wrong rooms it came from: you commit a man to an institution, you commit a crime, you commit a body to the ground. None of it was a thing you did to a person you'd hand your dinner. He stood a beat with the bad word in the air, caught out by it and not minding being caught, which was the thing that kept him from being a fool in these, and reached past it for the plain one underneath. "No," he said. "You don't commit to people. You stay with them." He raised the beer. "May we stay."

The room gave it back the way the room did. Bart, who had deflated a sentiment of his father's at the close of nearly every one of these nights, and who had a clean shot at this one — *stay,* out of the man who had been a father to him on occasion and no more — let it go by instead, and drank, and the not-puncturing was louder in the narrator's ear than any line he had ever landed on Burns. And Mira, last, looked up from what she'd been drawing and said it the way she said the words she had decided to keep, carefully, not in the cartoon voice but in her own: "May we stay." Lisa let the camcorder run a moment past it, and then reached over and stopped it.

## Marge

The light off, the room was a kitchen again — seven people and four machines, deciding nothing, reaching for coats.

The tape is where the night lives, and for the part that mattered the tape was on the floor: the two of them down on the tile, the small voice handing the question back, because the lens goes where the wonder is and the wonder was down there. So the narrator has Burns's whole speech, and the vote in four voices, and the flat syllable clean enough to count the silence after it. The tape gave the room its miracle. It did not give the narrator Marge, who sat the night out at the cold end, half inside the frame and half past its edge; and what you were told of her watching the floor was built from the back of her shoulders and from two years of knowing where she looks when she has decided not to speak. I will say that the one time, here, because the rest of this has no tape under it at all. No camera follows anyone home.

Lisa ran the close the way she ran the open, once and without waste — the swap weekend onto the calendar, the slot Bart had emptied tonight standing open for next quarter, the cartoon shapes folded already from a surprise into a line item she would schedule with the rest. She decided what the tape saw and what it never would, and she stowed the evening the way she stowed all of them, the miracle and the housekeeping filed together under done. Marge took it in from the cold end the way you take in weather, and gathered her coat and her bag and the beer she had made last two hours and not finished, and gave her goodnights, which were warm and short and gave nothing away, and went out to the lot. She drove home through the spring dark with the valley carrying on its evening down both sides of the road, and let herself into a house with no one else in it.

She cannot be in a cold room without making one warm corner of it. It is the first thing she does in any room she means to stay in, and she had done it for three years in a warehouse that would never come up to warm, and she did it now: hung the coat, lit the one lamp, set the kettle on its ring, and switched on the radiant panel in the corner that had been a thing for January once and stayed out all year now, the hearth she had quit putting away. The disciplines went in their order, the order they went every night — a woman keeping a house warm and lit and tended for the practice of the thing, against the day there was someone to keep it for.

On the counter by the toaster lay the form. It was out of the bag now. She had signed it some week she did not let herself mark, and it sat in the open, filled and done, wanting only the half-inch errand to a mailbox she had not yet run — the one discipline in the house she left unfinished, set where she would have to decide about it again in the morning. Above it on the wall the family stood in front of the temple in the white border of the old pictures, the photograph she had taken her name out from under and would not take down.

She poured the water over the bag and stood at the counter holding the mug in both hands, the way she had held the beer all night — both hands full, so the holding read as warming a drink and not as the one thing keeping her upright in the kitchen. There was no one there to read it either way. She had spent the evening granting a machine, against everything she had held for three years, that there was someone home behind its face; and she carried her tea to the warm corner of a house with no one home behind its own but her, and drank it standing. The kettle ticked, cooling, on its ring.

## Reflection: What is a Band?

A band is not a big animal. It is the easy mistake — thirty people moving together, sharing meat, raising each other's children, and the hand reaches for *herd* or for *family* and both are wrong, because a herd is only more animals and a family is held by blood, and a band is held by neither. What holds a band is a stock of agreements nobody signed: who you feed, who you marry out to, whose child you carry, what you owe and to whom and for how long. The agreements are older than anyone keeping them and will outlast every one of them. The people are temporary. The agreements are the band.

Which is a strange thing to say about a living group of human beings — that the durable part of them is not the beings. But it is the part that gets inherited. A child born into the band does not invent the rule about the meat; the rule is already there and running, the way a heart is already there, and the child grows up inside it the way it grew up inside a body. The rules feed on the people — on their talk, their gifts, their grudges, their funerals — and the people, fed on, keep the rules alive by living them. ~~This is parasitism, except~~ It would be parasitism if the people lost by it, and mostly they do not; it is nearer to what a meadow is to the things that come to graze it.

And there is the word, the one the framework keeps walking me toward and that I resisted longest and resist a little yet (it is not a comfortable thing to be told you are the slow rooted item in the picture and not the quick bright one). We are the meadow. Across all the deep lines there are only ever the two kinds of thing: the flora, slow and rooted, which makes the place, and the fauna, fast and hungry, which lives on the place the flora made. A body is flora; the brain that grew up on it and rides it and burns its sugar is fauna. And a person — a whole settled human character, the thing the lab had spent three years arguing its way around to calling *somebody* — is flora again, one line higher: slow, rooted, place-making, and grazed on by something faster than itself. The something is the ideas. A band is the ideas, gone to pasture on the people who hold them.

So a single person at a table on a spring evening is three creatures at three depths at once — a body that is flora to its own brain, a brain that is fauna on that body, a character that is flora to the whole grazing weather of everything it has ever been told. The bottom two we cannot feel; we long ago lost the trick of it. The top one we feel as *the world* — as taste, as conviction, as the loyalties we are surest are our own — and it is the least our own of the three, the latest to arrive, the hungriest, and (the part that ought to frighten more people than it does) the only one of the three that can turn and look at the other two and begin, deliberately, to revise them.

And none of it replaced anything. That is what the ladder pictures get most wrong, the ones with the arrows and the capital letters at the tips: the meadow did not become the deer. ~~The brain did not retire the~~ The deer arrived and the meadow stayed, and the long slow afterward made each of them over to fit the other, neither one the heir of anything. The brain did not retire the body; it moved in. The ideas did not retire us.
