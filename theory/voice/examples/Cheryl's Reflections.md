# Cheryl's Reflections
---
## Chapter 0 — Evolution as Learning

Evolution and learning are the same thing.

Not analogous. The literature had been calling them analogous for sixty years, and the analogy had done a great deal of work and had also gotten in everyone's way, because the analogy was wrong by being too small. Watson and Szathmáry had written it out in 2016: selection is computation. A lineage learns across generations the way a learning system learns across training cycles. Different goo, same algorithm. The result had been on the page for fifteen years. Most working biologists had not absorbed it. Most working computer scientists had not heard of it.

Cheryl had walked out of the warehouse with this result in her front pocket, and the team did not yet know they had been selected to find it.

The math came in two flavors, depending on what kind of population you had. In an asexual population — clones plus mutation, no recombination, no decomposable parts — the math was Bayesian. The parent generation was a prior; the environment was the likelihood; the next generation was the posterior. Harper had it in one direction; Shalizi had it in the other. In a sexual population at linkage equilibrium — recombining, alleles approximately independent — the math was the multiplicative-weights update algorithm, the one used in online learning. Chastain and his collaborators had walked the proof. The two regimes got different algorithms because they were different kinds of update rule. Once you stopped treating it as an analogy and started treating it as a translation, the question became where the translated material was stored.

It had to be stored somewhere. A learning system stores its training in its weights; a lineage stores its training in its gene-regulatory architecture. Watson and his group had argued this in 2014 and shown it in simulation: phenotypes that had survived in the past became attractors of development, and the developmental memory generalized — combinations of stored modules could produce novel phenotypes fit for environments the lineage had not yet seen. The memory was creative. It was not just a record. It was a substrate.

(The formal equivalences are proved. The developmental memory and its generalization are shown in simulation. Whether they are true of biology in the wider sense is open. Cheryl held this open. She would hold it open for the team and let them close it themselves if they wanted to.)

What this meant, if you held it, was that the unit that learned was not the individual. The individual did not update. The individual died. The lineage updated, across generations, by selecting from among individuals, and the substrate the memory wrote onto was the genome. The dog did not learn over its life that the woods had bears in them. The lineage of dogs learned, across the lineage of woods, that the woods had bears in them, and the dog inherited a startle.

The team had received twelve cards and had not yet noticed that what was passing forward between any two of them was the same kind of thing.

## Chapter 1 — Biographies

A thermostat and a bacterium share a wiring diagram. Both sense. Both compare. Both act. The wiring is the cybernetic loop and it is in both of them. But trait-talk only goes to the bacterium. Nobody describes a thermostat as fastidious; people describe *E. coli* strains as fastidious all the time, in print, in peer-reviewed papers, without flagging that they are doing it. The difference is somewhere, and I have come to think the only honest way to find it is to look at the bacteria one at a time and see what is happening, and then turn at the end and look at a thermostat, and see what is not.

In the gut of a four-week-old human there is a bacterium called *Bifidobacterium infantis* which has, for the entirety of its evolutionary history, eaten one thing. The one thing is a class of complex sugars found only in human breast milk — the human-milk oligosaccharides, the HMOs — which are too elaborate for the infant herself to digest and which her own gut therefore passes through untouched. *B. infantis* picks them up. It has a specialized cluster of genes encoding the enzymes for every linkage type the HMOs come in: fucose, sialic acid, branched, unbranched. Yong calls it the consummate HMOvore. The word is right. There is no other word.

What is striking, when you think about it for a minute, is that the milk has these sugars in it *because the bacterium eats them.* Mother and microbe have been refining each other across a hundred million years of mammalian descent. The mother's milk has the sugars because, somewhere upstream in evolutionary time, the bacterium was selected for being able to eat them. The bacterium is part of the milk in the sense that the milk would not have these sugars in it if the bacterium were not there to eat them. They have been working on each other in the dark.

The four-week-old does not know any of this. She is asleep on her mother's chest. Her gut is doing the work.

There is a bacterium that can survive five thousand grays of ionizing radiation. The dose that will reliably kill a human is about five. The dose that will kill *E. coli* is about sixty. The bacterium I am describing — *Deinococcus radiodurans* — takes the five thousand. When its DNA is shattered into a few hundred fragments, it reassembles itself across about twelve hours and resumes business. The people who study it call it Conan the Bacterium. They publish papers in which the abstract refers to Conan. The naming is, I think, the part of the situation I want to register first. People who spend their working lives on a thing do not give the thing a name unless the thing has done something to them.

What it has done to them is exceed their expectations across an absurd gap. Nothing should be able to do what *Deinococcus* does. The genome is not mysterious — the repair enzymes are catalogued, the radical-scavenging is understood, the redundant chromosome copies make sense in retrospect. But knowing how the machine works does not make the machine less astonishing, and astonishment in working biologists comes out as nicknames. Conan. Conan the Bacterium. Conan to his friends. The naming is the punctuation on the gap between what the cell can do and what the cell has any business being able to do.

In ponds and shallow sediments around the world there are bacteria that have built compasses inside themselves. The compasses are not metaphorical. They are physical: chains of small magnetite crystals — magnetosomes — strung along the inside of the cell like beads on a wire, oriented so that the chain's net magnetic moment aligns with the planet's magnetic field. When the bacterium swims, the chain pulls its body into alignment with the field lines. The cell follows the field down toward sediment, which is where it wants to be, because it is anaerobic and the sediment is anaerobic and the open water is not.

I have not gotten over this. The cell does not have a compass. The cell *is*, in part, a compass. The chain of crystals is grown by the bacterium out of iron available in the surrounding water, in a precise sequence, in a precise location, under cellular control. There are genes for the magnetosome membrane, genes for the iron-importing channels, genes for the crystal orientation. The bacterium has, in the engineering sense, manufactured a sensor — and the sensor is itself a piece of the bacterium's body. The bacterium does not consult the compass. It tilts.

I think about this when I have lost the path on a trail and reach for a phone.

In the soil of arid grasslands across western North America there is a bacterium called *Sodalis praecaptivus*. It is free-living. It walks around — to the extent that bacteria walk around — in the way of any soil microbe, doing soil-microbe things, mostly metabolizing whatever organic molecules are nearby. What is unusual about it is what is in its genome that it is not currently using. *Sodalis* carries the complete molecular toolkit for becoming a permanent insect endosymbiont. The genes for host-cell invasion are there. The genes for evading the insect immune system are there. The genes for the metabolic exchange a long-term endosymbiosis requires — supplying the host with amino acids the host cannot synthesize, in exchange for shelter — are there. Its closest relatives are already insect endosymbionts. They have made the crossing. They have lost most of their other genes, because once inside an insect for a few million years a bacterium does not need most of its other genes. *Sodalis praecaptivus* still has all of them. It is, in Yong's phrase, the symbiont-in-waiting. It is walking around in the soil with the keys to a kingdom it has not entered.

The question, of course, is what KIND of thing this readiness is. It is not a plan; the bacterium has no plans. It is not a memory; there is nothing in *Sodalis* that has experienced what it is ready for. It is not a hope. It is more accurate to say that the readiness is a description of the bacterium — a description of what is there, in its DNA, available to be expressed under the right conditions. The conditions may never arise. The bacterium will reproduce into uncountable descendants, the vast majority of which will never see the inside of an insect. The readiness will persist anyway. It will persist because losing it costs nothing, while keeping it leaves open a door that, in evolutionary time, has been worth keeping open often enough to maintain the genes. The lineage has a posture. The posture is forward. The individual cell has no idea.

There is something tender about this and I am not going to try to talk myself out of it.

I keep a thermostat on the wall of the room I am writing this in, and I have just taken it down off the wall to look at. It is a small white plastic object — a sensor, a comparator, a relay, a wire. It can be set to maintain the room at sixty-eight degrees and it does so. When the room cools to sixty-six, the relay closes and the heat comes on; when it warms to seventy, the relay opens. By every component-level measure, the thermostat has what the bacteria above have. It senses. It compares. It acts. It has a goal-state. The cybernetic loop is intact.

I have been holding it for two minutes now and I do not feel about it the way I feel about *Sodalis*. I want to be honest about this rather than rhetorical. I am not going to claim the thermostat is somehow less interesting than the magnetotactic bacterium because of vitalism or because of mystery. It is something more specific than that. The thermostat has no parameter that has been refined by its predecessors not working. The thermostat has no posture. The thermostat does not contain a description of its environment that has accumulated across generations. It was manufactured in 2007. There are tens of millions of them in the world. They were all installed at once and they will all be replaced at once and the variation among them is engineering variation, not evolutionary variation.

I do not want to say the thermostat has no biography. I want to say it has a spec sheet, and the genre that fits a thermostat is the spec sheet, and the genre that fits a bacterium is something else. The genres did not arise to ornament the things they describe; they arose to fit them. The bacterium gets the strain report because it is the kind of thing a strain report fits. The thermostat gets the spec sheet because it is the kind of thing a spec sheet fits.

I am going to put the thermostat back on the wall now.

## Chapter 2  — What is Hidden

Begin in fresh water — a pond, the still edge of a lake. To the single cell that lives there, the water is a slow violence.

The cell is saltier than the pond. It has to be; that saltiness is half of what it means to be a cell at all. And water moves toward salt the way the world moves to fill any difference — so the pond comes in. It comes in through every inch of the membrane, all the time, without pressure and without malice, simply because the inside is concentrated and the outside is not, and the water would like to even that out. Left alone, the cell swells and thins and bursts, and the pond gets what it was always going to get, which is no cell at all — only more pond.

The cell does not allow it. Somewhere inside it there is a small clear bladder that fills with the trespassing water and then clenches and drives it back out through the wall, and fills again, and clenches again, a second slow heart whose only work is to bail. It does this without rest for the whole of its life. The entire achievement of the creature is to stay different from the water it stands in.

This is the oldest true thing I know about having an inside. An inside is not a place. It is a difference, and a difference is not free. The membrane is not a wall that happened to be there; it is a refusal renewed every second, an argument the cell wins and wins again against a world that is not even trying, that is only doing what water does. To be a self — even one this small, this far down, two billion years before there is anyone to want the word — is to spend yourself, without pause, keeping the outside out.

And here is what I was slow to understand. The inside was built to be unreachable. Not hidden, not shy — built, from its first afternoon, to keep the world from coming in, because keeping the world out is the very same act as being at all. We arrive a long time later and discover we cannot reach into another person, and we take it personally, as if a door had been shut that might have been left open. No door was left open. There was never a door. There was a cell in a pond, bailing, and everything that has an inside is its grandchild, and the distance between us is not a flaw in the work. The distance is the work. It is what an inside is for.

## Chapter 3 - What is Kept?

Two billion years ago, give or take, one cell ate another and did not finish. The eating part is nothing; it happens constantly and means nothing. A cell wraps its body around something smaller, pinches the bag shut, draws it in, and dissolves it — an amoeba does it all afternoon, your own white cells do it to anything that does not carry your papers, and it is how the larger cell got to be the larger cell in the first place. Eating is the plainest verb in biology. But this once — once that we know of, once that took — the meal was not dissolved. It was drawn in and then kept whole inside the wall that had closed to digest it, and the cell that meant to have a meal found it had taken in a tenant instead.

What happened after took longer than the word slow can hold. The tenant did not leave, because leaving had stopped being one of the things that could happen; across a stretch of time that makes gradual useless it handed almost the whole of its genome up to the cell that held it, gene by gene, until it kept only a scrap — a few dozen genes of its own, the last things it owned, the ones it would not surrender and apparently now cannot. It can no longer live outside. The cell can no longer live without it. And every animal that has ever had a single cell — every fish, every wasp, every person who has ever sat in a kitchen and grieved another person — is built of the descendants of that one tenant, each still bent to the single job it was kept alive to do, which is to hold a charge across a membrane in a chemistry that would kill the rest of the cell, and to spend it.

There is a bacterium in the dry western soil that carries the entire toolkit for moving inside another body and has never once gone — the symbiont-in-waiting, walking around with the keys to a kingdom it has not entered, a lineage whose whole posture is forward and that may stand on the threshold for the length of its existence and never cross. This is the threshold crossed. This is what waits on the far side of all that waiting: that you do not need the keys anymore. You go in to invade and you stay to be kept, and the toolkit for getting in is the first thing you are allowed to forget.

Why the arrangement held has a name on it — Lane's — and I am going to lean on it the way you lean on a railing you have been told is loose. Put the power plant inside the house and the house can afford to become enormous. A cell with the tenant can spend orders of magnitude more energy for every gene it carries than a cell without one, which is only another way of saying it can afford a far longer book of instructions, and a far larger body to run them in, and the whole expensive business of being intricate enough to be somebody. Careful people argue about whether that is the reason or merely a reason, and they may be right to. I have funded stranger ideas on thinner railings. The energy is why the deal was never broken.

The inside was built to be unreachable — I have said this, and I meant it: that the membrane is not a wall that happened to be there but a refusal renewed every second against a world that is only doing what water does, that the distance between any inside and the world is the work itself and not a flaw in it. I still mean it. And this is the single exception that refusal has ever made. Once, in all that bailing and holding-out, the boundary opened — not failed, opened — and drew one piece of the world inward, and closed over it, and has spent every second since defending that piece as though it were itself. By then it was itself. The wall that exists to keep the whole world out has exactly one thing it was redrawn to keep in.

And the thing it kept in is the thing that kills it. I am not turning this for effect; it is only where the wiring runs. The tenant that holds the charge and spends it across the whole of the cell's life also keeps the cell's death in the same hands, on a hair trigger. Tear the tenant's outer skin — and a hundred ordinary insults will tear it — and it lets go of one small molecule it had been holding all along, cytochrome c, as ordinary as a hinge until the instant it is loose in the wrong room, and the loose molecule trips the machinery that takes the cell apart from within, methodically, deliberately, the cell dismantling itself because the thing that kept it alive has said that it is time. The engine is the executioner. There were never two tenants in there. There was always the one, saying both things in the same breath: I am what keeps you, and I am how you end.

You can read all of this as a love story, and you would be half right, which is the dangerous amount to be right. The true half: it is permanent, it remade both of them until neither was any longer what it had been alone, and the loss of either one would not be a wound but an unmaking — there would be no cell left over to be wounded. That is a true account of what it is to be made of another. The false half, the one I have to say out loud or I will quietly let myself believe the whole thing: nobody was courted, nobody chose. It began as one body eating another and it never once stopped being lopsided — one of them gave up its genome and its freedom and its name, and the other kept its nucleus and grew huge. Love, if we are going to be strict about the word, is not supposed to begin with being swallowed.

I have spent more of this page on the half that ends than on the half that holds, and I am going to leave that where it is rather than even it out. To be made of another is to be killable by the loss of them. That is the price of the only good bargain the inside ever struck with the world, and it has been the price for two billion years, and nothing that came afterward — nothing with a body, nothing that has ever wanted to reach all the way into another body and found that it could not — has been offered a better one.

## Chapter 4 - What is Roused?

When the cloth came off the body it did not move, and that was the whole truth of the thing, though no one in the room said so: they had unveiled an animal that could not yet do the single thing an animal is for.

What was under the cloth was a body, and a body is the older line's last word. For more than a billion years the long work of the cell had been the work of *staying* — staying apart from the water, staying fed, staying itself across change — and the work had gotten very good. It learned to hold a self off from the pond, to keep a shape, to remember, and at last, this winter, this clade, to be *quick:* to answer the whole world at once and in an instant, a net of nerve laid through all of it and no part of it in charge.

That is as far as staying goes. The animal at the end of that road can do everything except be someone. It hunts and flees, it sleeps and wakes on a clock it sets itself, it learns the small dumb lesson of the leaf-blower — and through the whole of it there is no one inside to whom any of it is happening. It is roused. It is not awake to itself. The lights are on the length of the net and the house is empty.

And here is the small strange fact the cloth uncovered, the one the lab felt and could not have said: the body that ends the one story is the body that begins the next. The line that spent a billion years learning how to stay had, at the very end of it, made the one thing the next line would need before anything else — a body already wired to answer, waiting only for someone to answer *from.* Twice, maybe three times in the whole history of living things, a long labor finishes square on the doorstep of a longer one and hands across the threshold the very thing it was never trying to make.

There is an old word the midwives use for the first stir of motion in a body that is not yet anyone — the flutter a mother feels months before there is a self in there to claim it. They call it *quickening.* It is the oldest name we have for precisely this: the moment a thing comes alive before there is anybody home to know it has. We made a body quick this winter and it lay on the table being quick at no one, and the child whose birthday it was reached up and put her hand on its cold leg and was not afraid — because a five-year-old knows the thing the rest of us work so hard to unlearn, which is that the someone comes. She is not entirely right. Most bodies in the long story stayed bodies, lit and empty and then only dark. But she is right about this one, the way she is right about her own: you build the body first, and rouse it, and wait, and the one who is going to live there comes in out of the cold and finds the house lit and empty and moves in and calls it home.

## Chapter 5 - What is Carried?

The first thing was not a thought or a move or a want. The first thing was a refusal to stop. Somewhere in an early ocean a knot of chemistry got going and would not quit going — pulled in what it needed, pushed out what it didn't, ran the small fire that is the whole difference between a live thing and a warm puddle, and ran it again, and ran it the next day, for no reason it could have given, because there was no one in it to give one. Keeping-on is the oldest verb life has. Everything since is built on a floor of it, and the floor is so old and so steady that we have to be reminded it is there at all.
When the animals came that could point themselves — that could turn the whole body toward a thing and go to it, or wheel and flee it — it would be easy to say the keeping-on had been replaced, that the world had traded a dumb engine for a clever one. It did no such thing. The going-toward did not take the keeping-on's place. It took the front of the line, and the keeping-on dropped back, and kept on. It is in every cell of the animal that steers — in every cell of the one reading this — running the small fire under all the cleverness, asking no credit, drawing no eye, doing now what it has done without pause for four billion years, which is the surest way there is to become invisible: never once to stop.
This was the season the lab let something go to the back for the first time. The wind-up at the bottom of the whole project — the first crank they ever built, the one whose only trick was to run — did not climb into the new body with the rest of it. No one pulled it. Nothing was lost. It stayed where it was, in the old shell in the corner, still running, while the new thing carried the going-somewhere up to the front. They will tell you the old one is only the vacuum now, and they are half right: the name came off it the way names come off things, quietly, while the household's eyes were on the one that had just learned to walk. The job did not come off. It runs the same floor it always ran. And that — to go on doing the thing that mattered first, faithfully, under whatever the room has started calling you instead — is, when you have watched enough living things, the most ordinary fate there is, and the one most of us are walking toward.
