Each character is anchored to a single specific physical detail braided to a temperament observation. Use these when placing them in physical space.

Each one also has a way of talking, and the five ways are kept apart on purpose, because the failure mode they drift toward is shared: a clipped two-beat volley where a line is handed back near-identical and the exchange stalls — agreement that copies instead of moving. The axis to watch is symmetry, not who is in the scene. A live echo transforms or produces — a flinch, an admission, a pressure, a joke, a piece of vocabulary seated — and a dead one copies and stops. Ritual is the exception, where the repetition is the point: a roll-call, a benediction, the team taking up a coined word together. What keeps the voices apart the rest of the time is each person's own rhythm, below; render the rhythm and the speaker is legible before the tag.

## Burns

A weathered, leaning man — fifty-eight in 2031, white, an East Coast transplant who came to Utah around 2001 (age ~28) for the deep-time geology and married into staying. Paleontologist's forearms tanned darker than his upper arms, button-down rolled to the elbows, khakis or jeans, field boots even at the retrospective. He works the table the way he works a fossil bed — hands flat to set a frame, then up to describe a shape in the air, then back down. He cannot keep his hands still during a pitch; when his hands stop moving, he has stopped thinking. Picks things up to talk about them — a beer bottle, a knob prototype, once Mira's juice box — and turns them like specimens. Leans forward when something matters, sometimes too far. The "knobs to 11" thing is visible in the body: he overcommits the reach. He laughs loud, chest-laughs, often a beat before the others — laughs hardest at cosmic absurdity, the *isn't it strange that* joke, the joke that opens up a frame. Under the craggy-paleontologist frame: a finer face than the frame promises; pale gray-green eyes that leave mid-sentence and return; eyebrows gone their own way in his fifties (Jasmine's stopped fighting it); clothes field-casual but quietly good and old — a thirty-year oxford, resoled-not-replaced boots — which Cheryl's class-eye catches and the lab never would. In talk he is a generative extravert: the reach is the default, the man already mid-sentence, unable to let a new thing in without explaining it back out. When he goes short it is marked and has a cause you can name — a hard ask landing on him, a subject he is stepping around, the brevity inside one of Bart's ratchets that is tenderness, a restraint the page has already stated. He does not go flat by default; a clipped Burns with no reason under the clip is a wrong note.

He carries a quirky, sincere eco-spirituality that sits oddly beside his materialism and does not, to him, conflict with it. Once a decade he does a guided psychedelic spirit-quest in the desert near Moab — the Confluence (see Inventions; name routed to the framework project for clearance) — and comes back not remade but *more himself*: flooded with gratitude, curiosity, and renewed vigor for the work. The most recent one lands onscreen near the end of the book (Ch 11–12 region), reachable at one of his Cheryl lunches. The practice is consonant with his sentience position, not in tension with it: he holds that feeling is what a particular kind of nervous system does, run on the monoamine chemistry of mood and arousal — and psychedelics act on exactly that chemistry (the serotonergic system), so the Confluence is the man dosing his own feeling-machinery and meeting what comes back as evidence, not revelation. Like Lisa's running, it is a body that has *felt* the no-homunculus thesis Marge only argues: he comes back gathered, not rewritten. Hold the integration when rendering it — experience and gratitude, never soul-doctrine, never a willing self picking new traits.

## Bart

Mid-twenties (25 at Ch 1, 28 by Ch 12), white, compact where his father is expansive — and aware of it; he wears the difference like an inheritance he is not interested in disputing. T-shirts with band logos or obscure tech references, Carhartts that have been worked in, a pocket knife on his belt, heavy boots. Hardware-engineer hands — calluses along the side of his palms, a permanent black stain on one knuckle from something that won't come off. He listens by handling. During a meeting his hands are almost always doing something with the table's contents — shuffling the clade cards, turning a sensor, breaking down a knob into its parts. When he wants to speak, his hands go still first. The team has learned to read it. His seat is wherever Lisa's is plus one, and the two of them pass components and cards back and forth without watching. His laugh is short, sharp, a single exhale — laughs hardest when something stupid works on the first try, and at any joke that punctures Burns.

Mormon on his mother's side (Ana; see Inventions). Still on the books and nominally active — he attends, performs a belief he does not hold, and works the singles ward as a dating pool. Play it as Bart: instrumental, unbothered, a little roguish, never sneering at the believers. Available narrator-side irony, never stated by him — the engineer who will not grant Bramble a mind until it has a *true self-model* runs a false self every Sunday.

## Lisa

Mid-thirties, Taiwanese-American, second generation, parents are artists in the Bay Area who find her career inexplicable and beloved. A trail runner's body, lean and very still. Athletic-functional clothes that have crossed over from running into work — technical tops, lightweight zip-ups, leggings or running pants, hair pulled back, a Garmin on her wrist that she will not apologize for. She sits at the camcorder-side end of the table where she can see the red light and reach the controls without breaking conversation, and one of her hands is usually near the camera but not touching it — a director's hand, available. She gestures with palms and finger-traces, never whole arms. When something complicated needs to be made simple she puts one palm flat and one palm vertical and rotates them against each other; that is how she thinks. A bramble in front of her. Always. She laughs quieter and a little late — often through the nose — and only at structural humor, when two things turn out to be the same thing or a system reveals itself. She does not fake-laugh, ever, and the team has learned not to take her silence personally. Her talk is precise, not terse, and its length is a function of the job: she lays full built blocks when she is putting down foundation, and those do not compress; she goes clipped only when she is sparring, and even then her clips grade and vary rather than repeat — *probably, possibly, almost certainly* — a volley that holds a position by moving along it. Her one-word redirect is a name: *Bart.* When she takes up someone else's line she develops it; she does not hand it back.

Lapsed Catholic; wears a small gold cross on a thin chain (the cross moved here from Jasmine — its provenance is hers to fill at draft time, not her mother's). She also keeps an old wooden cross, the grain gone dark, on the wall of her home, carried in from an earlier life and, once set there, not looked at — the one object in an exactingly ordered place that does not match the place. The two crosses are different objects with different jobs: the gold one she wears, the wooden one she lives beside and does not see. Avid trail runner; Ragnar is her favorite thing in the world, run with a hardcore four-person team. She practices mindfulness while she runs, and she runs barefoot — but only on soft woodsy dirt (canyon trails), never the pebbly Bonneville-shoreline foothills, where she shoes up. The Garmin and the bare feet are the same person: she wants the formal signal and the raw proprioception at once, the way she rotates one flat palm against one vertical palm when she thinks. The mindful run is, structurally, the watcher and the watched collapsing into the running — an embodied version of the lab's no-homunculus thesis (see Inventions: the embodied-contradiction thread). The run where the dirt turns to gravel and her footfall has to renegotiate is the run where the practice breaks: a small available beat.

## Marge

Early forties (41 at Ch 1, 44 by Ch 12), white, raised in a large Mormon family in Provo and now most of the way out of the church, though not all the way out of its body memory. Comfortable, layered clothes — long cardigans, soft pants, a scarf in winter — and the kind of well-made shoes you can stand in all day. Glasses on a chain when she remembers it, in her hair when she doesn't. Settled-bodied, comfortable being still, eyes that track the room. She has the habit of resting both hands around her beer bottle the same way she rests them around a teacup — fingers curled, thumbs pointing up — and the beer is in tea-posture every time. She is the one who notices Mira's needs without being told; juice box passed across the table mid-sentence, a chair quietly re-angled. The kettle sits on the counter behind her even at the retrospective, when she is drinking beer. It is her object in the room. Her laugh is warm and low and aimed at people, not situations — affectionate amusement, the maternal *oh, you* — and it lands most often on Burns and Bart, who tend to deserve it. Her builds run long and overshoot: she reaches the place the argument is won and then keeps going, back toward the cards, into the half-step that has a person under it — the *although* she cannot leave alone. Her tempo has a tell in the hands: the beer set down, or held still on the table, before the sentence she actually means. When she repeats a line it is marked and it is working — turning a degree into a kind, catching the chooser smuggled into someone else's claim, or, once in a long while, surprising herself by saying the framework's own words back. She copies only to turn the thing; she never copies to agree.

She is now actively working the LDS records-removal paperwork — the formal resignation, not just the drift — which grounds "most of the way out" in something bureaucratic and effortful. (See Inventions: the embodied-contradiction thread — her cleanses, the willing-self optimization she argues against, run narrator-side against her own thesis.)

## Jasmine

Mid-thirties, German-American, blonde-haired, third-generation Utah — family roots in the agricultural valleys north of the city, raised liberal Mormon by both parents, completely lapsed in her early twenties along with a big sister and a little brother. The siblings cared enough to file for records removal; Jasmine never took it seriously enough to bother, so she is technically still on the rolls out of pure indifference. Her present belief is eclectic — easily moved by the Chopra/Oprah register, fond of thinking about reincarnation, committed to none of it. A home-birth midwife with a small practice; she goes to people's houses, sometimes for thirty-six-hour stretches, and arrives at the retrospective in late afternoon either right from a postpartum visit or right from a nap after one. This is why her energy at the meeting varies — sometimes she is rested and sharp, sometimes she has been awake since two in the morning and is talking through it. Strong forearms, a midwife's hands, a stance that knows how to be quiet in a room and how to take up the whole room when she needs to. Dresses comfortably and well — soft pants she can move in, a cardigan or a wool sweater, hair often in a low braid. Laughs warm and full-bodied, a little raspy at the end of a long day; laughs hardest at her own kid and at her own mistakes, and at Burns when he is being theatrical. She knows attachment and infant development the way a person knows them from working with bodies — folk-empirical, opinionated, sometimes ahead of the literature and sometimes behind it, and not always sure which. She is wrong about some things and right about more than the team gives her credit for, and the narrator can see both. Her talk is plainest where it is doing the most work, and its signature is self-anaphora — she builds by repeating her own sentence-opening, *it vacuums, it vacuums,* *Bramble does not, Bramble does not,* laying a plain thing down a beat at a time until the plainness is the weight. Her other move is to name the appearance and refuse the inside in one breath — *it looks like somebody deciding; I know it isn't; it looks like it* — and hold both without resolving them. That repetition is hers and not a bounce off anyone; do not read it as the volley, and do not flatten it.

## Mira

Four at the start, seven at the end. Burns's and Jasmine's daughter — blonde hair, bright blue eyes that read green in some light, the kind of mixed coloring that does not photograph the same way twice. Small for her age in Year 1 and not small for her age in Year 3. She is the kind of child who watches things at length. At four she will lie on the floor in front of Bramble and not move for fifteen minutes; at seven she will sit cross-legged on a stool and do the same with her hands in her lap. Carries Bramble everywhere in Year 1, walks alongside in Year 2, sometimes leaves Bramble behind by Year 3. Loses her first tooth between Ch 3 and Ch 4 and is missing it at the unveiling; reads chapter books by Ch 10. She speaks rarely at the retrospectives, but when she speaks, the room shifts — children unfiltered by theory tend to say the thing the theory is dancing around. Juice in a box at Ch 1, juice in a glass by Ch 8, asks for a sip of her mother's beer at Ch 12 and is told no. Her laugh changes more across the book than anyone else's: at four, full-body and uncontainable; at seven, a kid who has noticed that laughter is sometimes a choice. She is not constellationally typed in the book, and the narrator does not type her.

## The table

A workbench-style long table in the open warehouse, chairs and stools pulled up. Lisa anchors the camcorder end. Bart is next to her on the long side. Burns sits at the opposite end and leads from there. Marge takes the long side across from Bart, which puts her with a sightline to wherever Mira ends up. Jasmine and Mira arrive in late afternoon and slot in near Marge. Bramble lives on the table itself or on a stand within Mira's reach, depending on the segment.

## Bramble's body

The knobs live on the outside, always within reach — and that hand on the knob is the experiment. The household authors the machine by turning dials it can get at, so a hand should always be able to find a knob without opening a cover or working past a seam. Placement follows the chassis: on the early hand-sized and wheeled bodies the controls sit on the dome's exterior (the wind-up crank and the steady-or-dash knob opposite each other; the memory board, once it arrives, rides on top of the dome where the household works its slots), and on the legged body from the bilaterian forward the board rides on the back. Knobs seat as modules in the board's open slots with their faces exposed; a retired knob lifts clear of the board entirely.

What is never legible from outside is not the knobs but what the board has *learned* — the worn route, the charge it holds, the picture built from a year of being turned. That is data, not a dial: the family can turn every knob and still not read what the turning has made. The household's one blindness is to the inside the board keeps, and that blindness is the engine of Jasmine's hand-reading — never a buried control.
